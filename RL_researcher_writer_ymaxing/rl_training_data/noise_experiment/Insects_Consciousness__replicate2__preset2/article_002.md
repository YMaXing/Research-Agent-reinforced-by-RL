# The New York Declaration: Why Animal Consciousness Just Got a Lot Weirder

In 2022, researchers watched bumblebees do something weird: they played. In a lab, the bees rolled little wooden balls around, not for food or any other reward, but just because they seemed to enjoy it [[1]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347222002366). They even went back to the balls after they were done eating, suggesting the activity itself was rewarding [[2]](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2022/oct/27/bumblebees-playing-wooden-balls-bees-study). This wasn't for survival; it looked like fun.

This discovery, showing what looks like a positive emotional state in an insect, is just one piece of a wave of evidence that has led to a major shift in how scientists think about animal minds. This shift is now formalized in a landmark scientific statement: the 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness [[3]](https://www.kimmela.org/2024/05/05/a-new-declaration-on-animal-consciousness). For years, the consensus was that animals with brains like ours—mammals and birds—were conscious. But a lot of recent research suggests that consciousness might be common even in creatures with completely different nervous systems.

The declaration was launched on April 19, 2024, at a conference at New York University called "The Emerging Science of Animal Consciousness" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). It states that there is "a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates... and many invertebrates" [[5]](http://www.nydeclaration.com/). This includes, at a minimum, three major invertebrate groups: cephalopod mollusks (like octopuses), decapod crustaceans (like crabs), and insects. The key phrase here is "realistic possibility." The authors are not claiming certainty, but they are saying the evidence is now strong enough that we have to take the idea seriously.

This wasn't a fringe movement. The declaration was spearheaded by philosopher Kristin Andrews, environmental scientist Jeff Sebo, and philosopher Jonathan Birch. The list of initial signatories is a who's who of the field, including neuroscientists Anil Seth and Christof Koch, psychologists Nicola Clayton and Irene Pepperberg, and philosophers David Chalmers and Peter Godfrey-Smith [[6]](https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2024/04/animal-consciousness-declaration-new-york/678223). This shows a broad agreement across different disciplines.

The declaration is focused on a specific kind of consciousness, often called subjective experience. It's not about complex thoughts like self-awareness or planning for the future. It's about the basic question posed by philosopher Thomas Nagel in his 1974 essay: "fundamentally an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism—something it is like for the organism" [[7]](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/activities/ieg/e-library/sources/nagel_bat.pdf). In simple terms, does it *feel* like something to be a bee or an octopus? The declaration argues that it probably does, even if we can't imagine what that feeling is like.

This growing evidence for animal experience puts the current state of AI into perspective. As neuroscientist Anil Seth points out, the declaration should spark "an understanding and appreciation that we have much more in common with other animals than we do with things like ChatGPT" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). LLMs can generate text, but they don't show any signs of the play, pain, or pleasure we're now finding in animals with brains smaller than a single GPU.

As illustrated in Image 1, the scientific consensus has expanded significantly in just over a decade. The 2024 Declaration is the formal result of years of behavioral evidence that has been piling up. The next section will break down that evidence and explain why the old arguments against widespread animal consciousness no longer hold up.

## A Growing Awareness

The New York Declaration didn't come out of nowhere. It was built on a foundation of surprising discoveries over the last 15 years that showed complex behaviors in animals we used to think of as simple automatons. This research has forced a rethink of the idea that you need a big, human-like brain to have a rich inner life.

The evidence is varied and compelling. For example, a 2021 study showed that octopuses don't just react to injury; they seem to experience a lasting state of pain. After being injected with a mild acid, they not only groomed the specific wound site but also learned to avoid the place where the pain occurred. Later, when given a painkiller in a different location, they developed a preference for that spot, suggesting they actively seek pain relief [[8]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2021.102229).

We've also seen cognitive abilities once thought to be exclusive to vertebrates in invertebrates. Cuttlefish, for example, have a form of episodic-like memory. They can remember not just what they ate, but also where and when, a complex integration of information that helps them plan future foraging. They even demonstrated "source memory," recalling whether they had previously seen or smelled a food item, which points to a more detailed memory of past events [[9]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-62335-x).

In the fish world, the cleaner wrasse fish has been shown to pass a version of the classic mirror-mark test for self-recognition. When a colored mark was placed on their body, the fish would see it in a mirror and then try to scrape it off on a nearby surface. This behavior suggests a degree of self-awareness, as the fish appears to recognize the reflection as its own body [[10]](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3000021).

And as we saw, insects are also showing surprising complexity. Beyond the playful bumblebees, research has found that fruit flies have distinct "active" and "quiet" sleep phases, similar to the Rapid Eye Movement (REM) and slow-wave sleep cycles in humans. Their sleep patterns are even disrupted by social isolation, hinting at a link between their social environment and their basic biological states [[11]](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.88198.2).

Finally, studies on crayfish have shown they display anxiety-like behaviors. When stressed by mild electric shocks, they become more averse to exploring brightly lit areas of a maze. This behavior can be reversed by giving them the same anti-anxiety drugs used in humans, like benzodiazepines, which links their emotional state to neurochemical pathways we understand [[12]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1248811).

As this evidence grew, so did an informal agreement among scientists. However, this consensus wasn't reaching policymakers or the public. Jeff Sebo, one of the declaration's organizers, said that while consciousness in mammals and birds was widely accepted, "less attention has been paid to other vertebrate and especially invertebrate taxa" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). The declaration was a deliberate effort to communicate this new understanding and push for more research and better welfare standards [[13]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background).

This new statement builds on the 2012 Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, which stated that mammals and birds have the "neurological substrates that generate consciousness" [[14]](http://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf). The 2024 New York Declaration is both broader and more careful. It expands the scope to all vertebrates and many invertebrates but uses the more cautious language of a "realistic possibility" of subjective experience [[5]](http://www.nydeclaration.com/). As octopus researcher Peter Godfrey-Smith said, the complex behaviors of these animals make it "very hard not to think that there’s quite a lot going on inside them" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419).

One of the biggest mental blocks to accepting invertebrate consciousness has been the assumption that it requires a brain like ours. This idea is now being taken apart. A bee's brain has only about a million neurons to our 86 billion, but it's enough for complex behaviors like play and social learning. Clearly, raw neuron count isn't the whole story [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419).

Nervous systems can also be built differently. The octopus nervous system is highly distributed, with about two-thirds of its 500 million neurons located in its arms [[15]](https://neuroscience.stanford.edu/news/octopus-brains). A severed octopus arm can continue to perform complex actions, like grasping objects, without any input from the central brain [[16]](https://www.sciencealert.com/octopus-arms-are-controlled-by-a-nervous-system-thats-like-no-other), [[15]](https://neuroscience.stanford.edu/news/octopus-brains). This shows that sophisticated control doesn't have to be centralized. As Godfrey-Smith puts it, consciousness-like behaviors "can exist in an architecture that looks completely alien to vertebrate or human architecture" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419).

The final argument—that you need a cerebral cortex—has also been challenged. Research shows that other species have evolved different brain structures to do similar jobs. Birds use a structure called the pallium, and octopuses use their vertical lobe for memory and learning [[17]](https://asknature.org/strategy/bird-brains-use-unique-structure-to-support-high-intelligence), [[18]](https://asknature.org/strategy/complex-memory-processing-in-the-cephalopod-vertical-lobe). As Kristin Andrews explains, "some of those brain structures, we’re finding, are doing the same kind of work that a cerebral cortex does in humans" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419).

With the evidence mounting and the old architectural arguments weakening, the scientific default has shifted. This raises new ethical questions and practical challenges now that we have to consider the possibility of subjective experience in a much wider range of animals.

## Mindful Relations

Accepting that a much wider range of animals might have subjective experiences has real-world consequences. The ethical bar is now higher than just preventing pain. According to Jeff Sebo, we also have to "provide them with the kinds of enrichment and opportunities that allow them to express their instincts and explore their environments and engage in social systems and otherwise be the kinds of complex agents they are" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). This means we need to think about creating conditions for positive feelings, not just avoiding negative ones.

This creates some practical problems, especially with insects. As Peter Godfrey-Smith notes, our relationship with some species is "inevitably a somewhat antagonistic one" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). Mosquitoes spread diseases, and other insects are agricultural pests [[19]](https://rethinkpriorities.org/research-area/next-steps-in-invertebrate-welfare-part-3-understanding-attitudes-and-possibilities). "The idea that we could just sort of make peace with the mosquitoes," he says, is a lot harder than making peace with fish or octopuses [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). Acknowledging their potential for experience doesn't solve these conflicts, but it does force us to make more conscious ethical trade-offs.

This new perspective also highlights a major gap in how we treat animals in research. While there are strict rules for using vertebrates like mice, the same isn't true for most invertebrates. As researcher Matilda Gibbons points out, "We think about the welfare of livestock and of mice in research, but we never think about the welfare of the insects" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). Millions of fruit flies (*Drosophila melanogaster*) are used in labs with no similar oversight, a practice that's becoming harder to justify [[20]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/policy/knowledge-gaps-in-cephalopod-care-could-stall-welfare-standards).

However, there is a clear path for policy to catch up with the science. In the UK, a scientific review of sentience led to the government including decapod crustaceans and cephalopods in the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Bill, which became law in 2022 [[21]](https://www.eurogroupforanimals.org/news/uk-sentience-bill-passes-final-stages-recognise-decapod-and-cephalopod-sentience-law). This shows that scientific consensus can lead to real legal protections.

This whole discussion also serves as a useful reality check for AI. While researchers think that "current AI systems are very unlikely to be conscious," the rapid shift in our understanding of animal minds suggests we should be cautious about making definitive claims about future AI systems [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). A key difference is that AI can be programmed to say "ouch" without feeling anything, simply by mapping inputs to outputs [[22]](https://undark.org/2023/07/14/interview-the-ethical-puzzle-of-sentient-ai). This ability to fake it means the bar for proving experience in machines will be much higher.

The way forward is more research. As Kristin Andrews suggests, the subjects for this research are already all around us. "All these nematode worms and fruit flies that are in almost every university—study consciousness in them," she says. "You already have them... Make that project a consciousness project. Imagine that!" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419).

This is a call to action. By studying simpler organisms like the nematode *C. elegans*, which has only 302 neurons, we might be able to find the most basic brain activity linked to experience [[23]](https://www.multiverses.xyz/podcast/animal-minds-kristin-andrews-on-assuming-consciousness-in-other-species). If we can find even a basic form of feeling in such a simple system, it would completely change our understanding of where consciousness begins. The tools to expand our knowledge are already in our labs; we just need to start asking the right questions.
