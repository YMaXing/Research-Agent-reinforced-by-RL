# The Bird's Eye Paradox: How Vision Functions Without Oxygen

In human medicine, if a blood vessel in your retina gets blocked, the outcome is severe. Without a steady flow of oxygen, retinal tissue dies in minutes, causing permanent vision loss. Yet, birds have vision far superior to yours and operate with retinas that are completely avascular. They have no internal blood vessels at all. A hawk can spot a mouse from hundreds of feet away, a feat that seems to defy a basic rule of biology. This brings us to a long-standing puzzle: how do birds power one of the most energy-hungry tissues in the animal kingdom without the one thing all other vertebrates need?

The retina is an incredible consumer of energy. It is a specialized part of the brain, and its dense network of neurons burns through oxygen and glucose at two to three times the rate of typical brain tissue. In fact, it uses more energy than any other tissue in the body [[7]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036). For centuries, scientists just assumed that birds must have some hidden mechanism for getting oxygen to the retina. The alternative, that this high-performance system could work without it, seemed impossible. As evolutionary physiologist Christian Damsgaard of Aarhus University stated, the bird retina "was a complete paradox."

A recent study finally cracked this mystery. The research shows that the inner layers of the bird retina exist in a state of chronic anoxia, which is a total lack of oxygen. Instead of some new oxygen-delivery system, birds evolved a tolerance for conditions that would be fatal to us. They power their vision using anaerobic glycolysis, a less efficient but sustainable metabolic pathway. This discovery doesn't just rewrite a chapter of vertebrate physiology. It reveals an evolutionary extreme in how life can adapt to survive.

This work has direct relevance for treating human conditions like stroke, where tissues are damaged by oxygen deprivation. By understanding the metabolic strategies that allow the bird retina to function in anoxia, we might find new ways to protect our own vulnerable neural tissues. Before you can appreciate how birds solved this paradox, you need to understand how oxygen came to dominate complex life and why its absence is normally catastrophic.

## Oxygenated Life: The Great Oxidation Event and Metabolic Trade-offs

About 3.4 billion years ago, cyanobacteria developed photosynthesis, a process that slowly but completely reshaped our planet. They released massive amounts of oxygen into the atmosphere, causing what we now call the Great Oxidation Event. This was a game-changer because oxygen makes energy production extremely efficient. The anaerobic glycolysis pathway yields only two molecules of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), life's universal energy currency, from a single molecule of glucose. In contrast, aerobic respiration can produce up to 32 ATP. That is a more than fifteen-fold increase in efficiency [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/BirdFlying-crJean-PaulWettstein-scaled.webp>
Image 2: Birds, such as this alpine chough (in the crow family), use their exceptional vision to hunt, forage, and migrate. - Jean-Paul Wettstein

This energy advantage was so significant that evolution favored organisms that could use it, leading to a mass extinction of those that couldn't. As molecular physiologist Gary Lewin of the Max Delbrück Center in Berlin puts it, “We’ve been hooked on 20% [atmospheric] oxygen for millions of years.” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). Today, almost all complex, multicellular life depends on a constant supply of oxygen to survive. Without it, energy-intensive processes, especially in the brain and retina, shut down quickly, and cells start to die.

Tolerance for anoxia varies widely across the animal kingdom. Humans can only last a few minutes without oxygen before suffering permanent brain damage. The naked mole rat, a strange subterranean mammal, can survive for 18 minutes by switching to an anaerobic metabolism fueled by fructose [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aab3896). On the other end of the spectrum, some cold-blooded animals like freshwater turtles can survive for months in low-oxygen environments at the bottom of a frozen lake. These examples show just how remarkable the bird retina's permanent state of anoxia is for a warm-blooded, metabolically active animal [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NakedMoleRats-crJavierAbalos-scaled.webp>
Image 3: Naked mole rats can survive without oxygen for 18 minutes. To generate energy without oxygen, they use anaerobic glycolysis fueled by fructose. - Javier Ábalos

With this metabolic context established, we can now look at the mysterious vascular structure that allows birds to push the vertebrate eye to an extreme never seen in other lineages.

## A Mysterious Structure: The Pecten Oculi and Chronic Anoxia

The long-standing theory for the bird's avascular retina focused on a strange structure called the pecten oculi. First described in the 17th century, the pecten is a comb-like, highly vascularized organ that extends into the eye. For centuries, scientists came up with over 30 different hypotheses about its function. The most popular theory was that it somehow delivered oxygen to the retina. But as Damsgaard pointed out, “Nobody had really done direct physiological measurements on this structure.” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Bird_Retina-Fig1-crMarkBelan_Desktopv1.svg>
Image 4: Mark Belan/ _Quanta Magazine_

Damsgaard’s team was the first to conduct these difficult measurements. They used microsensors to probe the retinas of live birds and discovered that the inner retina had no oxygen at all. "Half of the retina lives in a chronic state of anoxia, where there’s no oxygen present at all," Damsgaard explained [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). This single finding overturned centuries of assumptions and pointed to a completely different metabolic strategy.

To figure out how the retina could function without oxygen, the team used spatial transcriptomics, a method that maps gene activity within tissues. The results were striking. Genes related to aerobic respiration were active only in the outer retina, which gets some oxygen from the choroid behind the eye. In the anoxic inner retina, only genes for anaerobic glycolysis were active [[3]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w). Damsgaard described the technique as giving them "a kind of molecular GPS" to see exactly where different metabolic pathways were being used [[7]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036). This clear metabolic split brought up another problem. Since anaerobic glycolysis is so inefficient, how does the inner retina get enough fuel?

The answer brought them back to the pecten oculi. The team discovered that the inner retina required 2.5 times more glucose than other parts of the bird's brain. Their transcriptomics data showed that genes for glucose transporters were highly active in the pecten. This confirmed that the structure was not an oxygen supplier but a metabolic gateway. It pumps huge amounts of glucose into the eye to fuel the inefficient anaerobic process.

At the same time, it removes lactic acid, a toxic byproduct of this process, preventing it from building up [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[4]](https://uol.de/en/news/article/birds-retinas-function-without-oxygen). This is supported by the high expression of monocarboxylate transporters like MCT1 in the pecten, which shuttle lactate out of the eye and into the bloodstream [[8]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen). Specialized glial cells in the retina, known as Müller cells, also play a key role by transporting glucose to the nerve cells and moving lactate away [[4]](https://uol.de/en/news/article/birds-retinas-function-without-oxygen).

This discovery—that a vertebrate tissue can survive in a permanent state of healthy anoxia—was unprecedented. Thomas Baden, a neuroscientist at the University of Sussex, commented on the finding: “The insight that the retina basically goes oxygen-free, at least in some layers, is surprising. … It really gets properly down to zero.” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). Senior author Jens Randel Nyengaard added that the discovery "fundamentally changes the understanding of a structure that has been misinterpreted for centuries. We are essentially collapsing one house of cards and replacing it with another." [[7]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036).

While cancer cells use a similar anaerobic pathway, known as the Warburg effect, and our muscles use it temporarily during intense exercise, this was the first time a healthy vertebrate tissue was found to rely on it for its entire lifetime. Birds are not entirely alone in having such a structure. Lizards have a similar, smaller organ called the conus papillaris, and some fish have a falciform process. However, the avian pecten is the most extreme example of this strategy [[8]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen). Now that we have established how the avian retina operates, we can explore when and why this radical solution evolved and what it teaches us about selective pressures and future applications.

## Eyes Like a Hawk: Evolutionary Origins, Selective Pressures, and Implications

The bird’s retina and its no-oxygen power system are so unusual that they naturally raise questions about how they could have evolved. This is a perfect example of evolution acting not as a grand designer but as a tinkerer, a concept described by biologist François Jacob in his 1977 essay [[5]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.860134). Instead of creating entirely new systems from scratch, evolution works with what is already there, repurposing existing structures for new functions. In this case, it modified the ancestral vertebrate eye to meet the unique demands of birds.

By comparing bird retinas to those of their reptilian relatives, researchers have been able to pinpoint when this adaptation likely occurred. The oxygen-free inner retina and the pecten oculi seem to have evolved in the theropod dinosaur lineage, after it split from the lineage that led to modern crocodiles. This anatomical shift happened at the same time as a thickening of the retina, suggesting that these traits evolved together [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[3]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w).

The selective pressures that drove this change were likely connected to the need for exceptional vision. Birds rely on their sight for everything from hunting and foraging to navigating during long-distance migrations. As biologist Coen Elemans pointed out, "This pecten allows a snake eagle the incredible acuity of vision to spot a tiny still lizard from great heights." [[9]](https://www.sdu.dk/en/om-sdu/fakulteterne/naturvidenskab/nyheder-2026/bird-retina).

The presence of blood vessels in the retina scatters light, which limits visual resolution. By getting rid of these vessels, birds were able to pack photoreceptors and other neurons more densely. This created a thicker retina capable of much higher acuity [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[6]](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0008992). This avascular design, supported by the metabolic function of the pecten, allowed birds to develop the sharpest vision in the animal kingdom. Damsgaard suggests that this system first evolved in theropod dinosaurs for tracking prey and was later co-opted for high-altitude flight where oxygen levels are low, a process known as exaptation [[13]](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41565811).

However, it is still an open question whether the vessel-free retina is a direct adaptation for better vision or an evolutionary coincidence that turned out to be useful. Dan-Eric Nilsson, a sensory biologist at Lund University, stated, “I fully support their interpretation that the anoxic retina probably evolved in the dinosaur lineage.” [[8]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen). But Gary Lewin advises caution against applying these findings to all birds, since migratory species have not yet been studied in this context [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). The story of the bird's eye is a powerful reminder that evolution is a historical process, a patchwork of solutions put together from available parts.

The implications of this research go beyond evolutionary biology and into biomedicine. Conditions like stroke and ischemic retinal diseases are caused by a lack of oxygen delivery to tissues. In fact, official guidelines from the American Stroke Association categorize retinal artery occlusion as a type of stroke because it involves the same mechanism of ischemic cell death [[10]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11328846).

Studies have shown that a stroke can cause massive retinal damage within hours [[11]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3595340). This direct parallel is not just academic. The avascular primate fovea, the center of our high-acuity vision, shares important features with the bird retina, making it a surprisingly relevant model for human eye diseases [[12]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7405834).

By studying how animals like birds and naked mole rats have evolved to tolerate anoxia, scientists can gain insights into how to protect our own tissues from hypoxic damage. "Maybe we can get inspiration for how nature solved these problems by millions of years of natural selection,” Damsgaard suggests. “There’s so much to be learned from these animals that are able to do something that we cannot do.” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).
