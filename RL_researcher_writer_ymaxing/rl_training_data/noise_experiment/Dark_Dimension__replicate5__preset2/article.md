# A Tangled Web: Is the Universe’s Dark Sector a Unified Force?

When you look at the universe's composition, it is a humbling picture. Current estimates show that about 70% of the cosmos is dark energy, an unknown force driving its expansion. Another 25% is dark matter, the mysterious material that holds galaxies together. That leaves just 5% for the ordinary matter that makes up everything you can see and touch [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The terms "dark energy" and "dark matter" are a bit misleading. They are not literally dark, but rather invisible. They do not emit, reflect, or absorb light, which has made them impossible to observe directly so far [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). For decades, the standard Lambda-CDM model of cosmology has treated these two as separate phenomena. In this model, dark matter provides the gravitational scaffolding for galaxies, while dark energy acts as a cosmological constant driving cosmic expansion. But recent observations are challenging this assumption, suggesting the two might be physically intertwined [[2]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time).

Data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) in 2024 and 2025 indicates that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The instrument, which uses 5,000 tiny robotic arms to capture light from distant galaxies, has created the largest 3D map of the universe to date. These measurements suggest dark energy peaked roughly two billion years ago and has been weakening ever since. More surprisingly, the results also imply that in an earlier cosmic era, dark energy could have grown stronger [[3]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Researchers call this entering the “phantom regime,” a situation akin to a ball spontaneously rolling uphill. It is a physical impossibility unless some external force is at play [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This has spurred a growing number of theorists to investigate the possibility that dark energy is coupled to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait notes, while we have assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In this article, we will examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension. We will then explore how string theory provides a common geometric origin for these phenomena through the "dark dimension" proposal.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter might interact is not new. In 2005, a model by Justin Khoury and collaborators posed a hypothetical question. Could dark energy’s density increase over time by drawing energy from dark matter? They found that if the two sectors could affect one another, they could produce what looked like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0510628), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury, Meng-Xiang Lin, and Mark Trodden to construct a more explicit model. Their framework is based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory governing strong nuclear forces. In their model, both the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter change in concert, providing a field-theoretic realization of the interaction [[5]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Another recent study, published in January 2025, imagines a similar scenario. According to this model, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy in a previous cosmic era. Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist and one of the study's authors, explains the effect. "Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion, so easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion of the universe to accelerate" [[6]](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Physicist David Andriot argues that the appearance of a phantom regime is simply a result of bookkeeping. "Any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy," he said. In other words, if dark matter particles are losing mass over time, an observer who assumes their mass is constant will misinterpret this lost energy as an increase in dark energy. The error arises because the components are treated as non-interacting [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Harvard physicist Cumrun Vafa agrees, stating that the standard assumption of separation is flawed. "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong," he said. "That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This framework of interacting dark sectors does more than just explain away phantom energy. It also offers a potential solution to the Hubble tension. This tension refers to the ~9% discrepancy between measurements of the universe's current expansion rate. Measurements based on the early universe, specifically the cosmic microwave background (CMB), yield a value of approximately 67.4 km/s/Mpc. In contrast, those based on more recent, "local" phenomena like Type Ia supernovae give a consistently higher value around 73 km/s/Mpc [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, this "has provoked heated debates... about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics" [[6]](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). An interaction between dark matter and dark energy could alter the expansion history of the universe in just the right way to reconcile these two measurements. This makes the discrepancy an expected outcome of the model rather than a crisis [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological models receive a natural ultraviolet completion and common geometric origin within string theory. We explore this next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter do interact, it could mean they share a common origin. The motivation for such a radical idea stems from the "cosmological constant problem." Calculations of the vacuum's expected energy density based on quantum theory yield a result 10¹²⁰ times larger than what is observed. This discrepancy suggests our understanding is fundamentally incomplete [[8]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy). Ongoing work in string theory, which posits that the universe is made of vibrating strings at its most fundamental level, suggests how this might be possible.

Building on ideas that dark energy and dark matter particle masses may vary over time, Cumrun Vafa and collaborators proposed in 2019 and 2022 that both could be linked to a so-called "dark dimension" [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This proposal originates from Vafa's "distance conjecture," which posits that when a parameter like the cosmological constant is extremely small, a tower of light particles must emerge. The existence of a micron-scale extra dimension is a direct consequence of applying this principle to the observed value of dark energy [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

String theory requires the existence of extra spatial dimensions beyond our familiar three. These are typically thought to be curled up at the microscopic Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). The dark dimension proposal suggests one of these extra dimensions could be significantly larger. Its size would be on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[8]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This provides a precise mechanism for a unified dark sector. Theoretical particles called gravitons, which mediate the force of gravity, could leak into this enlarged dimension. In doing so, they would acquire a small mass and become "dark gravitons." These massive gravitons would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would still be felt in our dimensions. This allows them to fulfill the role normally ascribed to dark matter [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This framework also explains why gravity is so much weaker than other fundamental forces. As it permeates all dimensions, its strength is diluted by seeping into the vast dark dimension, making it appear feeble in our world [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). In this scenario, as physicist Georges Obied explains, "there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter." Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both components. The vacuum energy density (dark energy) is tied to the volume of the extra dimensions, and the mass of the dark gravitons (dark matter) is set by the dimension's radius. The two cannot be varied independently [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya and David Wu, showed that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Their model predicts that both the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will decrease slowly over time. These checks involve comparing predictions against detailed measurements of baryon acoustic oscillations from data like the DESI Lyman-alpha forest, which tightens constraints on the early universe [[10]](https://cosmolya.ifae.es/post/2026-lyalpha-paper). The rate of change is proportional to dark energy's density, which is extraordinarily small. "It won’t change fast," Vafa noted. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A direct consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity. This arises from the decay of heavier dark gravitons into lighter pairs. The mass difference becomes kinetic energy, giving the new particles a "kick velocity." This could allow some dark matter to escape nascent galaxies, forming structures slightly less massive than standard cosmology predicts [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Coincidentally, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden had already looked for such an effect in 2006. They reasoned that if dark matter has a stronger attraction to itself than to ordinary matter, it would affect how galaxies are tidally disrupted. This would create a distinctive "tidal tail" of stars. They did not find this effect, which allowed them to set an upper limit on the strength of any such extra force [[12]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10410), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The value predicted by Vafa's team was about 20 times smaller than this observational limit, falling well within the allowed range. "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Other tests are also being pursued. Physicists are looking for deviations from gravity at the micron scale, with experiments at the University of Washington and in Austria pushing the limits of measurement [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). Furthermore, primordial fluctuations could generate a stochastic background of gravitational waves, and a dark dimension could modify their signals in ways detectable by future observatories [[11]](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12948v1).

The fact that a prediction from string theory roughly agrees with astrophysical evidence does not confirm the model's validity. However, any correspondence between string theory and experiment is a significant step for Vafa and others who have spent decades trying to generate testable predictions [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This convergence of ideas from observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory represents a powerful, multi-pronged attack on one of the biggest mysteries in science. As Obied puts it, "it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
