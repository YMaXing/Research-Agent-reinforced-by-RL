# A Dark Dimension: Unifying Dark Energy and Dark Matter

As AI engineers, we are used to dealing with complex models and vast datasets. We build systems that try to make sense of the world, and we know that sometimes, new data can completely upend our most trusted models. It is a process of constant refinement, where a surprising result is not a failure, but an invitation to build a better model. Now, imagine applying that same process to the entire universe. That is exactly what is happening in cosmology today.

According to our best models, the universe we can see—the stars, galaxies, and planets—makes up only 5% of the cosmos. The other 95% is a "dark sector," composed of about 25% dark matter and 70% dark energy. These components are not literally dark. They are invisible because they do not interact with light, making them impossible to observe directly [[1]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201), [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). For decades, the standard model of cosmology, known as Lambda-CDM, has treated these two as separate, unrelated entities. Dark matter provides the invisible gravitational structure that holds galaxies together, while dark energy, treated as a fixed "cosmological constant," is the force driving the universe's accelerating expansion.

However, recent data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) is challenging this long-held assumption. DESI is a massive data-gathering project, mapping the universe in 3D by measuring the spectra of tens of millions of galaxies and quasars [[11]](#https://news.fnal.gov/2021/05/dark-energy-spectroscopic-instrument-starts-5-year-survey), [[13]](#https://www.desi.lbl.gov). The 2024 and 2025 results from this survey suggest that the strength of dark energy is not constant. It appears to have peaked about two billion years ago and has been weakening since. Even more strangely, the data hints that in an earlier era, dark energy might have grown stronger [[2]](#https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF), [[3]](#https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve). This behavior is known as the "phantom regime," and it is like watching a ball spontaneously roll uphill. It seems to violate the laws of energy conservation, unless some external force is at play.

This has led a growing number of theorists to explore an old but less popular idea: that dark energy and dark matter are physically connected. As particle physicist Tim Tait puts it, while we have assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

We will now examine the concrete models that explain this apparent phantom behavior as a kind of accounting error, and in doing so, may also solve one of cosmology's other persistent problems.

## Dark Interactions

The idea of an interaction between the dark sectors is not new. Back in 2005, a model by Justin Khoury and his colleagues asked a hypothetical question: could dark energy's density increase over time by drawing energy from dark matter? They found that such an interaction could produce what looks like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[5]](#https://cerncourier.com/four-reasons-dark-energy-should-evolve-with-time), [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury said, reflecting on the work [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Fast forward two decades, and the DESI findings have revitalized this line of inquiry. Khoury, along with Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden, recently developed a model based on a dark-sector version of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory governing the strong nuclear force. In their framework, the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles evolve together, in a tightly coupled system [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[6]](#https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf). Another model, published in January 2025, proposes a similar energy transfer. According to one of its authors, cosmologist Elsa Teixeira, "dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," so if some of its energy is transferred to dark energy, it would naturally cause cosmic expansion to accelerate [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Physicist David Andriot suggests that the phantom regime is simply a bookkeeping error. From an AI engineering perspective, this is like misattributing a feature's impact. If our model for predicting user churn suddenly shows strange behavior, it might be because we are not accounting for a change in a related input, like a new marketing campaign. Andriot argues the same is happening here. "Any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy," he explains. We assume dark matter's properties are constant, so when they change, our model incorrectly assigns that change to dark energy, creating the illusion of phantom behavior [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Physicist Cumrun Vafa takes this critique a step further, arguing that the assumption of independence is fundamentally flawed. "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong," he states. "That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This coupled-sector approach also offers a compelling solution to the Hubble tension. This is the persistent ~9% disagreement between measurements of the universe's expansion rate from the early universe (derived from the cosmic microwave background) and the recent universe (from exploding stars called supernovae). As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, this tension "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics" [[7]](#https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf). In a world where dark energy and dark matter interact, their changing relationship over cosmic time would naturally alter the expansion history, making the discrepancy an expected feature of the model, not a flaw in the data.

These interaction models provide a powerful phenomenological framework. They find a more fundamental geometric origin within string theory, through the intriguing proposal of a dark dimension.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter interact, it suggests they might share a common origin. String theory, which proposes that all fundamental particles are just different vibrations of tiny strings, offers a way to connect them. Building on work from 2019 and 2022, Cumrun Vafa and his collaborators have proposed that both dark components are linked through a "dark dimension" [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

String theory requires the existence of extra spatial dimensions beyond the three we experience. These are usually assumed to be curled up at the minuscule Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). The dark dimension proposal, however, suggests that one of these extra dimensions is much larger, on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters). This is still incredibly small, but it is many orders of magnitude larger than the others [[1]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201), [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The mechanism is elegant. Gravitons, the hypothetical particles that mediate gravity, are unique in that they can travel through all dimensions. According to the model, when gravitons leak into this larger dark dimension, they acquire a small mass determined by the dimension's size. These massive particles are called "dark gravitons." While they exist in the extra dimension, their collective gravitational pull is felt in our familiar dimensions, perfectly mimicking the effects we attribute to dark matter [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This framework provides a "very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter," as physicist Georges Obied explains. The two are intrinsically linked by the geometry of the dark dimension. The vacuum energy of the universe (dark energy) depends on the dimension's volume, while the mass of the dark gravitons (dark matter) depends on its radius. Therefore, any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously alter both dark energy and dark matter. They cannot be varied independently [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied, Vafa, Alek Bedroya, and David Wu showed that this scenario aligns remarkably well with the DESI data [[8]](#https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03090), [[9]](#https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=24NnAxcAAAAJ&hl=en). Their model predicts that both dark energy's strength and dark matter's mass should decrease over time, with the rate of change being proportional to dark energy's density. Because this density is so small, the change is incredibly slow. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now," Vafa commented. "We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A key prediction of this coupling is the existence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles. This force, mediated by the massive gravitons, would be distinct from gravity and could be tested by observing its effects on galactic structures [[20]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In 2006, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden had already considered such a possibility. They theorized that an extra attractive force in the dark sector would alter the formation of tidal tails. These are long, thin streams of stars and gas pulled from a smaller galaxy as it orbits a larger one. They searched for this effect and, finding no evidence, placed an upper limit on the strength of any such force [[10]](#https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0606566), [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The value predicted by the dark dimension model falls comfortably within this existing observational constraint. "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski noted [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The fact that a prediction from string theory aligns with astrophysical evidence does not prove the theory correct. However, any connection between the abstract world of string theory and the observable universe is a significant step forward for researchers like Vafa, who have worked for decades to produce testable predictions [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Solving the puzzle of the dark sector requires an interdisciplinary approach, combining insights from observational cosmology, particle physics, and fundamental theory. As Obied concludes, "it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[4]](#https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
