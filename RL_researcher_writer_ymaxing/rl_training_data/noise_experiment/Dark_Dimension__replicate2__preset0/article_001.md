# A Dark Dimension Could Link the Universe’s Greatest Unknowns

Ordinary matter, the stuff we can see and touch, makes up just 5% of the cosmos. The rest is a mystery. Approximately 70% is dark energy, an unknown force pushing space to expand, and another 25% is dark matter, a strange material that holds galaxies together [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These components are called "dark" simply because they are invisible. They do not emit, reflect, or absorb light, which has made them impossible to observe directly so far [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM, treats dark energy as a cosmological constant and assumes it is entirely separate from dark matter. However, recent astronomical observations suggest the two might be physically connected [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In 2024 and 2025, results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration indicated that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The data suggest it reached a maximum value about 2 billion years ago and has weakened since. Furthermore, in an earlier era, dark energy could have grown stronger [[2]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF).

Researchers describe this as dark energy entering the “phantom regime.” They compare the situation to a ball rolling uphill on its own. It is possible, but only if an external force is acting on it. This apparent violation of energy conservation has led theorists to consider that the influencing force might be dark energy’s ties to dark matter [[2]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF), [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Even though scientists have assumed they “don’t have anything to do with each other,” said Tim Tait, a particle physicist at the University of California, Irvine, “you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe” [[27]](https://indico.cern.ch/event/473000/contributions/1993414/attachments/1209863/1764345/tait-Aspen.pdf). We will next examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. In 2005, a model by Justin Khoury and collaborators asked a hypothetical question: Could dark energy’s density increase by taking energy from dark matter? They found that if the two could affect one another, they could produce what looked like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation. “It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this,” Khoury said [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury and his colleagues Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden to build a model of dark interactions based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD). In their new model, the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles change together, providing a field-theoretic way for the two to be linked [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[18]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf).

Another recent study, published in January 2025, proposes something similar. In this model, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy in the past. “Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion,” said one of the authors, Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier. Easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion of the universe to accelerate [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The appearance of a phantom regime is basically a bookkeeping problem, said David Andriot, a physicist at CNRS, the French National Center for Scientific Research. When cosmologists analyze data, they assume dark matter's energy density scales in a fixed way as the universe expands. If dark matter's mass is actually changing over time due to an interaction, that change in energy is not accounted for. Instead, it gets incorrectly "put into the box of dark energy," making dark energy's budget seem to grow in impossible ways [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[21]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Cumrun Vafa, a physicist at Harvard University, agreed. “The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong,” he said. “That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior” [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The DESI findings are not the only reason to consider a link between dark energy and dark matter. Interacting models can also help with one of cosmology's most persistent problems: the Hubble tension. This issue is a disagreement over the universe's current expansion rate. Measurements using the early universe's light, the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB), predict one value. Direct measurements using more recent objects like supernovas give a different value, about 9% higher [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This discrepancy “has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics,” Teixeira and her co-authors wrote. Their model suggests that if dark energy and dark matter interact, the expansion history itself is altered. This means the early and late universe measurements are no longer expected to be the same, turning a crisis into an expected outcome [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These interaction models get a natural explanation and a common geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter do interact, it could mean they have a common origin [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Ongoing work in string theory suggests a way these concepts might be connected. In 2019, building on the idea that dark energy can vary over time, Vafa and two collaborators proposed that the mass of dark matter particles may also change. This led them to suggest that both dark matter and dark energy could share a link with a so-called dark dimension [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

String theory requires extra dimensions to be mathematically consistent. These are typically thought to be curled up at the tiny Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). The researchers proposed that one of them, the dark dimension, could be significantly larger, on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[9]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy), [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In their study, Ivano Basile and Dieter Lüst discovered that if one of these extra dimensions is approximately a micron in size, the resulting energy density aligns with the observed value of dark energy [[9]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

This framework provides a precise mechanism for their connection. Gravitons, the theoretical particles that carry the force of gravity, could "leak" into this larger dark dimension. By doing so, they would gain mass and become "dark gravitons." These massive gravitons would exist in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would still be felt in our dimensions. This allows them to play the role normally given to dark matter [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In this scenario, “there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter,” said Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago. Any change in the size of the dark dimension would affect both dark energy and dark matter at the same time [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya of Princeton and David Wu of Harvard, found that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data. Their model, the Fading Dark Sector (FDS), predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will both decrease over time. The rate of change for dark energy is proportional to its energy density [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[13]](https://indico.global/event/551/contributions/130592/attachments/61097/117637/Dark%20Sector%20and%20the%20Swampland.pdf), [[15]](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=24NnAxcAAAAJ&hl=en).

Because the energy density of dark energy is extremely small, “it won’t change fast,” Vafa said. “It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small” [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A direct result of this coupling is that dark matter particles could interact with each other through a new, long-range force separate from gravity. The good news, Obied said, is that “there could be astrophysical ways of testing this” [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Coincidentally, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden already looked into such a test in 2006. They considered what would happen when a small satellite galaxy is tidally disrupted by a larger one, leaving behind streams of stars.

If dark matter particles pull on each other with an extra force, the dark matter in the satellite would be more tightly bound than its stars. This would create an asymmetry in the resulting tidal tails. They looked for this effect and did not find it, which allowed them to set an upper limit on the strength of this extra force. The value predicted by Vafa’s team fell well within this observational limit [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). “It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work,” Kamionkowski said [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The fact that a prediction from string theory calculations roughly agreed with astrophysical evidence does not confirm the models. However, any link between string theory and experiment is gratifying to Vafa, who has spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the theory [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Understanding dark energy, dark matter, and their relationship is a difficult problem. Obied, one of Vafa’s former students, believes it is helpful to approach it from different angles, including observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory. “This is how people should do science,” Obied said. “I mean, it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide” [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
