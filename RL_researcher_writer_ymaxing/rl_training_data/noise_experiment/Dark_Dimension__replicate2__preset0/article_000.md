# A Dark Dimension Could Link the Universe’s Greatest Unknowns

For those who see the world as a dark place, the universe seems to offer little solace. According to current estimates, ordinary matter—the stuff we can see and touch—accounts for just 5% of the cosmos. The rest is a murky domain, with approximately 70% consisting of dark energy, an unknown force pushing space to expand, and another 25% made of dark matter, a mysterious material holding galaxies together. Semantically speaking, they are not so much “dark” as they are invisible; they do not emit, reflect, or absorb light and have so far proven impossible to observe directly [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM, treats dark energy as a cosmological constant and assumes it is entirely separate from dark matter. However, recent astronomical observations have spurred scientists to look into a less popular idea: that the two are physically intertwined [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In 2024 and 2025, results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration indicated that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The data suggest it reached a maximum value about 2 billion years ago and has weakened since. Furthermore, in an earlier era, dark energy could have grown stronger [[2]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF).

Researchers describe this as dark energy entering the “phantom regime.” They liken the situation to a ball rolling uphill. It is certainly possible, but only if an external force is acting on it. This apparent defiance of energy conservation has led a growing number of theorists to consider that the influencing force might be dark energy’s ties to dark matter [[2]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF), [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Even though scientists have assumed they “don’t have anything to do with each other,” said Tim Tait, a particle physicist at the University of California, Irvine, “you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe” [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

We will next examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. In 2005, a model by Justin Khoury and collaborators posed a hypothetical question: Could there be a form of dark energy whose energy density increased over time? They found that if dark energy and dark matter could affect one another, they could produce what looked like, but was not, phantom behavior. “It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this,” Khoury said [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury and his colleagues Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden to construct a model of dark interactions based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD). In their new model, both the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter change in concert [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[18]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf).

Another recent study, published in January 2025, imagines something similar. In this model, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy during a previous era of cosmic history. “Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion,” said one of the authors, Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier, so easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion to accelerate [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The appearance of phantom behavior is basically the result of bookkeeping, said David Andriot, a physicist at CNRS, the French National Center for Scientific Research. “Any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy,” said Andriot, who presented his own coupled model in May 2025 [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Cumrun Vafa, a physicist at Harvard University, agreed. “The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong,” he said. “That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior” [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The DESI findings are not the only reason to consider an association between dark energy and dark matter. Interacting models can also help ameliorate one of cosmology's most persistent problems: the Hubble tension. This issue concerns the universe's current expansion rate, which varies by approximately 9% depending on whether it is measured using light from the early universe or more recent phenomena like supernovas [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). That discrepancy, or tension, “has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics,” Teixeira and her co-authors wrote. Their model posits that in a world where dark energy and dark matter interact, what seemed to be a crisis caused by disparate expansion rates instead becomes something to be expected [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological interaction models receive a natural ultraviolet completion and common geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter do interact, it could mean they have a common origin [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Ongoing work in string theory suggests a way these concepts might be connected. In 2019, building on the idea that dark energy can vary over time, Vafa and two collaborators proposed that the mass of dark matter particles may also change. This led them to suggest that both dark matter and dark energy could share a link with a so-called dark dimension [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

String theory posits the existence of six or seven extra dimensions in addition to our familiar three of space and one of time. These are typically thought to be curled up on incredibly small scales, but the researchers proposed that one of them—the dark dimension—could be significantly larger, on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[9]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy), [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In their study, Ivano Basile and Dieter Lüst discovered that if one of these extra dimensions is approximately a micron in size, the resulting energy density aligns with the observed value of dark energy [[9]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

This framework provides a precise mechanism for their connection. Gravitons, the theoretical particles that impart the force of gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire mass and become "dark gravitons." These massive gravitons would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects could be felt in our dimensions, allowing them to fulfill the role normally ascribed to dark matter [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In this scenario, “there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter,” said Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago. Changes in the size of the dark dimension would affect both dark energy and dark matter simultaneously [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya of Princeton and David Wu of Harvard, found that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data. Their model, called the Fading Dark Sector (FDS) model, predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will decrease over time. The rate at which dark energy changes is proportional to its energy density [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[13]](https://indico.global/event/551/contributions/130592/attachments/61097/117637/Dark%20Sector%20and%20the%20Swampland.pdf), [[15]](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=24NnAxcAAAAJ&hl=en). Because astrophysical measurements tell us that the energy density of dark energy is extraordinarily small, “it won’t change fast,” Vafa said. “It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small” [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A direct consequence of this coupling is that dark matter particles could interact with one another through a new, long-range force separate from gravity. The good news, Obied said, is that “there could be astrophysical ways of testing this” [[22]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Coincidentally, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden already looked into one such test in 2006. They imagined a scenario where one galaxy's gravity tugs at another. If dark matter has a stronger attraction to other dark matter than to ordinary matter, a special kind of “tidal tail”—an extended stream of stars, gas, and dust—would form. They looked for this effect and did not find it, which allowed them to set an upper bound on the strength of this extra force. The value predicted by Vafa’s team fell well within this observational limit [[22]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). “It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work,” Kamionkowski said [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The fact that a prediction based on string theory calculations roughly agreed with astrophysical evidence does not confirm the validity of these models. However, any correspondence between string theory and experiment is gratifying to Vafa, who has spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the theory [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Understanding dark energy, dark matter, and their relationship is a daunting problem. Obied, one of Vafa’s former students, believes it is beneficial to approach it from different angles—be it from observational cosmology, particle physics, or string theory. “This is how people should do science,” Obied said. “I mean, it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide” [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
