# Sight Without Oxygen: The Biological Paradox of the Bird Retina

In system design, running a critical, high-energy process without a direct power line is a recipe for failure. Yet, in the biological world, birds have been doing just that for millions of years. In human medicine, a blood clot in the retinal artery starves the eye of oxygen and causes rapid, irreversible blindness. Birds, however, operate with retinas that are almost entirely avascular—they have no internal blood vessels. Despite this apparent design flaw, a hawk can spot a mouse from hundreds of feet in the air, achieving a level of visual acuity that far surpasses our own.

This presents a long-standing biological paradox. The retina is one of the most energy-hungry tissues in the animal kingdom, consuming oxygen and glucose at two to three times the rate of the brain [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). For centuries, engineers and scientists alike assumed birds must possess some undiscovered, highly optimized mechanism for acquiring oxygen. Based on everything we know about physiology, their retinas simply should not be able to function without a dedicated supply [[2]](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/translational-medicine/how-bird-retinas-function-without-oxygen-may-inform-future-stroke-therapies). As evolutionary physiologist Christian Damsgaard of Aarhus University puts it, "According to everything we know about physiology, this tissue should not be able to function" [[2]](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/translational-medicine/how-bird-retinas-function-without-oxygen-may-inform-future-stroke-therapies). It was a complete black box.

A recent study finally cracks open this box, and the answer is not a novel oxygen-delivery system. Instead, the inner layers of the bird retina exist in a permanent state of oxygen deprivation, or chronic anoxia. They survive by relying on anaerobic glycolysis, a much less efficient method of energy production [[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09978-w). This finding documents an evolutionary extreme, a case of highly active tissue that functions effectively without oxygen. From an engineering perspective, it is a fascinating example of a system optimized for performance under extreme constraints, and it may hold valuable lessons for treating human conditions caused by ischemia, such as stroke [[4]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036).

Before you can appreciate how birds engineered this solution, you need to understand the legacy system: how oxygen came to dominate complex life and why its absence is normally catastrophic.

## Oxygenated Life: The Great Oxidation Event and Metabolic Trade-offs

About 3.4 billion years ago, cyanobacteria triggered the Great Oxidation Event, a planetary-scale platform shift. By pumping oxygen into the atmosphere through photosynthesis, they unlocked a new, high-performance "algorithm" for energy production [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

The performance difference is staggering. Anaerobic glycolysis, the old system for breaking down glucose without oxygen, yields a mere two molecules of ATP—life's energy currency. The new system, aerobic respiration, uses oxygen to generate up to 32 molecules of ATP from a single glucose molecule [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). This fifteen-fold efficiency gain was a transformative advantage. It enabled the evolution of complex, energy-intensive multicellular organisms and locked them into a dependency on oxygen. As molecular physiologist Gary Lewin of the Max Delbrück Center in Berlin states, “We’ve been hooked on 20% [atmospheric] oxygen for millions of years” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/BirdFlying-crJean-PaulWettstein-scaled.webp>
Image 2: Birds, such as this alpine chough (in the crow family), use their exceptional vision to hunt, forage, and migrate. - Jean-Paul Wettstein

This dependency makes most animals extremely vulnerable to oxygen deprivation. Think of it as a system with poor fault tolerance. Humans can suffer irreversible brain damage after just a few minutes without oxygen. Other species have engineered better failover mechanisms. The naked mole rat, a subterranean rodent living in a low-oxygen environment, can survive for 18 minutes in complete anoxia by switching its metabolic pathway to use fructose [[5]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aab3896). At the far end of the spectrum, some freshwater turtles can survive for months at the bottom of frozen lakes with no oxygen at all [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). For humans, however, a steady supply of oxygen is non-negotiable, especially for high-performance hardware like the brain and retina, where energy-intensive processes shut down almost immediately in its absence.

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NakedMoleRats-crJavierAbalos-scaled.webp>
Image 3: Naked mole rats can survive without oxygen for 18 minutes. To generate energy without oxygen, they use anaerobic glycolysis fueled by fructose. - Javier Ábalos

With this metabolic context established, we can now examine the mysterious structure that enables birds to push the vertebrate eye to an evolutionary extreme.

## A Mysterious Structure: The Pecten Oculi and Chronic Anoxia

When Christian Damsgaard first encountered the paradox of the avascular bird retina, he found that all research pointed to a mysterious structure: the pecten oculi. First described in the 17th century, the pecten is a comb-like, highly vascularized organ that protrudes into the eye. For centuries, it was the subject of intense speculation, generating over 30 different hypotheses about its function, most assuming it was some kind of specialized oxygen-delivery system. Yet, none of these theories were based on direct physiological measurements [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). "Nobody had really done direct physiological measurements on this structure," Damsgaard noted. "That’s where we came in" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Bird_Retina-Fig1-crMarkBelan_Desktopv1.svg>
Image 4: Mark Belan/ _Quanta Magazine_

Damsgaard's team approached the problem like engineers debugging a system. They used microsensors to instrument the eyes of zebra finches, pigeons, and chickens to get direct readings. The results were shocking. The inner retina, the part of the tissue completely lacking blood vessels, had no oxygen at all. The outer retina, which is adjacent to the blood vessels of the choroid, was oxygenated. "Half of the retina lives in a chronic state of anoxia, where there’s no oxygen present at all," Damsgaard said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). This single measurement overturned centuries of assumptions.

To understand how this anoxic tissue could function, the researchers used spatial transcriptomics, a technique that maps gene activity across a tissue sample. This is like profiling the running code to see which subroutines are active in different parts of the system. The data revealed a clear division of labor: genes associated with aerobic respiration were active only in the oxygenated outer retina, while genes for anaerobic glycolysis were exclusively active in the anoxic inner retina [[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09978-w). This less efficient process required a massive amount of fuel. The team found that the inner retina's glucose demand was 2.5 times higher than other parts of the bird's brain [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[6]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen).

This brought them back to the pecten oculi. The transcriptomics data showed that genes for glucose transporters were highly active in the pecten. Its true function was not oxygen delivery, but resource management. It acts as a high-throughput metabolic gateway, pumping huge amounts of glucose into the retina to fuel anaerobic glycolysis [[7]](https://uol.de/en/news/article/birds-retinas-function-without-oxygen). At the same time, anaerobic glycolysis produces lactic acid as a toxic byproduct. The team also found that genes for lactic acid transporters were highly active in the pecten, showing that it simultaneously functions as a waste removal system, preventing toxic buildup [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[8]](https://www.sdu.dk/en/om-sdu/fakulteterne/naturvidenskab/nyheder-2026/bird-retina).

The conclusion was extraordinary. Roughly half of the bird retina exists in a state of permanent anoxia, a metabolic strategy never before documented in any vertebrate tissue. Thomas Baden, a neuroscientist at the University of Sussex, called the finding surprising, noting that the oxygen level "really gets properly down to zero" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). While cancer cells also use anaerobic glycolysis (a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect), and our muscles do so temporarily during intense exercise, the bird retina is the first known example of a vertebrate tissue that functions this way for a lifetime in a healthy, stable state [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

Having established how the avian retina operates, we can now trace when and why this radical solution evolved and what it teaches us about selective pressures and future applications.

## Eyes Like a Hawk: Evolutionary Origins, Selective Pressures, and Implications

The bird’s retina and its no-oxygen power system are so unusual that they naturally raise questions about how they could have evolved. From an engineering standpoint, this is a classic case of optimizing a system under severe constraints. Evolution often acts not as an engineer with a master plan, but as a tinkerer, repurposing existing parts for new functions. In a 1977 essay, biologist François Jacob argued that evolution "does not produce novelties from scratch. It works on what already exists" [[9]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.860134). This is precisely what appears to have happened with the vertebrate eye. Instead of inventing an entirely new oxygen-delivery system, evolution refactored the existing blueprint.

Researchers have pinpointed the origin of this adaptation to the theropod dinosaur lineage, after it split from crocodilians. By comparing bird retinas to those of reptiles like turtles and caimans, which have normal oxygen levels, the team concluded that the oxygen-free tissue likely evolved around the same time the retina began to thicken [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[10]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.12.028). The pecten oculi, or a similar precursor structure, was repurposed from a simple vascular extension into a sophisticated metabolic support system, a dedicated pipeline for fuel and waste [[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09978-w).

The selective pressures driving this change were likely intense, equivalent to strict performance requirements in an engineering project. High-acuity vision is critical for birds' survival, enabling them to hunt, forage, and navigate during long-distance migrations, sometimes at high altitudes where oxygen is scarce [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[8]](https://www.sdu.dk/en/om-sdu/fakulteterne/naturvidenskab/nyheder-2026/bird-retina). The problem with the standard vertebrate design is that blood vessels scatter light. This creates signal noise and limits how densely photoreceptors and neurons can be packed, thereby reducing the maximum possible resolution [[11]](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0008992). By engineering a system that eliminated the need for internal blood vessels, evolution allowed for a thicker, more densely packed retina. This hardware upgrade directly translates to sharper vision [[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09978-w).

Whether this avascular design was a direct adaptation for better vision or an evolutionary coincidence that was later co-opted for high-altitude flight remains an open question. Damsgaard speculates that the system first evolved in theropod dinosaurs for tracking prey and was later exapted for migration when birds took to the skies [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[6]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen). However, others, like physiologist Gary Lewin, urge caution. He points out that the current research has not yet included migratory species, so universal claims about its function in high-altitude flight are still speculative until more data is gathered [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

Beyond its evolutionary significance, this discovery has direct biomedical relevance. In conditions like stroke, human tissues are damaged by both a lack of oxygen and the accumulation of metabolic waste [[4]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036). The bird retina offers a production-proven blueprint for a system that handles both problems. By studying how birds and other anoxia-tolerant animals like the naked mole rat cope with these challenges, we can gain new insights into how to protect tissues from hypoxic damage. "Maybe we can get inspiration for how nature solved these problems by millions of years of natural selection," says Damsgaard. "There’s so much to be learned from these animals that are able to do something that we cannot do" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).
