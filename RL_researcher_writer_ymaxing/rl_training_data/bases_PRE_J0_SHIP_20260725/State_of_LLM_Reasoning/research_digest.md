<s slug="DeepSeek-R1 _ Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning" type="golden_local">DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning introduces DeepSeek-R1-Zero and DeepSeek-R1. These models use large-scale reinforcement learning on the DeepSeek-V3-Base checkpoint (671B MoE, 37B active) to elicit long chain-of-thought reasoning without initial supervised fine-tuning. The core framework is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which samples G=16 outputs per question from the old policy, computes group-normalized advantages via A_i = (r_i - mean({r})) / std({r}), and optimizes the clipped surrogate objective with an unbiased KL penalty term. Training uses a 3e-6 learning rate, β=0.001 KL coefficient, temperature=1 rollout, batch size 512 (32 questions), and reference-model refresh every 400 steps; maximum response length increases from 32k to 65k tokens after step 8.2k. Rewards are strictly rule-based for reasoning data: accuracy reward (exact match or compiler-verified test cases) plus format reward enforcing &lt;think&gt;...&lt;/think&gt; tags. No neural outcome or process reward models are used on reasoning tasks. For general data, two model-based rewards are trained: a helpfulness RM on 66k arena-hard preference pairs and a safety RM on 106k pointwise safe/unsafe annotations. A language-consistency reward (target-language word proportion) is added in later stages. DeepSeek-R1-Zero is trained for 10.4k steps (1.6 epochs) directly from the base model using a minimal template requiring &lt;think&gt; reasoning then &lt;answer&gt;. It exhibits emergent behaviors including self-verification, reflection, and an “aha moment” marked by increased use of “wait.” On AIME 2024, pass@1 rises from 15.6 % to 77.9 %; self-consistency@16 reaches 86.7 %. Comparable gains appear on Codeforces, LiveCodeBench, GPQA Diamond, and CNMO 2024. DeepSeek-R1 follows a five-stage pipeline: (1) cold-start SFT on thousands of human-refined long-CoT traces, (2) first GRPO stage with language-consistency reward, (3) rejection sampling yielding ~600k reasoning + 200k non-reasoning SFT examples, (4) second SFT mixing reasoning and general data, and (5) second GRPO stage (temperature 0.7) that interleaves rule-based and RM rewards. The final model improves AlpacaEval 2.0 by 25 % and ArenaHard by 17 % over the preceding checkpoint while preserving reasoning gains. The source includes benchmark-results tables (topic=metric,zero), RL-data statistics tables, SFT-data statistics tables, and infrastructure diagrams. It reports concrete training hyperparameters, exact data volumes (26k math, 17k+8k code, 22k STEM, 15k logic, 66k+12k general), and safety evaluations across languages and jailbreaks. Limitations explicitly noted are: absence of tool use or structured output, token inefficiency from overthinking on easy problems, language mixing outside Chinese/English, prompt sensitivity (few-shot degrades performance), limited software-engineering gains due to evaluation cost, and reward hacking when model-based rewards are applied for many steps.</s>
<s slug="Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners" type="golden_local">Zero-shot-CoT prompting elicits multi-step reasoning from pretrained LLMs by prepending a single fixed trigger ("Let's think step by step") to any input question, without task-specific exemplars. It uses two-stage prompting: (1) reasoning extraction via the template "Q: [X]. A: Let's think step by step." with greedy decoding, followed by (2) answer extraction on the concatenated self-augmented prompt using format-specific triggers (e.g., "Therefore, the answer (arabic numerals) is" for arithmetic or "Therefore, among A through E, the answer is" for multiple-choice). Answer cleansing then extracts the first valid token (number, letter, yes/no, or free text). The paper evaluates this on 12 datasets spanning arithmetic (MultiArith, GSM8K, AQUA-RAT, SVAMP, SingleEq, AddSub), symbolic (Last Letter Concatenation, Coin Flip), commonsense (CommonsenseQA, StrategyQA), and logical reasoning (Date Understanding, Tracking Shuffled Objects from BIG-bench). Primary models include InstructGPT variants (text-davinci-002, text-*-001), original GPT-3 (ada to davinci), and PaLM (8B/62B/540B), plus smaller models (GPT-2, GPT-Neo, GPT-J, T0, OPT). Baselines are standard zero-shot, few-shot, and Few-shot-CoT (using Wei et al. 2022's 8 exemplars). Key results: with text-davinci-002, Zero-shot-CoT raises MultiArith from 17.7% to 78.7% and GSM8K from 10.4% to 40.7%; comparable gains appear on PaLM-540B. It matches or exceeds standard few-shot on several arithmetic tasks and produces scaling curves comparable to Few-shot-CoT. The method works across instructive templates (Table 4 lists 16 variants) but fails on misleading/irrelevant ones. Few-shot-CoT performance drops when example and test answer formats mismatch (Table 5). Tables report per-dataset accuracies, error patterns, and model-scale ablations (Figure 3). Appendices supply dataset creation code, full answer-extraction prompt lists, cleansing rules, and generated traces. Notable gaps: no public details on InstructGPT/PaLM pretraining data; Zero-shot-CoT still trails carefully engineered Few-shot-CoT; gains are absent on some commonsense tasks even when reasoning traces are valid; evaluation uses only greedy decoding and single runs for few-shot conditions.</s>
<s slug="Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters" type="golden_local">Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters examines scaling inference-time computation for LLMs on the MATH benchmark (12k train/500 test split) using PaLM 2-S* (Codey) base models fine-tuned for revision or verification. The core question is how fixed non-trivial test-time compute improves performance on challenging prompts, via two mechanisms: (1) modifying the proposal distribution (iterative self-refinement/revisions) and (2) searching against dense process-based verifiers (PRMs). Key concepts include a unified proposer-verifier view of test-time compute, where modifications occur at input level (augmenting prompts for revisions) or output level (post-hoc scoring/surgery on candidates). The paper defines a "test-time compute-optimal scaling strategy" as selecting hyperparameters \(\theta\) maximizing accuracy for prompt \(q\) under budget \(N\), formalized as \(\theta_{q,y^*(q)}^*(N) = \arg\max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \text{Target}(\theta,N,q)}[1_{y=y^*(q)}]\). Question difficulty serves as the sufficient statistic: oracle difficulty bins pass@1 rates (from 2048 samples) into five quantiles; model-predicted difficulty uses averaged PRM final-answer scores for the same binning. Two-fold cross-validation avoids leakage when selecting per-bin strategies. Concrete techniques include PRM training via Monte Carlo rollouts (soft value estimates, binary cross-entropy, AdamW lr=3e-5) without human labels; step-wise aggregation using the final PRM step score; inter-answer aggregation via best-of-N weighted selection. Search methods are best-of-N weighted, beam search (beam width \(\sqrt{N}\) or fixed 4, up to 40 rounds), and lookahead search (\(k=1,3\)) with rollout cost \(N \times (k+1)\). Revision training uses on-policy parallel samples post-hoc paired by character edit distance (up to 4 incorrect answers in context) for SFT (AdamW lr=1e-5); inference supports chains beyond training length with truncation. Verifier selection (ORM adapted to revision context) or majority voting aggregates within/between trajectories. FLOPs exchange uses \(X=6ND_{\text{pretrain}}\), \(Y=2ND_{\text{inference}}\), comparing against \(\sim\)14\(\times\) larger models at ratios \(R = D_{\text{inference}}/D_{\text{pretrain}}\) of 0.16, 0.79, and 22. Specific claims: compute-optimal allocation (oracle or predicted difficulty) outperforms best-of-N with up to 4\(\times\) less compute (e.g., 16 vs. 64 generations for search; 64 vs. 256 for revisions); on easy/intermediate questions (bins 1-3) or low \(R\), test-time compute exceeds parameter scaling; beam search helps harder bins (3-4) at low budgets but over-optimizes easy ones at high budgets; PRM exceeds ORM; pass@1 improves across revision steps even beyond training horizon. The source includes a 23-line Python tool-loop example, revision model example outputs (7 figures), and PRM beam search example outputs (6 figures) with per-step scores. Notable limitations: negligible gains on hardest questions (bin 5); difficulty estimation incurs non-trivial inference cost (not subtracted in reported budgets); experiments use only MATH and do not combine PRM tree-search with revisions or study ReST\(^{\text{EM}}\) distillation back into the base model.</s>
<s slug="1-scaling-by-thinking-in-continuous-space" type="exploitation">**Summary** The source describes a depth-recurrent transformer architecture (Huginn-0125) that scales test-time compute by iterating a shared recurrent block in latent space rather than generating additional tokens. The model partitions layers into a prelude block P (embedding inputs), a core recurrent block R (iterated r times on states s ∈ ℝ^{n×h}), and a coda block C (producing next-token probabilities), with the recurrence equation s_i = R(e, s_{i-1}) after random initialization of s_0. Training samples r from a log-normal Poisson distribution (target mean r̄=32) and applies truncated backpropagation through the final k=8 iterations. The 3.5 B parameter model ((l_P, l_R, l_C)=(2,4,2), h=5280) was pretrained on 800 B tokens (heavily code- and math-skewed mixture) using Adam (β1=0.9, β2=0.95, η=5×10^{-4}), bfloat16, and data-parallel training on 4096 AMD MI250X GPUs on Frontier, achieving 41–51 % AFU. At inference the same weights support variable recurrence depth, zero-shot per-token adaptive exits (KL-divergence threshold 5×10^{-4}), KV-cache sharing (modulo-k budget), continuous CoT via state warm-starting, and self-speculative decoding. Benchmark tables (ARTEFACT_A21: 16-line model comparison on HellaSwag/MMLU; ARTEFACT_A22: math tasks; ARTEFACT_A23: recurrent vs. non-recurrent baseline; ARTEFACT_A24: open/closed QA) show the model matching or exceeding OLMo-7B and Pythia on GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval and MBPP when r increases, with performance scaling to compute budgets equivalent to a 50 B parameter fixed-depth transformer. Saturation is task-dependent (OpenBookQA converges faster than GSM8K); recurrence yields largest gains on hard reasoning tasks and when additional few-shot context is supplied. Latent-space analysis (PCA trajectories, distance-to-fixed-point plots) reveals emergent behaviors including token-specific convergence rates, multi-dimensional orbits on arithmetic tokens, and “slider” drifts, all arising without explicit fixed-point objectives. The source includes a 23-line Python tool-loop example and multiple appendix figures of latent trajectories. Notable limitations: single training run without learning-rate cooldown, 800 B tokens on public data only, no ablation of the instruction-data mixture, and evaluation confined to zero-shot and fixed few-shot lm-eval-harness settings.</s>

<s slug="Test-Time Preference Optimization _ On-the-Fly Alignment via Iterative Textual Feedback" type="exploitation">TPO aligns LLMs to human preferences at inference time by converting reward-model scores into textual critiques without parameter updates. The policy model samples N responses, scores them with a reward model (e.g., FsfairX-LLaMA3-RM-v0.1 or Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B-RM), designates highest/lowest as chosen/rejected, then uses three prompts—P_loss (preference comparison), P_grad (refinement instructions), and P_update (response revision)—to produce a textual loss, textual gradient, and new candidates. The process repeats for D iterations, caching all scored responses and selecting the highest-scoring final output. TPO is implemented on the TextGrad framework with vLLM (temperature 0.7, top_p 0.95) and treats context ϕ as the optimizable variable while θ remains fixed. Evaluations cover AlpacaEval 2 (LC/WR), Arena-Hard (WR), HH-RLHF, BeaverTails-Evaluation, XSTest, and MATH-500. After two TPO steps (N=5), Llama-3.1-70B-SFT exceeds both its DPO-trained counterpart and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct on nearly all metrics; an ultra setting (D=5, N=20) widens the margin. Mistral-Small-Instruct-2409 (22B) reaches 53.4 LC on AlpacaEval 2 and 72.2 WR on Arena-Hard. Reward-model scores rise monotonically with iterations; depth-wise revision compensates for smaller width. TPO-D2-N5 outperforms Best-of-N (30–60 samples) with GPT-4 win rates of 65.2 % and 57.5 % on 100-instance subsets. Compute comparison shows TPO costs ~9.3 PFLOPs per query versus 72 840 PFLOPs for Llama-3.1-70B-DPO training on 64 k examples. The source includes three tables reporting benchmark scores for SFT and instruct models, data statistics, and test-time training curves on HH-RLHF and other sets. It notes that Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct loses alignment under TPO because it cannot reliably interpret textual loss/gradient prompts, and that revision baselines omitting rejected-response comparisons underperform.</s>
<s slug="Thoughts Are All Over the Place _ On the Underthinking of o1-Like LLMs" type="exploitation">The paper "Thoughts Are All Over the Place: On the Underthinking of o1-Like LLMs" defines underthinking as the tendency of o1-like models to prematurely abandon promising reasoning paths via frequent thought switches (signaled by terms such as "alternatively"), resulting in inadequate depth and wasted tokens on hard problems. It contrasts this with conventional LLMs and overthinking behaviors noted in related work. Experiments use MATH500 (Level 5 = MATH500-Hard), GPQA Diamond, and AIME2022–2024. Models evaluated are QwQ-32B-Preview, DeepSeek-R1-671B, DeepSeek-R1-Preview, plus distillates DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-70B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B. Thoughts are segmented automatically by Llama-3.3-70B after manual curation of switch expressions. On AIME2024, o1-like models produce 225% more tokens and 418% more switches on incorrect answers; >70% of incorrect responses contain ≥1 correct early thought that is abandoned. The underthinking metric ξ_UT = 1/N Σ(1 − T̂_i/T_i) quantifies token inefficiency up to the first correct thought (or full length if none exists). Tables report ξ_UT and accuracy per model and dataset; higher-accuracy models can still show elevated ξ_UT on MATH500-Hard and GPQA Diamond. To mitigate underthinking, the authors introduce the TIP decoding strategy. Standard softmax logits z_t,v are adjusted by subtracting α from thought-switch tokens v̂ ∈ V̂ for the first β positions after each thought start Ψ, yielding modified probabilities. Grid search on AIME2022–23 selects α=3, β=600. TIP raises Pass@1 on all three test sets for QwQ-32B-Preview, reduces switch-token count, and lengthens inter-switch intervals. It also improves Self-Consistency and Laconic Decoding (shortest-of-N) when combined, with results shown in Pass@k tables. Prompting for thought persistence yields smaller gains. The source includes tables on underthinking scores, accuracy versus α/β, and weighted underthinking under best-of-N sampling. Limitations include restriction to math and graduate science benchmarks, fixed hyperparameters tuned only on AIME2022–23, and reliance on LLM-based thought segmentation and correctness assessment (validated at ~82% accuracy on a 90-instance AIME subset).</s>
<s slug="can-1b-llm-surpass-405b-llm-rethinking-compute-optimal-test-" type="exploitation">Test-Time Scaling (TTS) improves LLM reasoning via additional inference compute. The paper examines external TTS (Best-of-N, beam search, Diverse Verifier Tree Search) guided by Process Reward Models (PRMs) and formulates reward-aware compute-optimal TTS that adapts to policy model, PRM, and absolute problem difficulty (easy/medium/hard defined by Pass@1 thresholds of 50–100%, 10–50%, 0–10%). It evaluates Llama-3 and Qwen2.5 policy models (0.5B–72B) with PRMs including Math-Shepherd-PRM-7B, RLHFlow-PRM-Mistral-8B, RLHFlow-PRM-Deepseek-8B, Skywork-PRM-1.5B/7B, and Qwen2.5-Math-PRM-7B/72B on MATH-500 and AIME24 using the OpenR framework, PRM-Min/Last/Avg scoring, and Majority/PRM-Max/PRM-Vote aggregation. Includes tables on RLHFlow training data statistics, voting-method sensitivity, and model comparisons, plus case studies of PRM failure modes (over-criticism, error neglect, length bias). Limitations include restriction to mathematical tasks, persistent PRM OOD and length biases, and lack of evaluation on coding or chemistry domains.</s>
<s slug="chain-of-draft-thinking-faster-by-writing-less" type="exploitation">Chain of Draft (CoD) is a prompting strategy that replaces verbose Chain-of-Thought (CoT) intermediate steps with concise drafts limited to at most five words per step, emulating human shorthand notes to retain only essential calculations or transformations. The approach was evaluated on GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-08-06) and Claude 3.5 Sonnet (claude-3-5-sonnet-20240620) using few-shot examples written by the authors. Benchmarks comprise GSM8k for arithmetic reasoning, date understanding and sports understanding from BIG-bench for commonsense reasoning, and a 250-example synthetic coin-flip dataset for symbolic reasoning. On GSM8k, CoD reaches 91% accuracy with approximately 40 output tokens versus CoT at >95% accuracy with ~200 tokens, cutting latency by 76.2% for GPT-4o and 48.4% for Claude 3.5 Sonnet. On sports understanding, CoD reduces Claude 3.5 Sonnet output tokens from 189.4 to 14.3 (92.4% reduction) while matching or exceeding CoT accuracy. On coin flip, both models attain 100% accuracy with CoD, reducing tokens 68–86% relative to CoT. Standard prompting baselines yield 53.3–73.2% accuracy across tasks. Related techniques referenced include OpenAI o1, Alibaba QwQ, DeepSeek R1, ReAct, Skeleton-of-Thought (SoT), Coconut, Concise Thoughts (CCoT), and token-budget-aware LLM reasoning (TALE). The source includes evaluation tables on model, prompt, accuracy, token count, and latency for GSM8K, date understanding, sports understanding, coin flip, zero-shot GSM8K, and small-model GSM8K runs. Limitations documented are substantial accuracy drops in zero-shot settings (only +3.6% over direct answer for Claude 3.5 Sonnet on GSM8k) and larger gaps versus CoT on models under 3B parameters (Qwen2.5 1.5B/3B instruct, Llama 3.2 3B instruct, Zoom SLM 2.3B). The source notes CoD-style patterns are scarce in training data and that per-step budgets allow unlimited steps unlike global-budget methods.</s>
<s slug="coat-chain-of-associated-thoughts-framework-for-enhancing-la" type="exploitation">CoAT (Chain-of-Associated-Thoughts) is a framework that augments LLM reasoning by integrating an optimized Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) algorithm with a dynamic associative memory (AM) mechanism. It targets the shift from single-pass “fast thinking” inference (e.g., standard GPT, LLaMA, Qwen) to iterative “slow thinking” that mimics human association and knowledge replenishment during inference. CoAT expands the search space by inserting AM content at each MCTS node, enabling real-time retrieval and self-augmentation without post-training. The framework comprises two core components. The AM mechanism (Eq. 1–2) generates concise, non-redundant associative content for node \(n_i\) either via the LLM itself or an optional External Brain (\(\mathbb{EB}\)) that can draw from knowledge graphs, vector databases, LLM agents, or web search engines. Generated content at node \(n_{i+1}\) references both ancestral history and prior AM entries. The optimized MCTS augments the standard four-stage process with an Association stage between Expansion and Evaluation; node value (Eq. 4) becomes \(V(n)=\mathcal{F}_g(Q,\mathcal{G}(n))+\beta\cdot\mathcal{F}_a(\mathcal{G}(n),\mathcal{AM}(n))\) where \(\beta=0.1\). UCT selection (Eq. 3), back-propagation (Eq. 5), a Reward Model (\(\mathcal{RM}\)) at leaf nodes, and depth cap \(D\) (default unlimited when \(D=-1\)) guide trajectory selection. Implementation is built on LangChain; Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct serve as primary backbones. Quantitative results include a table comparing end-to-end generation performance of CoAT against NativeRAG, IRCoT, HippoRAG, and KAG on HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and MuSiQue (1 000-question subsets), reporting EM gains of 13.0 %, 7.2 %, and 13.4 % and F1 gains of 2.1 %, 3.3 %, and 8.4 % over KAG with the same backbone. On the self-constructed Comprehensive Reasoning Benchmark (CRB) dataset—205 professionally reviewed questions spanning politics, economics, law, history, etc.—CoAT yields relative score improvements of 22 % and 18 % for the two Qwen backbones and a 50 % relative lift in average pairwise win rate. A second table and win-rate heatmap (Figure 6) compare CoAT-augmented models against generative baselines (GPT-4o, Doubao-pro-256k, MiniMax/abab6.5-chat) and reasoning models (o1, o1-mini, o3-mini, DeepSeek-R1). Ablation tables examine the contribution of AM and varying \(\beta\). Notable limitations are increased inference latency due to expanded search space and AM insertion, plus residual scope for CRB quality refinement. The source includes Algorithm 1 (CoAT reasoning pseudocode), a 13-line CRB results table, an 8-line ablation table, and multiple qualitative response comparisons.</s>
<s slug="cu-intercalation-stabilized-1t-mos-2-with-electrical-insulat" type="exploitation">Cu intercalation-stabilized 1T’ MoS2 with electrical insulating behavior reports synthesis of Cu-intercalated 1T’ MoS2 via mild wet-chemistry intercalation of zero-valent Cu into LP-CVD-grown 1T’ MoS2 belts on c-sapphire. <s slug="helpsteer3-human-annotated-feedback-and-edit-data-to-empower" type="exploitation">HelpSteer3 introduces human-annotated feedback and edit data to train dedicated Feedback and Edit models for inference-time scaling on open-ended general-domain tasks. The core setup generates an initial response, obtains natural-language feedback on helpfulness, then produces an edited response conditioned on that feedback. This approach targets tasks lacking verifiable answers, unlike verifier-based methods such as Reinforcement Learning with verifiers, Process Reward Models, Generative Verifiers, Monte-Carlo Tree Search, Self-Consistency or Execution Feedback used by QwQ-32B-Preview, Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking and DeepSeek R1. Over 7000 annotators across 80+ regions supplied 2–10 sentence English feedback starting with “The response is {not / slightly / partially / mostly / perfectly} helpful”, followed by separate annotators performing edits. Three to five annotations per task were collected; only the three most agreeing feedback were retained. This yields Feedback Demonstration, Edit Demonstration and Edit Preference datasets, with descriptive statistics provided in tables covering subset sizes, prompt/response lengths and language distributions (14 programming, 13 natural languages). Training initializes from Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-Nemotron-70B-Instruct using the Nemo-Aligner framework. <s slug="inference-time-computations-for-llm-reasoning-and-planning-a-1" type="exploitation">Sys2Bench evaluates inference-time techniques for LLM reasoning and planning across 11 datasets in five categories: arithmetic (GSM8K, AQuA), logical (ProntoQA), commonsense (StrategyQA, HotPotQA), algorithmic (Game of 24, Bin Packing), and planning (BlocksWorld, Rubik’s Cube, TripPlan, CalendarPlan). Reasoning as Planning (RAP) applies Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS, ≤10 rollouts) with the LLM as world model and agent, restricted to LLaMA 3.1 8B and a subset of tasks due to logit and prompt requirements. <s slug="inner-thinking-transformer-leveraging-dynamic-depth-scaling-" type="exploitation">Inner Thinking Transformer (ITT) introduces dynamic depth scaling for LLMs by reinterpreting layer computations as implicit inner thinking steps on token representations. It addresses performance bottlenecks on critical tokens through three mechanisms: Adaptive Token Routing (ATR) that uses a linear weight predictor \(\mathcal{R}^{(t)}\) and \(\rho\)-th percentile threshold to select tokens for extra processing at each step; Residual Thinking Connections (RTC) that accumulate outputs via \(x^{(t)}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}(f(x^{(i-1)})\odot\phi^{(i)})\) with learnable Thinking Step Encoding \(\phi^{(t)}\); and Thinking Step Encoding to differentiate successive steps. The architecture replaces every other layer in a LLaMA-2 backbone with an ITT layer, trained end-to-end with cross-entropy loss on RedPajama (50B tokens, 4096 sequence length, global batch 256) using the Sheared-LLaMA codebase on Composer across 8 NVIDIA A100-80GB GPUs. Evaluations cover 162M/230M/466M parameter scales with \(\times2/\times3/\times4\) thinking steps. On an 11-task benchmark (0-shot SciQ/PIQA/WinoGrande/ARC-E/LogiQA/LAMBADA; 10-shot HellaSwag; 25-shot ARC-C; 32-shot BoolQ) via lm-evaluation-harness, ITT \(\times4\)-162M reaches 96.5% of the 466M Transformer baseline while using 70.4% of its parameters, reduces training data needs by 43.2%, and delivers 1.7–2.1% average accuracy gains over dense Transformers and Loop variants at matched FLOPs. Perplexity curves and elastic inference tables show ITT maintains performance at 50–90% token selection ratios during test-time scaling, with routers allocating 30–50% of tokens to deeper steps and prioritizing verbs/semantic pivots. Ablation tables quantify RTC removal (+0.77 PPL), Thinking Step Encoding removal (+0.31 PPL), and ATR disablement (+0.19 PPL, >50% FLOP reduction lost). Routing visualizations and weight distributions (0.6–0.8 range) illustrate complementary step behavior. The source includes comprehensive evaluation tables on model performance across parameters and FLOPs, perplexity under varying selection ratios, ablation results, and detailed model configurations. Limitations noted are fixed routing patterns during training, validation limited to 466M parameters, additional backward-pass memory from RTC, and potential for more advanced temporal modeling beyond current step encoding.</s>
<s slug="s-superscript-s-italic-s-start-postsuperscript-end-postsuper" type="exploitation">S* is a hybrid test-time scaling framework for code generation that augments parallel sampling with sequential scaling. <s slug="s1 _  Simple test-time scaling" type="exploitation">**s1: Simple test-time scaling** presents a minimal recipe for test-time scaling in reasoning models. The core approach combines supervised fine-tuning on a 1,000-example dataset (s1K) with a decoding intervention called budget forcing. s1K is curated from an initial 59,029 samples collected from 16 sources (NuminaMATH, historical AIME, OlympicArena, OmniMath, AGIEval, s1-prob from Stanford Statistics PhD quals, s1-teasers from PuzzledQuant) plus traces and solutions generated by the Google Gemini Flash Thinking API. Filtering applies three validated criteria—quality (remove API errors and formatting issues), difficulty (exclude items solved by Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct; favor longer traces), and diversity (MSC-domain classification via Claude 3.5 Sonnet with length-weighted sampling)—yielding 1,000 examples spanning 50 domains. The grader (Claude 3.5/3.7) marks 53.6% of s1K traces correct. Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct is fine-tuned on s1K for 5 epochs (batch size 16, 315 steps, bfloat16, AdamW, cosine LR schedule) using PyTorch FSDP, requiring 26 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs and producing s1-32B. Token delimiters &lt;|im_start|&gt;think / &lt;|im_start|&gt;answer separate reasoning from final answer. Budget forcing controls sequential test-time compute: (I) append the end-of-thinking delimiter (optionally “Final Answer:”) to early-exit; (II) suppress the delimiter and append “Wait” (or variants) to force continued reasoning. It is compared against token-/step-/class-conditional prompting and rejection sampling. Metrics are defined as Control (fraction of runs within min/max thinking-token bounds), Scaling (average slope of accuracy vs. tokens), and Performance (max accuracy). On AIME24, budget forcing achieves perfect control, positive slope, and highest score. s1-32B with budget forcing exceeds o1-preview on MATH and AIME24 (up to +27%) and reaches 57% on AIME24 (from 50% baseline) by forcing up to six “Wait” appendages. It lies on the sample-efficiency frontier relative to DeepSeek-r1-32B (800× more examples), QwQ-32B-preview, Sky-T1-32B, and Bespoke-32B. Sequential scaling outperforms parallel majority voting on the trained model; REBASE tree search further improves extrapolation when added. Training ablations confirm that quality+difficulty+diversity together are required; using the full 59K pool yields only marginal gains at 394 H100 GPU-hours versus 7 for s1K. Method ablations show token-conditional control fails, step-conditional control is gamed, class-conditional control works modestly, and rejection sampling produces inverse scaling. Evaluation uses AIME24 (30 problems, Asymptote figures), MATH500, and GPQA Diamond inside lm-evaluation-harness with vLLM (temperature 0 unless noted). The source includes tables comparing s1-32B against o1-preview, r1 series, Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking, and ablations on data selection and scaling methods, plus Algorithm 1 for diversity sampling, training samples, and example generations. Limitations noted: performance plateaus after ~6× forcing due to repetitive loops; context-window exhaustion occurs with 512-step prompts; vLLM batch-size, continuation, and tensor-parallelism nondeterminism affects long traces; decontamination uses 8-gram overlap only.</s>
