# A Dark Dimension Could Link Two of the Universe’s Great Unknowns

According to our best estimates, about 70% of the universe is dark energy, an unknown force pushing space to expand. Another 25% is dark matter, a mysterious material holding galaxies together. That leaves just 5% for the ordinary matter we can see and touch [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). These components are called "dark" because they are invisible; they don't emit, reflect, or absorb light, making them impossible to observe directly [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The standard model of cosmology, known as Lambda-Cold Dark Matter (Lambda-CDM), has long treated dark energy and dark matter as separate problems to solve. Their main connection was simply their shared elusiveness [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[3]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF). However, recent astronomical observations are pushing scientists to consider a more radical idea: what if these two are physically connected?

In 2024 and 2025, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration released data suggesting that the strength of dark energy hasn't been constant. The measurements indicate it peaked about 2 billion years ago and has been weakening ever since. Even more strangely, the data hinted that in an earlier era, dark energy might have actually grown stronger [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[3]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF). Physicists call this entering the "phantom regime" [[3]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF). This is analogous to a ball spontaneously rolling uphill. It seems to violate the laws of energy conservation unless some external force is acting on it.

This has led a growing number of theorists to suspect that the "external force" might be an interaction with dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait put it, while we have assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other... it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These new findings are challenging our simplest model of the cosmos. We next examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea of an interaction between dark energy and dark matter isn't entirely new. Back in 2005, a model by Justin Khoury and his collaborators explored a hypothetical question: could dark energy's density increase over time by drawing energy from dark matter [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[4]](https://cerncourier.com/four-reasons-dark-energy-should-evolve-with-time)? They discovered that if the two sectors could influence each other, it would create the appearance of phantom behavior without actually breaking any physical laws. As Khoury said, “It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this” [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Fast forward two decades, and the DESI results have brought this idea back to the forefront. In response, Khoury, along with Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden, developed a new model based on a dark-sector version of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory governing quarks and gluons. In this framework, the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles evolve together, in lockstep [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Another recent model, published in January 2025, proposes something similar. It suggests that dark matter could have transferred a small amount of its energy to dark energy during a previous cosmic era. One of the authors, cosmologist Elsa Teixeira, explained that since “dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion,” reducing that braking force would cause the universe's expansion to speed up [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The phantom behavior, then, seems to be a simple accounting error. When cosmologists analyze observational data, they typically assume dark matter is non-interacting and its density simply dilutes as the universe expands. Physicist David Andriot argues that this assumption is the source of the confusion. If dark matter's mass is actually changing over time due to an interaction, that change is incorrectly attributed to dark energy. He explains that “any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy” [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The effective dark energy fluid an observer infers is secretly receiving two contributions: one from the true dark energy (like a quintessence field) and another from the deficit or surplus in dark matter density compared to the standard assumption.

Harvard physicist Cumrun Vafa believes this points to a fundamental mistake in our approach. “The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong,” he stated. “That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior” [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The two quantities are linked at a deeper level and must be solved for simultaneously.

This interconnected view of the dark sector might also help resolve another major headache in cosmology: the Hubble tension. At issue is the current expansion rate of the universe. When measured using light from the early universe (the cosmic microwave background), scientists get one value. When measured using more recent phenomena like supernovas, they get a value that is about 9% higher. This discrepancy “has provoked heated debates... about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics,” wrote Teixeira and her co-authors. An interaction between dark energy and dark matter provides a natural explanation. By altering the expansion history of the universe over time, the interaction can reconcile the measurements from different cosmic epochs. In a world where the dark sector interacts, this difference in expansion rates is not a crisis but an expected outcome [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological interaction models receive a natural ultraviolet completion and common geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter are truly interacting, it suggests they might share a common origin [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Work in string theory—the idea that the universe is fundamentally made of vibrating strings—provides a potential framework for this connection. Building on this, Cumrun Vafa and his collaborators proposed that both dark energy and dark matter could be linked to a "dark dimension" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

String theory suggests the existence of extra dimensions beyond the three of space and one of time we experience. These are usually thought to be curled up at the tiny, undetectable Planck scale. The dark dimension proposal, however, suggests one of these extra dimensions is much larger. It is on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[5]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy). While still microscopic, this is enormous compared to the others [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

The mechanism is precise: gravitons, the hypothetical particles that carry the force of gravity, can leak into this larger dark dimension. When they do, they gain a small mass determined by the dimension's size and become "dark gravitons." These massive particles would exist within the dark dimension, but their collective gravitational pull would still be felt in our dimensions. This effect would perfectly mimic the behavior we attribute to dark matter [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This setup creates what physicist Georges Obied calls “a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter” [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the dark dimension would automatically affect both. The vacuum energy (dark energy) depends on the volume of the extra dimensions, and the mass of the dark gravitons (dark matter) depends on the dimension's radius. The two are intrinsically linked and cannot be varied independently.

In a July 2025 paper, Obied, Vafa, Alek Bedroya, and David Wu showed that this scenario aligns with the DESI data [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Their model predicts that both dark energy and dark matter mass will decrease over time, with the rate of change for dark energy being proportional to its tiny energy density. This explains the peak around 2 billion years ago and the subsequent weakening. “It won’t change fast,” Vafa explained. “It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small” [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

One testable consequence of this coupling is a new, long-range force between dark matter particles. As Obied said, “there could be astrophysical ways of testing this” [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In 2006, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden had already explored such a test. They theorized that if dark matter attracts other dark matter more strongly than normal matter, it would create a unique "tidal tail" when galaxies interact. This is an extended stream of stars, gas, and dust. They searched for this effect and, not finding it, set an upper limit on the strength of such an extra force. The value predicted by Vafa's team fits comfortably within this existing limit [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski remarked [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

While this alignment with astrophysical data doesn't prove the string theory models are correct, it is a significant step toward making the theory testable. For Vafa, who has worked for decades to connect string theory to the real world, any correspondence with experiments is gratifying [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Understanding the dark sector is a daunting problem, and attacking it from multiple, complementary directions is essential. As Obied said, “it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide” [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This interdisciplinary mindset, combining observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory, is how science moves forward, allowing us to slowly piece together the fundamental nature of our universe.
