# A Dark Dimension Could Link the Universe’s Greatest Unknowns

When you look at the cosmos, the stuff you can actually see—stars, galaxies, planets—accounts for just 5% of everything out there. The other 95% is a complete mystery. About 25% is dark matter, the invisible glue holding galaxies together, and a whopping 70% is dark energy, a strange force causing the universe to expand at an accelerating rate [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

You should know that these components aren't "dark" in the way you might think. They're called that because they're invisible; they don't emit, absorb, or reflect any light, making them impossible to observe directly with our current tools [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). For a long time, our standard model of cosmology, known as Lambda-CDM, has treated dark energy and dark matter as two completely separate things. But recent evidence is starting to suggest they might be physically connected [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In 2024 and 2025, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration released data showing that dark energy’s strength hasn't been constant. It seems to have peaked about 2 billion years ago and has been getting weaker ever since. Even stranger, the data suggests that in an earlier cosmic era, dark energy might have actually grown stronger [[2]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF).

This behavior is what physicists call the "phantom regime." Imagine seeing a ball roll uphill all by itself. Your first thought wouldn't be that the laws of physics are broken, but that some hidden force must be pushing it. That's exactly the situation here. This apparent violation of energy conservation has led theorists to wonder if the "hidden force" is an interaction between dark energy and dark matter [[2]](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DZ5qWCPK7LF), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Even though the default assumption has been that they “don’t have anything to do with each other,” said Tim Tait, a particle physicist at the University of California, Irvine, “you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe” [[3]](https://indico.cern.ch/event/473000/contributions/1993414/attachments/1209863/1764345/tait-Aspen.pdf).

These new observations are forcing us to reconsider our fundamental assumptions about the universe. We will next examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea of a connection between dark matter and dark energy isn't entirely new. Back in 2005, a model by physicist Justin Khoury and his collaborators explored a hypothetical question: could dark energy's density increase by drawing energy from dark matter? They discovered that if the two sectors could interact, it would create the appearance of phantom behavior without actually breaking the laws of energy conservation [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury explained [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Fast forward two decades, and the DESI findings have brought this idea back to the forefront. Inspired by the new data, Khoury, along with Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden, developed a model where dark energy and dark matter evolve together. Their framework is based on an analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory describing the strong nuclear force, but applied to the dark sector. In this model, the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles change in sync [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[5]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf).

A similar idea was proposed in a January 2025 study, which suggests that dark matter could have transferred a small amount of its energy to dark energy in a past cosmic era. "Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," explained Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the study's authors. By transferring some of its energy, dark matter effectively "eased up on the brake," allowing the universe's expansion to accelerate [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

So, why does this interaction create the illusion of a phantom regime? According to David Andriot, a physicist at CNRS, it comes down to a simple bookkeeping error. When cosmologists analyze observational data, they assume that dark matter's mass is constant and its energy density dilutes in a predictable way as the universe expands. If dark matter is actually interacting with dark energy and its mass is changing, that change in energy isn't being tracked correctly. Instead, it gets mistakenly added to the dark energy column on the cosmic ledger. This makes it look like dark energy is growing out of nowhere [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Cumrun Vafa, a physicist at Harvard University, puts it more directly. “The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong,” he stated. “That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This idea of an interacting dark sector does more than just explain away phantom energy. It also offers a potential solution to the Hubble tension. This tension refers to the ~9% disagreement between measurements of the universe's expansion rate today (from supernovae) and predictions based on the early universe (from the Cosmic Microwave Background). This discrepancy “has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics,” Teixeira and her co-authors wrote. In a universe with an interacting dark sector, the expansion history itself is different. The interaction changes how the expansion rate evolves over time, so the values measured from the early and late universe are no longer expected to match. What looks like a major crisis could simply be the signature of a deeper connection between the universe's two biggest mysteries [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological interaction models receive a natural ultraviolet completion and common geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter are truly interacting, it strongly suggests they might share a common origin [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Work in string theory provides a compelling framework for how this could be possible. Building on the idea that dark energy can change over time, Vafa and his collaborators proposed in 2019 that the mass of dark matter particles might also be dynamic. This led them to a unified concept: a "dark dimension" that could be the source of both [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

To make its equations work, string theory requires the existence of extra spatial dimensions beyond the three we experience. These are usually assumed to be curled up at the incredibly small Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). The dark dimension proposal, however, suggests that one of these extra dimensions is much larger—on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[8]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy), [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). While still tiny, this is enormous compared to the others. A study by Ivano Basile and Dieter Lüst found that an extra dimension of this size would produce a vacuum energy density that matches the observed value of dark energy [[8]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

This framework also provides a candidate for dark matter. Gravitons, the hypothetical particles that carry the force of gravity, could "leak" into this larger dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire a small mass determined by the size of the dimension, becoming what are called "dark gravitons." These massive gravitons would be confined to the dark dimension, but their gravitational pull would still be felt in our familiar dimensions, mimicking the exact effects we attribute to dark matter [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This is where the natural coupling comes in. “There is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter,” explained Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago. Because both phenomena are tied to the same geometric feature, any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously alter the density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter. The two are fundamentally linked [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied, Vafa, Alek Bedroya, and David Wu demonstrated that their "Fading Dark Sector" model, based on this idea, is consistent with the DESI data. The model predicts that both dark energy's strength and dark matter's mass should decrease over time, with the rate of change for dark energy being proportional to its own density [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[9]](https://indico.global/event/551/contributions/130592/attachments/61097/117637/Dark%20Sector%20and%20the%20Swampland.pdf), [[10]](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=24NnAxcAAAAJ&hl=en). Since dark energy's density is so small, Vafa noted, “it won’t change fast.” He added, “It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

One of the most exciting consequences of this coupling is that it predicts a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity. As Obied pointed out, “there could be astrophysical ways of testing this” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In fact, a test had already been done. In 2006, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden theorized that if such an extra force existed, it would affect how satellite galaxies are torn apart by larger ones. The additional attraction would make the dark matter halo of a satellite galaxy more tightly bound than its stars, creating a noticeable asymmetry in the streams of stars, or "tidal tails," left behind. They searched for this effect and, finding none, placed an upper limit on the strength of such a force [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The value predicted by Vafa's team's model was well within this existing observational constraint. “It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work,” Kamionkowski remarked [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

While this alignment with astrophysical data doesn't prove the string theory models are correct, it's a promising sign. For Vafa, who has dedicated his career to finding ways to test string theory, any connection between the theory and the real world is gratifying [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Ultimately, solving the puzzle of the dark sector will require a multi-pronged approach. As Obied puts it, it is beneficial to attack the problem from different angles—observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory. “This is how people should do science,” he said. “I mean, it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
