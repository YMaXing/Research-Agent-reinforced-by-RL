# The Anoxic Eye: How Birds Evolved Oxygen-Free Vision

In human medicine, a blocked retinal artery is a catastrophic event. Known as a retinal vascular occlusion, it starves the eye's light-sensing tissue of oxygen, causing rapid, widespread cell death and often irreversible vision loss. Yet, in the avian world, this "catastrophe" is the norm. Birds, from the soaring hawk that spots a mouse from hundreds of feet away to the common pigeon, operate with retinas that are almost entirely avascular. They have no internal blood vessels. This presents a profound biological paradox that has puzzled scientists for centuries.

The retina is one of the most energy-hungry tissues in the body, consuming oxygen and glucose at two to three times the rate of the brain itself [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). This intense metabolic demand made the absence of blood vessels in the bird eye seem impossible. For centuries, scientists assumed birds must possess some undiscovered, highly efficient mechanism for acquiring oxygen. Christian Damsgaard, an evolutionary physiologist at Aarhus University, noted the contradiction: "The bird retina is one of the most metabolically active tissues in the animal kingdom, yet it worked with no apparent blood perfusion. It was a complete paradox" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

A recent study finally resolves this mystery, and the answer overturns centuries of assumptions. Birds do not have a novel oxygen-delivery system. Instead, their inner retinas exist in a permanent state of anoxia, or total oxygen deprivation [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w). They survive by relying entirely on anaerobic glycolysis, a far less efficient metabolic pathway. This discovery documents the first known case of a vertebrate tissue operating in a lifelong anoxic state, pushing our understanding of biological extremes [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).![The evolutionary physiologist Christian Damsgaard measured gas exchange in bird eyes with microsensors. Surprisingly, the inner retina, a highly active tissue, used no oxygen.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/ChristianDamsgaard-crJesperEkmann-scaled.webp)
Image 1: The evolutionary physiologist Christian Damsgaard measured gas exchange in bird eyes with microsensors. Surprisingly, the inner retina, a highly active tissue, used no oxygen. - Jesper Ekmann

This finding is not just a biological curiosity. It offers a window into how metabolically active tissues can survive under extreme constraints, with direct implications for treating human conditions like stroke and ischemic diseases where oxygen deprivation is the primary cause of damage [[3]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036). Before you can appreciate how birds solved this paradox, it is important to revisit why oxygen came to dominate complex life and why its absence is normally catastrophic.

## Oxygenated Life: The Great Oxidation Event and Metabolic Trade-offs

Around 3.4 billion years ago, cyanobacteria developed photosynthesis, a process that steadily pumped oxygen into Earth's atmosphere. This Great Oxidation Event fundamentally reshaped the planet and the course of life. It triggered a mass extinction of anaerobic organisms and paved the way for a new, more powerful form of metabolism [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). Oxygen unlocked a new level of metabolic efficiency. The ancient pathway, anaerobic glycolysis, breaks down a glucose molecule to produce just two molecules of ATP, life's energy currency. With oxygen, however, aerobic respiration can wring out up to 30 additional ATP molecules from the same glucose molecule—a fifteen-fold increase in energy yield [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[4]](https://uol.de/en/news/article/birds-retinas-function-without-oxygen).

This energetic advantage was transformative. Organisms that could harness oxygen could grow larger, move faster, and support more complex structures, allowing them to outcompete those that could not. This evolutionary pressure locked complex, multicellular life into a dependency on aerobic respiration [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). As molecular physiologist Gary Lewin puts it, “We’ve been hooked on 20% [atmospheric] oxygen for millions of years” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). This dependency is most acute in high-energy tissues like the brain and retina. Without a constant oxygen supply, our energy-intensive neural processes shut down, and cells malfunction and die within minutes.![Birds, such as this alpine chough (in the crow family), use their exceptional vision to hunt, forage, and migrate.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/BirdFlying-crJean-PaulWettstein-scaled.webp)
Image 2: Birds, such as this alpine chough (in the crow family), use their exceptional vision to hunt, forage, and migrate. - Jean-Paul Wettstein

Still, tolerance for anoxia varies across the animal kingdom. While humans suffer irreversible brain damage quickly, the naked mole rat, a subterranean rodent, can survive for 18 minutes without oxygen by switching to a fructose-based anaerobic metabolism [[5]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aab3896). Some cold-blooded animals, like freshwater turtles, can survive for months at the bottom of frozen lakes in near-anoxic conditions by drastically lowering their metabolic rate [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). But for a warm-blooded animal to sustain one of its most active tissues in a permanent state of anoxia is unprecedented.![Naked mole rats can survive without oxygen for 18 minutes. To generate energy without oxygen, they use anaerobic glycolysis fueled by fructose.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NakedMoleRats-crJavierAbalos-scaled.webp)
Image 3: Naked mole rats can survive without oxygen for 18 minutes. To generate energy without oxygen, they use anaerobic glycolysis fueled by fructose. - Javier Ábalos

This metabolic context underscores the avian achievement. It also frames the central question that puzzled scientists for so long: if not from blood vessels, where did the oxygen come from? With this metabolic background established, we can now examine the mysterious structure that enables birds to push the vertebrate eye to an evolutionary extreme.

## A Mysterious Structure: The Pecten Oculi and Chronic Anoxia

For centuries, the answer to the avascular retina paradox was thought to lie in a bizarre structure called the pecten oculi. First described in the 17th century, the pecten is a comb-like, highly vascularized organ that protrudes into the eye's vitreous humor [[4]](https://uol.de/en/news/article/birds-retinas-function-without-oxygen), [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w). Its radiator-like appearance led to more than 30 different functional hypotheses, the most popular being that it served as an oxygen diffuser for the retina. However, as Damsgaard noted, “Nobody had really done direct physiological measurements on this structure. That’s where we came in” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

This was no simple task. The project took Damsgaard and a growing team of researchers eight years to complete. It required a collaboration with veterinary anesthesia experts to overcome the technical challenge of performing such delicate measurements on a living animal [[3]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036), [[8]](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/translational-medicine/how-bird-retinas-function-without-oxygen-may-inform-future-stroke-therapies).![A diagram showing the structure of the bird's eye, with the pecten inside the eyeball, and a cross-section of the retina showing light passing through to the retina.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Bird_Retina-Fig1-crMarkBelan_Desktopv1.svg)
Image 4: Mark Belan/ _Quanta Magazine_

Using delicate microsensors, Damsgaard’s team measured oxygen levels directly inside the eyes of living birds, including zebra finches, pigeons, and chickens. The results were definitive and shocking. The inner retina, the half of the tissue furthest from the choroid's blood supply at the back of the eye, had no oxygen at all [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w). In the team’s own words, their data showed that “oxygen diffusion limits the retinal oxygen supply and that the inner retina of zebra finches is fully anoxic under normal physiological conditions” [[8]](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/translational-medicine/how-bird-retinas-function-without-oxygen-may-inform-future-stroke-therapies). "That was 'striking'," Damsgaard said. "Half of the retina lives in a chronic state of anoxia, where there’s no oxygen present at all" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). The pecten was contributing less than 1% of the retina's total oxygen supply [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w).

If the inner retina was not using oxygen, how was it generating energy? The team turned to spatial transcriptomics, a technique that maps gene activity across tissue. They found a clear metabolic divide. Genes for aerobic respiration were active only in the outer retina, which receives oxygen from the choroid. In the anoxic inner retina, only genes for anaerobic glycolysis were active [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w). This less efficient process created an enormous demand for fuel; the inner retina required 2.5 times more glucose than other parts of the bird brain [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

This led the researchers back to the pecten. The same transcriptomics data revealed that genes for glucose transporters (GLUT1) were highly active in the pecten. The structure was not an oxygen supplier but a metabolic gateway. It pumped massive amounts of glucose *into* the eye and removed the toxic byproduct of anaerobic glycolysis, lactic acid, *out* of it [[4]](https://uol.de/en/news/article/birds-retinas-function-without-oxygen), [[3]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036). Genes for lactate transporters were also highly expressed in the pecten, preventing the acid from accumulating to damaging levels [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). The data suggests that specialized glial cells, known as Müller cells, which span the retina, work alongside the pecten to shuttle glucose in and waste out [[9]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen).![A grid of nine close-up photos showing the diversity of bird eyes.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/BirdEyeGrid-scaled.webp)

This system represents a metabolic strategy never before documented in a vertebrate. As neuroscientist Thomas Baden commented, “The insight that the retina basically goes oxygen-free, at least in some layers, is surprising. … It really gets properly down to zero” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). Jens Randel Nyengaard, a senior author on the study, described the work as “collapsing one house of cards and replacing it with another,” highlighting how new evidence can overturn centuries of scientific assumption [[3]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036). While cancer cells use a similar anaerobic pathway (the Warburg effect), and our muscles do so temporarily during intense exercise, the bird retina is the only known vertebrate tissue to survive in a permanent, healthy anoxic state for its entire lifetime [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). Having established how the avian retina operates, we can now trace when and why this radical solution evolved and what it teaches us about selective pressures and future applications.

## Eyes Like a Hawk: Evolutionary Origins, Selective Pressures, and Implications

The bird’s retina and its oxygen-free power system are so unusual they raise fundamental questions about how such a system could have evolved. This is a classic example of what biologist François Jacob called "evolution as tinkering" [[6]](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.860134). Evolution does not invent new systems from scratch like an engineer with a blueprint. It works like a tinkerer, repurposing existing parts for new functions. Instead of designing a novel oxygen-delivery system to support a thicker, more powerful retina, evolution rewired the bird eye to survive without it, co-opting the ancient pecten for a new metabolic role.

By comparing bird retinas to those of their reptilian relatives, researchers pinpointed when this change likely occurred. Reptiles like turtles and caimans have normal oxygen levels in their retinas. This suggests the oxygen-free adaptation evolved sometime during the dinosaur era, in the theropod lineage that gave rise to modern birds, after they split from crocodiles [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). Dan-Eric Nilsson, a sensory biologist who was not involved in the research, endorsed this interpretation, stating, “I fully support their interpretation that the anoxic retina probably evolved in the dinosaur lineage” [[9]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen). This evolutionary shift coincided with a thickening of the retina, a key clue to the selective pressures at play [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w).

The primary driver was likely an intense pressure for sharper vision. An owl tracking a mouse from the sky or a hummingbird locating hundreds of flowers daily requires exceptional visual resolution [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). In most vertebrate eyes, including our own, the network of blood vessels needed to supply oxygen scatters incoming light and physically obstructs the view of photoreceptor cells. By eliminating these vessels, birds could pack their retinas more densely with photoreceptors and other neurons, creating a smoother, higher-resolution sensor [[7]](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0008992). This avascular design, supported by the pecten's metabolic role, was a direct trade-off: sacrificing oxygen for optical clarity. As biologist Coen Elemans commented, “This pecten allows a snake eagle the incredible acuity of vision to spot a tiny still lizard from great heights” [[10]](https://www.sdu.dk/en/om-sdu/fakulteterne/naturvidenskab/nyheder-2026/bird-retina).

Damsgaard speculates that this system first evolved in theropod dinosaurs for tracking prey and identifying mates. Later, as birds took to the skies, it served as an exaptation—a trait evolved for one purpose that becomes useful for another. It allowed retinal function to be maintained during high-altitude flights where oxygen levels are low [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[2]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09978-w). However, some researchers remain cautious about extending these findings to all birds, especially migratory species that have not yet been studied in this context [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). Furthermore, key questions remain open. Researchers have noted the need for future studies to quantify the precise energy budget of this system and to clarify the pecten's role in removing other waste products, such as carbon dioxide [[9]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen).

The biomedical payoff is substantial. As researcher Jens Randel Nyengaard explains, “In conditions like stroke, human tissues suffer because oxygen delivery is reduced and metabolic waste accumulates.” The bird retina, he notes, offers a model for a system that “copes with oxygen deprivation in a completely different way” [[8]](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/translational-medicine/how-bird-retinas-function-without-oxygen-may-inform-future-stroke-therapies). By studying these evolutionary outliers, we can uncover fundamental principles of survival that may one day be harnessed to treat human diseases. As Damsgaard concludes, “Maybe we can get inspiration for how nature solved these problems by millions of years of natural selection. There’s so much to be learned from these animals that are able to do something that we cannot do” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).
