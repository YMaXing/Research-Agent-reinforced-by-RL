# Where Did Earth's Water Come From? The Three-Way Race Between Comets, Asteroids, and the Planet Itself

At this moment, a spacecraft is headed from Earth to Europa, an ice-veiled moon of Jupiter thought to contain a vast subsurface ocean. Affixed to the craft is a metal plate engraved with a poem by Ada Limón, which reads in part: “*And it is not darkness that unites us, / not the cold distance of space, but / the offering of water, each drop of rain, / each rivulet, each pulse, each vein*”. For decades, the search for water has dominated solar system exploration because, as far as is known, it is essential for life [[2]](https://poets.org/poem/praise-mystery-a-poem-for-europa).

It may come as a surprise, then, that scientists do not know for certain how water first arrived here on Earth. The question seems simple, but the answer is a complex story of cosmic bombardment, planetary evolution, and chemical alchemy. For years, the leading theory was that comets, icy relics from the dawn of the solar system, delivered our oceans. They were considered the most obvious candidates, given their high water content. But as spacecraft got close enough to "sniff" their vapor, the chemical evidence did not add up, forcing a major re-evaluation.

After that, “comets sort of fell out of favor,” said Ashley King, a meteoriticist at the Natural History Museum in London. Asteroids, rockier bodies that rain down on Earth more frequently, became the new frontrunner, as water found in meteorites looked much more like our own. This shift in consensus highlighted how isotopic data can overturn long-held assumptions. Yet, the asteroid theory also has its problems, leaving the door open for a more radical idea: Earth made its own water [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

This historical debate, from comets to asteroids and now to an internal source, reveals how science progresses. Early models faced falsification by new data, paving the way for alternatives. This article will first examine the evidence for and against comets and asteroids, looking at data from historic space missions. Then, it will explore the new theory that Earth generated its oceans from the inside out, a concept that could reshape our understanding of planetary habitability.

## A Showdown Between Comets and Asteroids

Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago. While much of its earliest eon has been lost to history, the basic story is agreed upon: it began as a ball of mostly molten rock and somehow became a blue marble. How?

Comets were the early frontrunner. These objects formed in the frigid outer reaches of the solar system, in a doughnut-shaped region of icy bodies beyond Neptune called the Kuiper Belt, or in the even more distant and nebulous Oort cloud. In these cold environments, water ice could easily survive and become a primary component of newly forming bodies. Compared to rocky asteroids, comets give you “a lot of bang for your buck,” said James Bryson, a meteoriticist at the University of Oxford, because they are so rich in water ice [[3]](https://www.earth.ox.ac.uk/article/earth-sciences-conversation-james-bryson). The idea was simple: a bombardment of comets could have delivered the oceans to a young, cooling Earth. But proving it required getting close enough to analyze their water.

The key to determining the origin of Earth's water is its "flavor," specifically the proportion of deuterium to normal hydrogen (the D/H ratio). Water (H₂O) is mostly made of standard hydrogen and oxygen, but a "heavy" version exists where one hydrogen atom is replaced by deuterium, a heavier isotope. This ratio is a critical fingerprint of where and when water formed in the early solar system. If comets supplied our oceans, their D/H ratio should be similar to ours.

In the 1980s, the European Space Agency (ESA) launched *Giotto*, its first deep-space mission, to investigate. Its target was Halley’s comet. *Giotto*’s measurements in 1986 were a surprise. “It didn’t match at all,” said Karen Meech, a planetary astronomer at the University of Hawai‘i. Halley’s D/H ratio was twice that of Earth’s water. More cracks in the comet theory appeared in the 1990s and 2000s, as spectroscopic observations of other comets like Hale-Bopp also found high levels of heavy water [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[4]](https://research.hawaii.edu/noelo/water-and-habitability).

The final blow came in 2014 from *Giotto*’s successor, ESA’s *Rosetta* mission. *Rosetta* made history by orbiting comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko and sending a lander to its surface. Its ROSINA instrument made the most precise measurements of a comet's composition to date and found that 67P had the highest D/H ratio of any comet measured, over three times that of Earth’s oceans. This result effectively ruled out this class of comets as the primary source of our planet's water [[5]](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Rosetta/Rosetta_fuels_debate_on_origin_of_Earth_s_oceans).

With comets largely ruled out, attention shifted to asteroids. These rocky bodies, mostly found between Mars and Jupiter, impact our planet far more often than comets. Scientists have collected thousands of meteorites and found that a particular group, carbonaceous chondrites, contains water with a D/H ratio that closely resembles our own. The meteorite that fell in Winchcombe, UK, in 2021 was recovered just hours after landing, providing a uniquely pristine sample. Analysis showed its D/H ratio was a near-perfect match for Earth’s oceans [[6]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abq3925).

This finding was confirmed by Japan’s Hayabusa2 mission, which collected samples directly from the asteroid Ryugu in 2018 and returned them to Earth. The analysis, free from terrestrial contamination, revealed that Ryugu's water also had a D/H ratio similar to Earth's. “The community is probably more favorable of asteroids than comets these days,” King said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[7]](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/2041-8213/acc393).

However, the asteroid-only model is not a perfect fit. Asteroids contain small amounts of noble gases like argon, krypton, and xenon, and their isotopic mixtures do not usually match what we find in Earth’s atmosphere, creating a significant chemical discrepancy [[8]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-022-00929-9).

Furthermore, both the comet and asteroid theories rely on a "late veneer." This is a period of intense bombardment after Earth’s initial formation and the cooling of its magma ocean. The existence and intensity of this bombardment are still heavily debated in the scientific community. These unresolved issues have led scientists to consider a radical alternative. Perhaps Earth did not need water delivered from space at all. This possibility challenges the entire exogenous-delivery paradigm and suggests our planet may have been far more self-sufficient than previously imagined.

## Hydrogen, Meet Magma

When astronomers simulate the formation of exoplanets, they find that many rocky worlds likely started with hydrogen-rich atmospheres. This has led scientists to wonder: could early Earth have been similar?

For a long time, the answer was thought to be no. This conclusion was based on enstatite chondrites, a type of meteorite with a chemical makeup so similar to Earth that they are considered our planet's primary building blocks. These meteorites appeared to lack hydrogen, suggesting Earth also formed "dry." However, recent studies found that hydrogen was there all along, hidden within organic molecules, silicate glasses, and sulfur compounds. This discovery opened the possibility that early Earth was awash in hydrogen, a key ingredient for making water [[9]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba1948), [[10]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0019103525001356).

This finding aligns with observations of exoplanets. The demographics of sub-Neptunes and super-Earths suggest that most rocky planets initially accrete thick, hydrogen-rich envelopes from their protoplanetary disks. If early Earth did the same, this hydrogen atmosphere would have blanketed a global magma ocean, which was full of oxygen locked in silicate melts. This created the perfect chemical and pressure conditions for water production. Under the extreme pressures and temperatures at the base of a magma ocean, dissolved hydrogen can react with oxygen atoms from the silicates to form hydroxyl groups (OH), which then exsolve as water (H₂O). This endogenous pathway bypasses the need for any external delivery of ice [[11]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-05823-0).

A 2023 study concluded that under such conditions, a planet could make its own water; they just were not sure how much. The answer came from a series of ambitious lab experiments designed to recreate the extreme conditions on young, hot exoplanets. The research team suspected that the immense pressure from a thick hydrogen atmosphere could facilitate chemical reactions with the underlying magma. “That higher pressure is a big part of what facilitates the water production,” said H. W. Horn, a physicist at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory. “It actually enhances the chemical reactions” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

To test this, they used diamond-anvil cells to put hydrogen under intense pressure (tens of GPa) and combined it with rock samples melted by lasers at thousands of Kelvin. The process took five years to perfect. “We broke a lot of diamonds,” said Sang-Heon Shim, a geophysicist at Arizona State University. The result was extreme: the reaction was so efficient that it produced far more water than predicted, up to 1,000 times more than previous theoretical models suggested. A similar study published around the same time reported comparable results [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[12]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09630-7), [[13]](https://faculty.epss.ucla.edu/~eyoung/reprints/Miozzi_etal_2025.pdf). “It doesn’t seem unreasonable [that you could] produce a huge amount of water quite quickly,” said Paul Byrne, a planetary scientist at Washington University in St. Louis. “And this is all homegrown, indigenous water” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

But could this have happened on Earth? “The paper doesn’t make strong claims about Earth,” Horn said, but both he and Shim think it is a valid link to make. The main issue is whether Earth’s early atmosphere contained enough hydrogen. Sub-Neptunes are far more massive, and their intense gravity is better at holding on to light gases like hydrogen. “Earth is right on the edge of where that kind of thing can start happening,” Horn noted. Quentin Williams, an experimental geophysicist, agrees that some water could have formed this way, but "how much might be generated is, however, pretty enigmatic" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

Some scientists are skeptical. “I’m a little bit doubtful whether you can have this for an Earth-mass planet,” said Anders Johansen, an astrophysicist at the University of Copenhagen. However, Byrne pointed out that the reaction might not need to last long to create a huge quantity of water. Earth may have been a water factory for only a moment, but that moment may have been enough. If so, the implications are vast. If rocky planets can be “born water-rich,” the requirement for a chance delivery of water is relaxed, expanding the number of potentially habitable worlds across the galaxy [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[14]](https://www.mpg.de/20541783/water-discovered-in-rocky-planet-forming-zone-offers-clues-on-habitability).

This endogenous mechanism, while powerful, does not necessarily exclude all external contributions. The final picture of Earth's water budget is likely a composite one. The next section will weigh this mixed-source synthesis, re-examine recent cometary data, and explore how increasing knowledge often replaces simple certainty with a richer, more nuanced story.

## Drowning in a Sea of Possibilities

While the indigenous water theory is compelling, it does not mean the story is over. In fact, comets are making a comeback. By 2014, scientists had studied the water of 11 comets, and all had D/H ratios unlike Earth’s, with one exception. In 2011, ESA’s Herschel Space Observatory found that the water in comet Hartley 2 had an Earth-like signature, a finding that seemed anomalous at the time [[15]](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/pia14737-heavy-and-light-just-right), [[16]](https://sci.esa.int/web/herschel/-/49386-herschel-finds-first-evidence-of-earth-like-water-in-a-comet).

Image 4: Comet Hartley 2, studied by the Herschel Space Observatory, has an Earth-like ratio of heavy to normal water. (Source: NASA/JPL-Caltech/UMD [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612))</em></small>

But in 2024, a re-investigation of *Rosetta*'s data from comet 67P suggested that the original high D/H measurement might have been skewed by dust in the comet's coma. The icy nucleus itself may have been more Earth-like. Then, in 2025, observations of comet 12P/Pons-Brooks also detected a D/H ratio similar to our oceans [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[17]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adp2191).

So, was it comets all along? Or asteroids? Or did Earth make its own water? “I suspect it was a combination of all of them,” Meech said. The most plausible model is a mixed one: Earth's oceans likely result from a combination of asteroidal delivery, some contribution from comets, and endogenous production within the magma ocean [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

The reason it is so hard to find a perfect extraterrestrial match for our planet’s chemistry is that it is a diverse cocktail. This initial mix has also been filtered over billions of years by Earth’s geology, atmosphere, and biology, all of which can alter isotopic ratios and erase original signatures. “We may never know,” she added. This puzzle is similar to the origin-of-life question. Just as deeper knowledge has replaced a single "warm little pond" with a network of possible pathways, our understanding of water's origins yields a more complex story. “The more you learn, the less you know—but the story becomes richer and more exciting” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[18]](https://www.aaas.org/membership/member-spotlight/where-did-earths-water-come-aaas-fellow-karen-meech-investigates).

## Conclusion

The quest to understand the origin of Earth’s water is a perfect example of the scientific process in action. What began as a seemingly straightforward question has evolved into a complex narrative involving multiple cosmic actors and planetary processes. We have moved from a simple "delivery" model to a richer story that includes comets, asteroids, and the surprising possibility that Earth itself was an active participant in creating its own oceans.

This journey from certainty to a more nuanced, multi-faceted understanding is not a failure of science but its greatest strength. Each new mission, each meteorite analysis, and each laboratory experiment adds another layer to the story. While a definitive answer may remain elusive, the pursuit continues to expand our understanding of how habitable worlds form, not just in our solar system, but across the galaxy.
