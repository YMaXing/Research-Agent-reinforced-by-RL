# The Platonic Representation Hypothesis

Read a story about dogs, and you may remember it the next time you see one bounding through a park. This is only possible because you have a unified concept of "dog" that is not tied to words or images alone. Bulldog or border collie, barking or getting its belly rubbed, a dog can be many things while still remaining a dog.

Artificial intelligence systems are not always so lucky. These systems learn by ingesting data in a process called training. Often, that data is all of the same type—text for language models or images for computer vision systems. This raises a fundamental question: to what extent do language and vision models have a shared understanding of dogs?

Researchers investigate such questions by peering inside AI systems and studying how they represent scenes and sentences. They have found that different AI models can develop similar representations, even if they are trained using different datasets or entirely different data types [[1]](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.01201v5). What is more, a few studies have suggested that those representations are growing more similar as models grow more capable [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987). In a paper from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, four AI researchers argued that these hints of convergence are no fluke [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987). Their idea, dubbed the Platonic representation hypothesis, has inspired a lively debate among researchers and a series of follow-up works [[3]](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16282), [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05283), [[5]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.03750), [[6]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02767).

The team’s hypothesis gets its name from a 2,400-year-old allegory by the Greek philosopher Plato. In his work *Republic*, Plato describes prisoners chained in a cave, facing a wall where they only see shadows of objects from the outside world. For them, these shadows are reality [[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory_of_the_cave). Plato maintained that we are all like those prisoners. The objects we encounter in everyday life, in his view, are pale shadows of ideal "forms" that reside in some transcendent realm beyond the reach of the senses [[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory_of_the_cave).

The Platonic representation hypothesis is less abstract. In this version of the metaphor, what is outside the cave is the real world, and it casts machine-readable shadows in the form of streams of data. AI models are the prisoners. The MIT team’s claim is that very different models, exposed only to the data streams, are beginning to converge on a shared “Platonic representation” of the world behind the data [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987). As senior author Phillip Isola puts it, "Why do the language model and the vision model align? Because they’re both shadows of the same world" [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). This idea echoes the concept of “convergent realism” in the philosophy of science, which suggests that scientific theories, over time, progressively converge on a true description of reality [[25]](https://phillipi.github.io/prh).

Not everyone is convinced. One of the main points of contention involves which representations to focus on. You cannot inspect a language model’s internal representation of every conceivable sentence, or a vision model’s representation of every image. How do you decide which ones are representative? Where do you look for the representations, and how do you compare them across very different models? It is unlikely that researchers will reach a consensus on the Platonic representation hypothesis anytime soon, but that does not bother Isola. "Half the community says this is obvious, and the other half says this is obviously wrong,” he said. “We were happy with that response" [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Having framed the Platonic representation hypothesis and its philosophical roots, we will now explore the precise geometric mechanism that makes such comparisons possible by examining how representations are compared through the company their vectors keep.

## The Company Being Kept

If AI researchers do not agree on Plato, they might find more common ground with his predecessor Pythagoras, whose philosophy supposedly started from the premise “All is number.” That is an apt description of the neural networks that power AI models. Their representations of words or pictures are just long lists of numbers, each indicating the degree of activation of a specific artificial neuron [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). Researchers typically write down these activations as a geometric object called a vector—an arrow that points in a particular direction in an abstract space. Modern AI models have thousands of neurons in each layer, so their representations are high-dimensional vectors that are impossible to visualize directly.

The geometric basis for comparison is not about matching vectors one-to-one, which is impossible between different models. Instead, evidence of conceptual alignment emerges when the relative distances and angles between many concepts match. If the "constellation" of concepts like "dog," "cat," and "wolf" has the same shape in two different models, we can infer that they share a similar understanding, even if their coordinate systems are completely different. Two representations are considered similar if their corresponding vectors point in similar directions and maintain a consistent relational geometry.

Within a single AI model, similar inputs tend to have similar representations. In a language model, for instance, the vector representing the word “dog” will be relatively close to vectors representing “pet,” “bark,” and “furry,” and farther from “Platonic” and “molasses.” This is a modern incarnation of an idea expressed more than 60 years ago by the British linguist John Rupert Firth: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps" [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). This principle also applies to neural networks, where representations can be reused to generalize from sparse data [[9]](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.11581v1).

What about representations in different models? It does not make sense to directly compare activation vectors from separate networks, but researchers have devised indirect ways to assess representational similarity [[10]](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.08775v1). One popular approach is to embrace the lesson of Firth’s quote and measure whether two models’ representations of an input keep the same company. Imagine you want to compare how two language models represent words for animals. You would feed a list of words—dog, cat, wolf, jellyfish—into both networks and record their representations. In each network, the representations will form a cluster of vectors. You can then ask: How similar are the overall shapes of the two clusters?

“It can kind of be described as measuring the similarity of similarities,” said Ilia Sucholutsky, an AI researcher at New York University [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). You would expect some similarity—the “cat” vector would probably be close to the “dog” vector in both networks, for instance. But the two clusters probably will not look exactly the same. Is “dog” more like “cat” than “wolf,” or vice versa? If your models were trained on different datasets or built on different network architectures, they might not agree [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

This same geometric approach is used in cognitive neuroscience to compare AI representations with brain activity. Researchers can extract “brain embeddings” from neuroimaging data like functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) or Electrocorticography (ECoG) recorded while a person listens to language. They have found that the geometric structure of these brain embeddings is surprisingly similar to the embeddings from language models, suggesting a potential alignment between artificial and biological language representation [[26]](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05036v1). More advanced methods go beyond comparing clusters of single concepts and instead align higher-order relational structures. For instance, some techniques build graphs where concepts are nodes and relationships (like “left of” or “behind”) are edges, then measure if the graph of linguistic relations from a text model aligns with the graph of geometric relations from a 3D vision model [[27]](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2026/papers/Tao_Geometry-Aware_Cross-Modal_Graph_Alignment_for_Referring_Segmentation_in_3D_Gaussian_CVPR_2026_paper.pdf).

Researchers began studying representational similarity with this approach in the mid-2010s and found that different models’ representations were often similar, though not identical [[12]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.07543). A few studies found that more powerful models seemed to have more similarities in their representations. One 2021 paper dubbed this the “Anna Karenina scenario,” a nod to the opening line of the famous novel. Perhaps successful AI models are all alike, and every unsuccessful model is unsuccessful in its own way [[13]](https://aiscientist.substack.com/p/musing-38-the-platonic-representation).

That paper, like much of the early work, focused only on computer vision. The methods worked well for comparing different convolutional neural network architectures trained on ImageNet, but extending them to language models proved difficult. The lack of large-scale, high-quality paired vision-language datasets meant there was no reliable "Rosetta Stone" to align the two modalities. This limitation kept cross-modal comparisons largely speculative until the recent explosion of multimodal models and datasets. With these measurement tools in hand, we can now look at the hierarchy of experimental evidence showing that convergence strengthens as models scale.

## Convergent Evolution

The question of why scaling works became central to AI research in early 2023, a few months after ChatGPT’s release. When performance improves with scale, are models simply memorizing more dataset quirks, or are they progressively approximating a true underlying world model?

“Everyone in AI research was going through an existential life crisis,” said Minyoung Huh, an OpenAI researcher then in Isola’s lab [[14]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). He, Isola, and their colleagues began discussing how scaling might affect internal representations [[14]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

The team identified a hierarchy of potential convergence evidence, in increasing order of strength. First, models trained on identical data might converge. Next, models trained on different data but the same modality could still converge, which would be more compelling evidence. Finally, and most strikingly, vision models looking at images might converge with language models reading only the corresponding captions. This would provide the strongest evidence that models are grasping shared features of the world behind the data [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). One proposed driver for this convergence is the “Contravariance Principle,” which argues that as models are trained to satisfy more and more task constraints, the set of possible internal representations that can solve all of them shrinks. In essence, general-purpose models are forced to become more alike because there are fewer “correct” solutions that work for everything [[25]](https://phillipi.github.io/prh). Recent research on scaling laws for multimodal models adds another layer, suggesting that performance is driven more by total compute than by model size or dataset volume alone, and that optimizing the mix of data modalities is key [[29]](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/bc1d640f841f752c689aae60b31198c1-Paper-Conference.pdf), [[30]](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.07951), [[31]](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/scaling-laws-native-multimodal-models), [[32]](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06754v4).

A year after their initial conversations, Isola and his colleagues decided to write a paper reviewing the evidence and presenting their argument for the hypothesis [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). By then, other researchers had found evidence of alignment between vision and language model representations [[15]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15162), [[16]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06555), [[17]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05224). Huh conducted his own experiment, testing five vision models and 11 language models on a dataset of captioned pictures from Wikipedia. He fed the pictures into the vision models and the captions into the language models, then compared the vector clusters. He observed a steady increase in representational similarity as models became more powerful, exactly as the hypothesis predicted [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

After reviewing the evidence for convergence, we must examine a host of experimental choices with respect to the measurements of representational similarity that may affect the validity and generalizability of the result.

## Find the Universals

Of course, it is never so simple. Measurements of representational similarity involve a host of experimental choices that can affect the outcome. Which layers do you look at in each network? Which of the many available metrics do you use to compare them, such as Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) or Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA) [[18]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/systems-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/neuro.06.004.2008/full), [[19]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00414), [[20]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06329)? And which representations do you measure in the first place? The choice of architecture also matters; while most research focuses on Transformers, alternatives like State Space Models (SSMs) offer different computational trade-offs and may develop representations differently [[33]](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v262/qiao24a.html).

“If you only test one dataset, you don’t necessarily know how [the result] generalizes,” said Christopher Wolfram, a researcher at the University of Chicago. “Who knows what would happen if you did some weirder dataset?” [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). Wolfram also critiques that such similarities often reflect structural constraints rather than meaningful convergence, questioning their generalizability [[21]](https://openreview.net/forum?id=8wKec6faAT). Other known failure modes include datasets with complex many-to-many relationships between concepts, scalability issues, and information that is inherently specific to one modality [[28]](https://akoepke.github.io/cave_umwelten).

This has led to two complementary scientific attitudes. One, associated with Isola, actively seeks the universals models appear to share. “The endeavor of science is to find the universals,” Isola said. “We could study the ways in which models are different or disagree, but that somehow has less explanatory power than identifying the commonalities” [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). Other researchers, like Alexei Efros at UC Berkeley, argue it is more productive to focus on where representations differ. Efros noted that Huh's dataset was designed for easy translation between image and text, unlike most real-world data. “There is a reason why you go to an art museum instead of just reading the catalog,” he said [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). The authors propose that this universal structure may reflect the pointwise mutual information (PMI) between underlying real-world concepts, a measure of their statistical co-occurrence [[25]](https://phillipi.github.io/prh).

Partial alignment can still be useful. Researchers have used this emerging alignment to translate internal representations of sentences from one language model to another [[22]](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12540) and to develop new ways to train models that learn from both language and vision data types [[23]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08492), [[24]](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/multimodal-alignment). In robotics, for example, shared embeddings allow agents to manipulate novel objects based on language instructions without explicit programming for each new case [[26]](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.05036v1).

Despite these promising developments, some researchers think it is unlikely that any single theory will fully capture the behavior of modern AI models. This tension between elegant Platonic explanations and the irreducible complexity of large systems remains. As AI researcher Jeff Clune notes, “You can’t reduce a trillion-parameter system to simple explanations. The answers are going to be complicated” [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).
