# The Widening Circle of Consciousness

In 2022, researchers observed bumblebees doing something remarkable. In a dedicated arena, with no reward or survival pressure, the bees voluntarily pushed and rolled small wooden balls. This behavior, which had no connection to foraging or mating, looked a lot like play [[38]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0003347222002366). This finding is part of a growing body of evidence suggesting that complex inner lives can exist in even the tiniest of brains, challenging our long-held assumptions about where consciousness resides.

For decades, a broad consensus among scientists has acknowledged consciousness in animals similar to us, like great apes. However, recent research has pushed the boundaries, suggesting consciousness may be widespread among animals evolutionarily distant from humans [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). This shift culminated in the 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, which formally embraces this expanded view. Its core claim is that “the empirical evidence indicates at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates (including all reptiles, amphibians, and fishes) and many invertebrates (including, at minimum, cephalopod mollusks, decapod crustaceans, and insects)” [[42]](http://www.nydeclaration.com/). The declaration deliberately uses the phrase “realistic possibility” to reflect scientific caution while acknowledging the weight of the new evidence.

Unveiled on April 19, 2024, at a conference at New York University, the declaration was spearheaded by philosopher Kristin Andrews, environmental scientist Jeff Sebo, and philosopher Jonathan Birch. It was initially signed by dozens of prominent researchers, including neuroscientists Anil Seth and Christof Koch, and philosophers David Chalmers and Peter Godfrey-Smith [[40]](https://www.kimmela.org/2024/05/05/a-new-declaration-on-animal-consciousness), [[42]](https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2024/04/animal-consciousness-declaration-new-york/678223).

The declaration focuses on phenomenal consciousness, a concept articulated by philosopher Thomas Nagel in his 1974 essay, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?". He argued that “an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism—something it is like for the organism” [[44]](https://www.cs.ox.ac.uk/activities/ieg/e-library/sources/nagel_bat.pdf). This is about raw, subjective feeling, not higher-order capacities like self-awareness or metacognition, which the declaration does not claim for these animals [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background).

This growing understanding of animal minds draws a sharp contrast with today's AI. While large language models can produce impressive text, they show none of the behavioral or physiological indicators of subjective experience seen in animals. As neuroscientist Anil Seth notes, the declaration should galvanize “an understanding and appreciation that we have much more in common with other animals than we do with things like ChatGPT” [[1]](https://www.theatlantic.com/science/archive/2024/04/animal-consciousness-declaration-new-york/678223).

The 2024 Declaration is the formal culmination of a rapid accumulation of behavioral evidence gathered over the last 15 years. The next section examines that evidence, explains why the field moved to a public declaration, and dismantles the old neural-complexity barrier.

## A Growing Awareness

The New York Declaration did not emerge from a vacuum. It was built on a foundation of striking behavioral research from the past 15 years that revealed surprisingly complex inner lives across a wide range of species [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background). These studies provided compelling evidence for subjective experience in animals previously thought to be simple automatons.

This shift represents a new paradigm, moving beyond observing intentional behavior to inferring internal states [[50]](https://advancedconsciousness.org/revisiting-animal-consciousness). However, this inference is not without challenges. Observing behavior can never give us direct access to another being’s subjective experience—a philosophical obstacle known as the "problem of other minds." Because we cannot be certain, the evidence relies on identifying behaviors that are best explained by attributing conscious states [[51]](https://www.animal-ethics.org/invertebrate-sentience-a-review-of-the-behavioral-evidence).

A key line of evidence comes from studies on pain and aversion. Octopuses, for example, show clear signs of experiencing pain. When given an injection of acetic acid in one chamber of a tank, they develop a lasting aversion to that place. Later, if given a pain reliever in a different chamber, they develop a preference for it, suggesting they value the relief from a negative affective state [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background). Similarly, crabs will balance their aversion to bright light against their aversion to electric shocks, making flexible decisions based on the intensity of each threat [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background). These trade-offs suggest a common currency for weighing different kinds of negative experiences, much like feelings do for us.

Memory research has also provided strong indicators. Cuttlefish have demonstrated a form of episodic-like memory, recalling not just what, where, and when a past event occurred, but also how they experienced it—for instance, whether they saw or smelled a piece of prey [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background). This capacity, known as source memory, points to a more detailed and personal recollection of past events. Other animals have shown signs of self-recognition, a building block of more complex consciousness. Cleaner wrasse fish have passed a version of the mirror-mark test, attempting to scrape off marks on their bodies that are only visible in a mirror. Garter snakes, which rely on scent, investigate their own scent more when it has been altered, suggesting they recognize it as their own [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background).

These examples highlight the need to design tests that consider animals on their own terms. A visual mirror test is inappropriate for a snake that navigates the world through smell, but a scent-based version revealed species-specific markers of self-recognition [[50]](https://advancedconsciousness.org/revisiting-animal-consciousness).

Evidence for positive experiences has also been found. The bumblebee study, where bees rolled wooden balls without any external reward, is a powerful example of apparent play behavior [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background). Zebrafish show sustained interest in new objects, suggesting they find learning intrinsically rewarding, a sign of curiosity [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background). Furthermore, studies on crayfish have shown that they exhibit "anxiety-like" states after being exposed to electric shocks, becoming more averse to bright areas of a maze. These states are reversed by benzodiazepines, the same drugs used to treat anxiety in humans [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background). Even fruit flies show complex states, with distinct active and quiet sleep patterns that are disrupted by social isolation [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background).

As this evidence mounted, an informal agreement grew among specialists. However, this consensus was not reaching policymakers or the broader scientific community. The declaration's organizers, including Jeff Sebo, felt it was time to make a clear public statement to "encourage reflection on animal welfare" and ensure that the "realistic possibility that an animal is conscious" merits consideration in policy [[32]](https://sites.google.com/nyu.edu/nydeclaration/background).

This new declaration significantly expands the scope of its predecessor, the 2012 Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness. The 2012 document focused on mammals and birds, stating they possess the "neuroanatomical, neurochemical, and neurophysiological substrates of conscious states" [[8]](CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.md). The 2024 declaration, by contrast, extends a "realistic possibility of conscious experience" to all vertebrates and many invertebrates. This careful wording reflects a more nuanced understanding of the evidence, acknowledging uncertainty while affirming that the possibility of consciousness is strong enough to take seriously [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419).

A long-standing objection to invertebrate consciousness was their vastly different neural architecture. Yet, this barrier is now seen as a failure of imagination. A bee’s brain has only about a million neurons compared to a human’s 86 billion, but its neurons are incredibly dense and complexly connected, sufficient for behaviors like play and social learning [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). Furthermore, nervous systems can be organized in radically different ways. An octopus has a highly distributed nervous system; two-thirds of its neurons are in its arms. A severed arm can continue to perform complex actions, like grasping and avoidance, for hours, showing that sophisticated motor control does not require a centralized brain [[10]](https://neuroscience.stanford.edu/news/octopus-brains), [[13]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8988249).

The belief that a cerebral cortex is necessary for consciousness has also been dismantled. As philosopher Kristin Andrews points out, birds, reptiles, and fish have different brain structures, but "some of those brain structures, we’re finding, are doing the same kind of work that a cerebral cortex does in humans" [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). The avian pallium in birds and the vertical lobe in octopuses are examples of structures that perform cortex-like functions such as memory integration and learning, despite their alien architecture [[47]](https://asknature.org/strategy/complex-memory-processing-in-the-cephalopod-vertical-lobe), [[49]](https://asknature.org/strategy/bird-brains-use-unique-structure-to-support-high-intelligence). Peter Godfrey-Smith agrees, noting that behaviors indicating consciousness "can exist in an architecture that looks completely alien" to ours [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419).

This is why studying invertebrates is so valuable. Octopuses and insects may represent instances of consciousness that evolved completely independently from the vertebrate lineage. Just as the octopus eye evolved separately from ours, its conscious experience is likely a fascinating blend of similarities and differences, offering a unique window into the nature of minds [[52]](https://undark.org/2023/07/14/interview-the-ethical-puzzle-of-sentient-ai).

The combined weight of this behavioral and architectural evidence has shifted the scientific default. Now, the focus turns to the ethical and practical consequences that follow from accepting this new reality.

## Mindful Relations

Accepting that there is a "realistic possibility" of consciousness in a vast range of animals forces us to reconsider our relationship with them. The ethical obligations extend beyond simply preventing pain. As Jeff Sebo argues, it is not enough to avoid causing suffering. We also have a responsibility to provide animals in our care with "the kinds of enrichment and opportunities that allow them to express their instincts and explore their environments and engage in social systems and otherwise be the kinds of complex agents they are" [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419).

However, applying these principles creates practical tensions, especially with insects. Our relationship with many insects is "inevitably a somewhat antagonistic one," as Peter Godfrey-Smith notes. Mosquitoes carry diseases, and other insects destroy crops. Unlike with fish or octopuses, the idea of making "peace with the mosquitoes" is not straightforward [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). This reality requires us to engage in explicit trade-offs rather than adopting a simple stance of avoiding all harm.

This new understanding also exposes significant welfare gaps in scientific research. While standards exist for the treatment of lab mice, little attention is paid to the well-being of insects like *Drosophila melanogaster* (fruit flies), which are used extensively in neuroscience and genetics. Under proposed NIH guidelines in the U.S., experiments on cephalopods would require approval from an animal care committee, a requirement that does not apply to any other invertebrate, including fruit flies or *C. elegans* worms [[19]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/policy/knowledge-gaps-in-cephalopod-care-could-stall-welfare-standards).

Policy can, however, follow the science. In the UK, a government-commissioned review confirming strong evidence of sentience in cephalopod mollusks and decapod crustaceans led to their inclusion in the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act in 2022. This law formally recognizes animals like octopuses, crabs, and lobsters as sentient beings deserving of protection, setting a precedent for how scientific consensus can translate into concrete policy [[6]](https://www.eurogroupforanimals.org/news/uk-sentience-bill-passes-final-stages-recognise-decapod-and-cephalopod-sentience-law), [[8]](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/CBP-9423/CBP-9423.pdf).

The conversation around animal consciousness provides a cautionary note for AI. While some researchers find current AI "very unlikely to be conscious," the shift in animal science counsels humility [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419), [[21]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ak3WuQhtoW4). Animal behaviors are plausible indicators of consciousness due to a shared evolutionary history, whereas AI may only produce superficial resemblances [[51]](https://www.animal-ethics.org/invertebrate-sentience-a-review-of-the-behavioral-evidence). Since sentience is a basis for moral standing, the distinction is critical [[53]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10436038).

Ultimately, the declaration is a call for more research. As Kristin Andrews suggests, labs should use their existing resources—nematode worms and fruit flies—to investigate the nature of their consciousness [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/insects-and-other-animals-have-consciousness-experts-declare-20240419). The goal is to stimulate work on even wider taxa, such as snails and worms, where evidence is currently limited, allowing for more informed judgments in the future [[54]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r5UoGpJ-Cwc). This research could help answer what consciousness is and how it arises.
