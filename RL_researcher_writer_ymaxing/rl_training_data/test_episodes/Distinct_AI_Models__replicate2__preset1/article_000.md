# The Platonic Representation Hypothesis

If you read a story about dogs, you form a mental picture. Later, seeing a dog in a park might remind you of that story. This connection is seamless because you have a unified, abstract concept of "dog" that isn't tied to words or images alone. AI systems, however, have historically been more fragmented. They often learn from data of a single type—text for language models, pixels for computer vision systems. This siloed learning raises a fundamental question: to what extent can a model trained only on text and another trained only on images develop a shared understanding of the same object?

Researchers investigate such questions by peering inside AI systems to study how they represent scenes and sentences. They have found two key patterns. First, different AI models can develop similar representations, even if trained on different datasets or entirely different data types. This convergence is not just theoretical; it shows substantial alignment with how representations are thought to develop in the biological brain [[8]](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.07987). Second, these representations grow more similar as the models become more capable [[2]](https://phillipi.github.io/prh). These findings form the basis of the "Platonic representation hypothesis," an idea that has inspired a lively debate among researchers [[1, 3]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

The hypothesis gets its name from Plato's 2,400-year-old allegory of the cave [[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory_of_the_cave). In his famous thought experiment, prisoners are trapped inside a cave, facing a blank wall. Their entire reality consists of shadows cast on this wall by objects passing behind them. Plato argued that our own perception of the world is similar; the objects we encounter are just imperfect shadows of ideal "forms" that exist in a higher, transcendent realm of truth.

In the AI adaptation of this allegory, the true reality outside the cave is the physical world. This world casts machine-readable shadows—streams of data like images, text, and audio—onto the "wall" that AI models can perceive. The models themselves are the prisoners, able to access only these data streams and nothing more. The hypothesis claims that as these models become more sophisticated, their internal interpretations of the shadows—their learned representations—begin to converge. They start to form a shared understanding, a "Platonic representation," of the true objects that cast the shadows [[1, 8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). As Phillip Isola, a senior author of the main paper on this topic, puts it, "Why do the language model and the vision model align? Because they’re both shadows of the same world" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Not everyone is convinced. A key point of contention is how to define and compare these representations across different models, a problem that plagues neuroscience as much as it does AI. In both fields, the term "representation" is widely used but lacks a consensus definition, making direct comparisons difficult [[12]](https://philosophymindscience.org/index.php/phimisci/announcement/view/53). Critics argue that observed similarities might be superficial or artifacts of the chosen measurement techniques rather than evidence of deep conceptual alignment. It is unlikely that researchers will reach a consensus soon, but that does not bother Isola. "Half the community says this is obvious, and the other half says this is obviously wrong," he said. "We were happy with that response" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Having framed the hypothesis and its philosophical roots, we will now explore the geometric mechanism that makes these comparisons possible, by examining how representations are compared through the company their vectors keep.

## The Company Being Kept

If researchers do not agree on Plato, they might find common ground with his predecessor Pythagoras, whose philosophy supposedly started from the premise "All is number" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). This is an apt description of the neural networks that power AI models. Their representations of words or pictures are just long lists of numbers, or vectors, pointing in a particular direction in an abstract, high-dimensional space that is impossible to visualize directly.

This geometric nature provides a basis for comparison. Even if the absolute coordinate systems of two independently trained models differ, we can find evidence of conceptual alignment. This is possible when vectors for the same concept point in similar directions or when the relative distances and angles between many concepts match across both models. Two representations are considered similar if their corresponding vectors point in similar directions [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

However, making these comparisons in high-dimensional spaces is not straightforward. Traditional metrics like Euclidean distance become less reliable as the number of dimensions grows, a phenomenon often called the "curse of dimensionality" [[13]](https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/cosine-similarity). Even cosine similarity, a more robust measure of vector direction, can saturate and lose its descriptive power when comparing massive datasets, potentially limiting its usefulness for very large models [[14]](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08623v4). This has led researchers to explore alternative, dimension-insensitive metrics.

The meaning of any concept inside a model is defined not by its absolute coordinates, but by its relationships to all other concepts. This is a direct application of the distributional hypothesis from linguistics, famously summarized by John Rupert Firth: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). To compare representations, we don't need to match individual vector components. Instead, we can ask whether the concept "dog" sits in the same relational neighborhood—close to "pet" and "furry," but far from "airplane" and "democracy"—in both models' spaces.

This leads to a powerful technique for measuring the similarity of similarities. Rather than attempting to directly align or rotate one model's high-dimensional space onto another—a mathematically ill-posed problem—researchers compare the internal geometries of concept clusters. For a set of concepts, one can compute a similarity matrix for each model, where each entry represents the similarity (e.g., cosine similarity) between two concepts. The core idea is then to compare these two matrices. This produces a single scalar score that quantifies how well the relational structure is preserved across models. As AI researcher Ilia Sucholutsky described it, "It can kind of be described as measuring the similarity of similarities" [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

This idea is mirrored in the "Anna Karenina scenario," a nod to the opening line of Tolstoy's famous novel [[3]](https://arxiv.org/html/2106.07682v5). The principle suggests that all successful, high-performing models are alike in that they converge toward the same optimal representational geometry. In contrast, every unsuccessful or under-trained model is unsuccessful in its own idiosyncratic way, failing to capture the true underlying structure of the data. This observed pattern—where success implies similarity and failure implies diversity—lends strong support to the idea that there is a single, "correct" structure for models to discover.

Much of the early work on representational similarity focused on computer vision, which was then the most popular branch of AI research. Scientists compared different convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures trained on the same datasets, like ImageNet, and found that their internal representations often shared similar structures [[1, 3]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). The advent of powerful language models, however, presented a more challenging and profound opportunity: to test whether similarity could bridge the gap between entirely different modalities. With these measurement tools in hand, we can now look at the hierarchy of experimental evidence showing that convergence strengthens as models scale.

## Convergent Evolution

The story of the Platonic representation hypothesis began in early 2023, a turbulent time for AI researchers. ChatGPT had been released a few months earlier, and it was increasingly clear that simply scaling up AI models made them better at many different tasks. But it was unclear why. This led to an existential question: when performance improves with scale, are models simply memorizing more dataset quirks, or are they getting better at approximating a true underlying world model?

"Everyone in AI research was going through an existential life crisis," said Minyoung Huh, an OpenAI researcher who was a graduate student in Isola’s lab at the time. He began meeting regularly with Isola and their colleagues to discuss how scaling might affect internal representations [[1, 8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

They considered a hierarchy of potential convergence evidence. The weakest evidence would be if models trained on the same data converge. Stronger evidence would be if models trained on different data but the same modality still converge. The most striking evidence, however, would be if models trained on entirely different data types, such as language and vision models, also converge [[1, 8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

A year after their initial conversations, Isola and his colleagues wrote a paper reviewing the evidence and presenting their argument for the hypothesis [[1, 8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). By then, other researchers had already found evidence of alignment between vision and language model representations [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Huh conducted his own experiment to test this. He used a set of vision and language models of varying sizes on a dataset of captioned pictures from Wikipedia. He would feed the pictures into the vision models and the captions into the language models, then compare the vector clusters. He observed a steady increase in representational similarity as models became more powerful, exactly what the Platonic representation hypothesis predicted [[1, 8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

This pattern of convergence is not unique to vision and language. Similar phenomena have been observed in other domains, suggesting a fundamental principle at play. For instance, researchers in materials science have found that as AI models learn the laws of physics, their internal representations converge on a shared geometric organization, a "platonic representation" that emerges from physical constraints rather than data artifacts [[15]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-026-01235-7). Likewise, models trained on embodied, sequential tasks have been shown to spontaneously develop computational structures that mirror those found in biological brains, suggesting that the demands of a task can channel learning toward universal solutions [[16]](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.11373v1).

After reviewing the evidence for convergence, we must examine the experimental choices that could affect the validity and generalizability of these results.

## Find the Universals

Of course, it is never so simple. Measurements of representational similarity involve a host of experimental choices that can affect the outcome. These include which layers to look at in each network, which similarity metric to use, and which representations to measure in the first place [[1, 8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Christopher Wolfram, a researcher who has studied representational similarity, critiques that these similarities might reflect shallow structural constraints rather than deep, meaningful convergence. For example, models might learn similar low-level feature detectors simply because they are efficient for processing natural images. He questions the generalizability of results from narrow datasets, noting, "If you only test one dataset, you don’t necessarily know how [the result] generalizes. Who knows what would happen if you did some weirder dataset?" [[1, 17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

This uncertainty has led to two complementary scientific attitudes. One, associated with Isola, actively seeks the universals that models appear to share. "The endeavor of science is to find the universals," Isola said. "We could study the ways in which models are different or disagree, but that somehow has less explanatory power than identifying the commonalities" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

The other attitude, held by researchers like Alexei Efros, argues that the differences between models are often more scientifically revealing than the similarities. He believes it is more productive to focus on where models’ representations diverge, as these are the areas that highlight their unique inductive biases and failure modes. Efros noted that in the dataset Huh used, the images and text contained very similar information by design. But most data we encounter has features that resist simple translation across modalities. "There is a reason why you go to an art museum instead of just reading the catalog," he said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Even with only partial alignment, there are immediate practical payoffs. Researchers have used this interchangeability to translate representations between language models and to train multimodal models more efficiently [[1, 6, 7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). In robotics, creating unified representations that abstract away the specifics of a robot's hardware allows a single policy to control different robots, enabling skill transfer between machines with significant embodiment gaps. This has led to substantial performance gains, with one study showing up to a 25.3% improvement in manipulation success rates [[18, 19]](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.14608v3). These findings show that even partial alignment can be engineered into more robust and versatile AI systems.

Despite these promising developments, other researchers think it is unlikely that a single theory will fully capture the behavior of modern AI. "You can’t reduce a trillion-parameter system to simple explanations," said Jeff Clune, an AI researcher. "The answers are going to be complicated" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). While the Platonic representation hypothesis offers a clean philosophical story, real models contain so many interacting parts that full convergence may coexist with vast regions of model-specific idiosyncrasy.
