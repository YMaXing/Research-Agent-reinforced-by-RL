# Is Gravity an Illusion?

Isaac Newton gave us the laws of gravity, but he was never comfortable with them. The idea that two objects could pull on each other across the vast emptiness of space, with no physical connection, struck him as a profound absurdity. "That one body may act upon another at a distance thro' a Vacuum, without the Mediation of any thing else... is to me so great an Absurdity," he wrote in a 1692 letter. He believed some hidden agent, "material or immaterial," must be responsible [[1]](https://philosophy.stackexchange.com/questions/122488/what-are-the-historical-philosophical-arguments-for-and-against-action-at-a-dist).

Newton’s contemporaries tried to fill this gap with mechanical "push" models. They imagined invisible streams of particles or swirling ether vortexes that physically pushed objects together, creating the appearance of attraction. This preserved a universe of tangible cause and effect, where nothing could be moved without being touched. Two centuries later, Einstein offered a more elegant solution. He replaced Newton's mysterious force with the curvature of spacetime. In general relativity, planets orbit the sun not because they are pulled, but because they are following the straightest possible path through a geometry warped by mass.

Einstein’s theory eliminated action-at-a-distance, but it too is incomplete. At the heart of a black hole or the birth of the universe, the equations of general relativity break down, and spacetime curvature becomes infinite. This signals that geometry cannot be the final story. Today, we see a minority of physicists exploring a radical alternative. This idea returns to Newton's era in spirit, if not in detail. They propose that gravity is not a fundamental force at all, but an emergent phenomenon, an illusion born from the statistical behavior of microscopic components.

This brings us to the world of entropic gravity. In this view, the universe is filled with unseen, randomly moving parts, like a thermal gas. The force we call gravity is simply the swarm's collective tendency to maximize its entropy, or disorder. As physicist Daniel Carney, a leading researcher in this area, puts it, our goal is to see if gravity can arise as a "thermal or entropic interaction, rather than through a fundamental quantum field" [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17575).

This is one of many ways we have tried to understand spacetime as emerging from a deeper, microscopic reality. While the dominant approach, the holographic principle, has captured most of the attention, the entropic view persists. It asks profound questions and, more importantly, suggests that gravity might not be an immutable law but a statistical tendency, one whose fluctuations could be detected in a lab.

To understand this idea, we first need to look at the clues within Einstein's own theory that connect gravity to the laws of heat.

## A Force Emerges

We see the need for a theory beyond general relativity most urgently at its breaking points: singularities. Inside a black hole, the theory predicts a point of infinite density and curvature where the laws of physics cease to apply. This is a clear sign that the smooth, continuous fabric of spacetime described by Einstein is an approximation of a more fundamental, microscopic reality.

Long before we began the search for quantum gravity, general relativity itself contained hints of a deeper connection to thermodynamics. The area of a black hole's event horizon, for instance, never decreases, much like entropy in a closed system can only increase. When Stephen Hawking showed that quantum effects cause black holes to radiate heat and have a temperature, the analogy became an identity. A black hole is a thermal object. But if it has a temperature, you might ask what it's made of. It must have microscopic degrees of freedom whose statistical mechanics give rise to these thermal properties [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holographic_principle).

There are multiple ideas about how spacetime might emerge from these microscopic parts. The most popular is the holographic principle. This suggests that our three-dimensional universe is an illusion, a projection of information encoded on a distant, two-dimensional surface, much like a hologram. In this picture, gravity in our 3D world emerges from the quantum interactions of particles on that 2D boundary [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holographic_principle).

In 1995, however, we saw physicist Ted Jacobson propose a different path. Instead of starting with gravity and deriving its thermodynamic properties, he flipped the logic on its head. He assumed from the outset that spacetime has thermal properties. Specifically, he demanded that the fundamental relation connecting heat, temperature, and entropy (`δQ = TdS`) holds for all local Rindler causal horizons through each point in spacetime.

In his framework, `δQ` is the flux of matter energy crossing the horizon, `T` is the Unruh temperature perceived by an accelerated observer, and `dS` is the change in the horizon's area. For this thermodynamic relation to hold universally, the gravitational lensing caused by matter must distort spacetime in a very specific way. Jacobson showed that this constraint is precisely equivalent to Einstein's field equations. Gravity, in this view, is not a geometric necessity but an equation of state for the "atoms of spacetime." In 2010, Erik Verlinde pushed this idea further, arguing that gravity is purely an entropic force [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/gr-qc/9504004), [[5]](https://krishnamohan-parattu.weebly.com/uploads/6/4/3/1/64317995/einstein-eq-and-thermodyn.pdf), [[14]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1001.0785).

This was a significant result. It showed that the parallel between gravity and heat was no mere coincidence. As Carney states, "He turned black hole thermodynamics on its head. I’ve been mystified by this result for my entire adult life." Now that we have established this powerful thermodynamic foundation, we can explore modern models that bring this idea to life [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/is-gravity-just-entropy-rising-long-shot-idea-gets-another-look-20250613).

## Apparent Attraction

Inspired by Jacobson's work, we see Carney and his co-authors recently proposing two concrete models showing how Newtonian gravity could emerge from the statistical mechanics of microscopic components. Instead of a fundamental force, the attraction between two masses becomes an "apparent attraction," an illusion created by the underlying system's drive toward maximum entropy [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17575).

The first model imagines space as a crystalline grid filled with quantum bits, or qubits. Each qubit has an orientation, like a tiny compass needle. "If you put a mass somewhere in the lattice, it causes all of the qubits nearby to get polarized—they all try to go in the same direction," Carney explained. This polarization creates a small "pocket of order" in the otherwise random sea of qubits. High order means low entropy. The universe, however, relentlessly seeks to increase its total entropy [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/is-gravity-just-entropy-rising-long-shot-idea-gets-another-look-20250613).

If you place a second mass in the lattice, it creates another pocket of order. The system can increase its overall entropy by minimizing the total volume of these ordered regions. The most efficient way to do this is to push the two masses closer together, effectively merging their low-entropy pockets into a smaller, combined area. To an outside observer, this looks like an attractive force pulling the masses toward each other. In reality, the qubits are doing all the work, driven by the second law of thermodynamics. This mechanism naturally produces a force that weakens with the square of the distance, perfectly mimicking Newton's law [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17575).

The second model does away with the local lattice, better capturing the instantaneous "action at a distance" character of Newtonian gravity. In this version, the qubits are not fixed in space but can interact non-locally. The mechanism here relies on what can be described as a variable energy capacity for each qubit. When two masses approach each other, the effective energy capacity of the qubits changes. A fixed amount of total energy must then be distributed differently among the available qubits.

This redistribution increases the number of ways the energy can be arranged—the number of accessible microstates—which, by definition, increases the system's entropy. Therefore, the system entropically favors configurations where the masses are closer, as this maximizes the number of available states. Once again, what appears to be gravitational attraction is simply the system settling into its most statistically probable, highest-entropy state. Both models, one local and one non-local, paint a picture of gravity as an epiphenomenon, an after-effect of the universe's fundamental tendency to increase disorder. While these constructions successfully recover Newtonian gravity, they come with significant limitations [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17575).

## Strengths and Weaknesses

While the qubit models provide a useful proof-of-concept, they are far from a complete theory of gravity. A major weakness is their ad-hoc nature. The models require researchers to manually set the coupling strengths between masses and qubits, as well as the lattice spacing or the nature of non-local connections. These parameters are fine-tuned to produce the right answer, rather than being derived from a more fundamental principle. Carney himself acknowledges this, stating the models are not meant to be realistic but rather "explicit, proof-of-concept examples" [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17575).

Furthermore, the models only reproduce Newton's law in the weak-field limit. They do not recover the full, dynamic, curved spacetime of general relativity. A more significant issue, noted by physicist Mark Van Raamsdonk, is their failure to naturally incorporate the equivalence principle. This is the observation that all objects fall at the same rate regardless of their mass or composition. "Their construction doesn’t really have anything to do with gravity,” he said. If gravity is a statistical force arising from interactions with a sea of qubits, it is unclear why the coupling to these qubits would be perfectly universal for all types of matter [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/is-gravity-just-entropy-rising-long-shot-idea-gets-another-look-20250613).

Others have questioned the core thermodynamic assumption itself, arguing that while black hole horizons exhibit thermal properties, ordinary spacetime surfaces do not, which could undermine the entire emergent gravity program. The models also sidestep the hardest problems in gravity, like the behavior of black holes and the nature of singularities, by focusing only on the weak-field regime where physics is already well understood. “The real challenge in gravitational physics is understanding its strong-coupling, strong-field regime,” said Ramy Brustein, a theorist at Ben-Gurion University. In some thermodynamic gravity models, the strong-field, high-temperature limit is where the theory simply reduces back to standard general relativity, with deviations only appearing in the very weak-field, low-temperature regime [[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropic_gravity), [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/is-gravity-just-entropy-rising-long-shot-idea-gets-another-look-20250613), [[10]](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.08236v1).

However, there is a counter-argument. If gravity is indeed a statistical phenomenon, its effects might be most visible not in the extreme environment of a black hole, but in the subtle fluctuations of weak gravitational fields. Standard general relativity predicts a perfectly smooth, deterministic interaction. An entropic model, on the other hand, implies that the gravitational force should be inherently noisy, subject to the random thermal fluctuations of its underlying microscopic constituents. These tiny, random deviations from Newton's inverse-square law could, in principle, be measured. These weaknesses notwithstanding, the entropic gravity picture makes distinctive predictions about quantum systems that open avenues for experimental tests [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17575).

## Testing Entropic Gravity

"The main benefit of the new models is that they prompt conceptual questions about gravity and open up new experimental directions," Carney said. This opens the door to experimental tests that could distinguish entropic gravity from standard theories. The key lies at the intersection of gravity and quantum mechanics [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/is-gravity-just-entropy-rising-long-shot-idea-gets-another-look-20250613).

Suppose you place a massive object in a quantum superposition of two different locations. According to standard quantum theory, its gravitational field should also enter a superposition. But what if gravity is entropic?

In the entropic picture, the two separate positions of the mass would create two distinct "pockets of order" in the underlying qubit field. The system's relentless drive to maximize entropy would strongly disfavor this state. Instead, the interactions with the qubits would act like a measurement, forcing the superposition to collapse into a single, definite position to minimize the entropic cost. This predicted decoherence is a concrete, testable effect [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17575).

This idea connects directly to a class of theories known as "objective wave-function collapse" models. These theories, such as the Ghirardi-Rimini-Weber (GRW) or Continuous Spontaneous Localization (CSL) models, propose that the Schrödinger equation is only an approximation. They modify it by introducing a physical mechanism, often linked to gravity, that causes superpositions of massive objects to spontaneously collapse. Both entropic gravity and objective collapse models predict similar experimental signatures, such as mass-dependent decoherence rates that deviate from standard quantum predictions [[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Objective-collapse_theory), [[7]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11305101).

Tabletop experiments using highly sensitive nanofabricated torsion pendulums are being developed to measure minute gravitational interactions. These are already placing ever-tighter constraints on collapse models. The same experiments could be used to search for the fluctuations and decoherence effects predicted by entropic gravity. Some theorists even suggest that signatures could appear in strong-field environments detectable by gravitational-wave observatories [[8]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yPvRQ5nyKZY), [[12]](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.11366v1), [[13]](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202601.2277).

Even if these long-shot theories prove incorrect, exploring them deepens our understanding of emergence. As Mark Van Raamsdonk noted, “Since it hasn’t been established that actual gravity in our universe arises holographically, it’s certainly valuable to explore other mechanisms by which gravity might arise”. They suggest the immutable laws we observe may arise from a chaotic, statistical reality. Perhaps gravity is not a law written in stone, but the most likely outcome of a cosmic roll of the dice [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/is-gravity-just-entropy-rising-long-shot-idea-gets-another-look-20250613).
