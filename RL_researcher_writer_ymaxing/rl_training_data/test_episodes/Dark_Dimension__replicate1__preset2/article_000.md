# A Dark Dimension: The Hidden Link Between Dark Matter and Dark Energy

Our universe, as we observe it, is mostly invisible. According to current estimates, about 70% consists of dark energy, an unknown force driving cosmic expansion, and another 25% is dark matter, a mysterious substance holding galaxies together. The ordinary matter we can see and interact with makes up a mere 5% of the cosmos. Semantically, "dark" is a misnomer; these components are invisible, as they do not emit, reflect, or absorb light, and have so far been impossible to observe directly.

For decades, the standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), has treated dark energy and dark matter as separate, unrelated phenomena. Dark energy is modeled as the cosmological constant (Λ), an unchanging property of space itself, while Cold Dark Matter (CDM) is a stable, non-interacting particle. The concept of a cosmological constant has a winding history. Einstein first added it to his equations to allow for a static universe, but dismissed it when the universe was found to be expanding. Decades later, the idea was reclaimed to represent the constant dark energy driving the accelerated expansion, forming the basis of the standard model for the last 20 years [[29]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time). However, recent astronomical observations are challenging this assumption, suggesting the two might be physically intertwined.

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration released groundbreaking results in 2024 and 2025. By meticulously mapping the positions of tens of millions of galaxies and quasars, DESI created the largest 3D map of the universe to date. This unprecedented map allows cosmologists to measure the universe's expansion history with high precision. From this data, DESI found compelling evidence that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The measurements indicate it may have peaked about two billion years ago and has been weakening since. More strangely, in an even earlier cosmic era, dark energy might have grown stronger, seemingly defying the law of energy conservation [[5]](https://www.ohio.edu/news/2025/03/universe-might-be-changing-new-desi-data-shows-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time), [[16]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve). Researchers call this entering the "phantom regime." The situation is like a ball spontaneously rolling uphill; it is possible, but only if an unseen force is pushing it.

This apparent violation of physical law has spurred a growing number of theorists to investigate the possibility that dark energy's influencer is its connection to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait notes, while scientists have long assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe."

This article will explore the theoretical models that resolve this apparent violation. First, we will examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a simple bookkeeping artifact. Then, we will see how these same models can, in the process, help ease the long-standing Hubble tension. Finally, we will connect these ideas to a deeper origin within string theory.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. In 2005, a model by physicist Justin Khoury and his collaborators posed a hypothetical question: could there be a form of dark energy whose energy density increased over time? They found that if dark energy and dark matter could transfer energy between each other, they could produce what looked like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[6]](https://cerncourier.com/four-reasons-dark-energy-should-evolve-with-time), [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury explained [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury, along with Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden, to construct a model of dark interactions based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a cornerstone of particle physics. In their new model, both the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter change in concert, providing an explicit field-theoretic realization of the coupling [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[18]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf).

A similar model published in January 2025 proposes that dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy in a past cosmic era. Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the study's authors, explained that "dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," so easing up on that brake by reducing dark matter's energy would have caused the expansion to accelerate [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The appearance of a phantom regime is essentially a bookkeeping problem. David Andriot, a physicist at CNRS, argues that when cosmologists analyze data, they assume the mass of dark matter is constant. Therefore, "any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy" [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This happens when an evolving dark matter mass is incorrectly logged in the dark energy ledger instead of being properly tracked as a time-dependent property of dark matter itself.

Harvard physicist Cumrun Vafa agrees, stating that "the notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong." He argues that this flawed assumption, made by many cosmologists and the DESI team, "led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The two quantities are fundamentally linked and must be solved for simultaneously.

Beyond explaining phantom energy, these coupled-sector scenarios can also help resolve one of cosmology's most persistent problems: the Hubble tension. This tension refers to the approximately 9% discrepancy between measurements of the universe's current expansion rate. Measurements using early-universe light (the cosmic microwave background) predict a slower expansion rate than what is observed locally using more recent phenomena like supernovae. As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, this discrepancy "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics" [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Their model shows that in a universe where dark energy and dark matter interact, this difference in expansion rates is not a crisis but an expected outcome, as the interaction alters the expansion history between the early and late universe.

These phenomenological models find a natural home and a common geometric origin within string theory. Next, we will explore this connection through the "dark dimension" proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

The turn to string theory is motivated by the cosmological constant problem, where quantum theory predicts a vacuum energy 10¹²⁰ times larger than what is observed. This discrepancy has led physicists to explore alternative frameworks [[30]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy). If dark energy and dark matter interact, it could mean they share a common origin [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Work in string theory—the idea that the universe is made of vibrating strings at its most fundamental level—suggests how this might be possible. Vafa's insight stems from the "distance conjecture" in string theory. It posits that when a parameter like the cosmological constant is extremely small, a tower of unexpectedly light particles must appear [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). Building on this idea, Cumrun Vafa and collaborators proposed in 2019 and 2022 that dark matter particle masses might also be time-dependent. This led them to a shared link: a so-called dark dimension [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

String theory posits the existence of six or seven extra spatial dimensions. These are typically thought to be minuscule, near the Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). However, the researchers proposed that one of these—the dark dimension—could be significantly larger, on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). That is 29 orders of magnitude larger than the others [[9]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

The mechanism works like this: gravitons, the theoretical particles that mediate gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire mass and become "dark gravitons." These massive gravitons would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would be felt in our familiar dimensions, fulfilling the role typically ascribed to dark matter [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This model also explains why gravity is so much weaker than other forces. Gravity's strength is diluted as it spreads across all extra dimensions, including the larger dark dimension, making it appear feeble in our world [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

This scenario creates what Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago, calls "a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter" [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both dark energy (through its dependence on the volume of spacetime) and dark matter (since the mass of dark gravitons depends on the dimension's radius).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya and David Wu, demonstrated that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Their model predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will both decrease over time. It also predicts that the rate at which dark energy changes is proportional to its own energy density. Because astrophysical measurements show that dark energy's density is extraordinarily small, "it won’t change fast," Vafa said. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A direct consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity. Obied noted that "there could be astrophysical ways of testing this" [[22]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). One key test involves "kick velocities" from decaying dark gravitons. The resulting energy could help particles escape forming galaxies, making them slightly less massive than standard models predict. Recent survey data is consistent with this effect [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

Coincidentally, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden had already looked into such a test back in 2006. They theorized that if dark matter has a stronger gravitational attraction to other dark matter than to ordinary matter, a special kind of "tidal tail"—an extended stream of stars and gas—would form behind a smaller galaxy as it is pulled apart by a larger one. They looked for this effect and did not find it, which allowed them to set an upper limit on the strength of this extra force. The value predicted by Vafa's team fell well within this observational bound [[22]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski said. The theory is also testable in labs. If gravity leaks into a micron-scale dimension, its force should deviate from predictions at that specific distance. High-precision tabletop experiments are currently searching for such a deviation [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

The fact that a prediction from string theory roughly agrees with astrophysical evidence does not confirm these "stringy" models. But any correspondence between theory and experiment is gratifying to Vafa, who has spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the purely conceptual realm of string theory [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The proposal has its skeptics, who argue the underlying conjectures are ambitious and lack strong evidence. As Oxford physicist Joseph Conlon commented, "There are many ideas that would be important if true, but are probably not. This is one of them" [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

Understanding the dark sector is a daunting problem, and as Obied suggests, it is beneficial to approach it from different angles—observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory. "This is how people should do science," he said. "It’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
