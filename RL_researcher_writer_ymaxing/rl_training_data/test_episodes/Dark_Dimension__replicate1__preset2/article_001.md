# A Dark Dimension: The Hidden Link Between Dark Matter and Dark Energy

As AI engineers, we are used to dealing with the unseen. We work with abstract models and vast datasets that are invisible to the end-user. But the universe itself presents a similar challenge on a cosmic scale. According to our current understanding, about 70% of the cosmos consists of dark energy, an unknown force driving its expansion, and another 25% is dark matter, a mysterious substance holding galaxies together [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The ordinary matter we can see and touch makes up a mere 5% of everything. "Dark" is really just a placeholder for our ignorance; these components are invisible because they don't emit, reflect, or absorb light, making them impossible to observe directly [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

For decades, the standard model of cosmology, known as Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), has treated these two phenomena as separate and unrelated. In this model, dark energy is the cosmological constant (Λ), an unchanging property of space, while Cold Dark Matter (CDM) is a stable, non-interacting particle. This model has served us well for about 20 years [[1]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time). However, recent astronomical observations are starting to challenge this picture, suggesting the two might be physically connected.

The Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration released some compelling results in 2024 and 2025. By mapping the positions of tens of millions of galaxies, DESI created the largest 3D map of the universe ever made, allowing us to measure its expansion history with incredible precision. The data suggests that the strength of dark energy hasn't been constant. It appears to have peaked about two billion years ago and has been weakening ever since. Even more puzzling, in an earlier cosmic era, dark energy might have grown stronger [[2]](https://www.ohio.edu/news/2025/03/universe-might-be-changing-new-desi-data-shows-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time), [[3]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve).

Researchers call this behavior entering the "phantom regime." It is like watching a ball spontaneously roll uphill. This seems to violate the laws of energy conservation unless some external force is at play. This apparent paradox has led a growing number of theorists to investigate the possibility that dark energy is coupled to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait puts it, while we have long assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In this article, we will explore the theoretical models that resolve this apparent violation. We will first examine how dark-interaction models can explain the phantom appearance as a simple bookkeeping artifact and, in doing so, help ease the long-standing Hubble tension. Then, we will connect these ideas to a deeper origin within string theory.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. Back in 2005, a model by physicist Justin Khoury and his collaborators asked a simple question: could dark energy's density increase over time by drawing energy from dark matter? They found that if the two sectors could exchange energy, it would create the appearance of phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[4]](https://cerncourier.com/four-reasons-dark-energy-should-evolve-with-time), [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury said [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Two decades later, the DESI findings prompted Khoury, along with Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden, to build a model of these dark interactions based on an analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory describing the strong nuclear force. In their new model, the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles change in lockstep, providing a concrete field-theoretic framework for the coupling [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[5]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf).

Another model, published in January 2025, imagines something similar. It proposes that dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy during a past cosmic era. Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the study's authors, explained that "dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion." Easing up on that brake by reducing dark matter's energy would naturally cause the universe's expansion to accelerate [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The appearance of a phantom regime, then, is essentially a bookkeeping problem. David Andriot, a physicist at CNRS, argues that when we analyze cosmological data, we assume the mass of dark matter is constant. Because of this assumption, "any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy" [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In other words, if dark matter's mass is actually changing over time, our current methods would misinterpret that change as dark energy behaving strangely.

Harvard physicist Cumrun Vafa agrees, stating that "the notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong." He argues that this flawed assumption, made by many cosmologists including the DESI team, "led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The two quantities are fundamentally linked and must be solved for together.

Beyond explaining phantom energy, these coupled-sector scenarios can also help resolve one of cosmology's most persistent problems: the Hubble tension. This tension refers to the roughly 9% discrepancy between measurements of the universe's current expansion rate. Measurements using early-universe light, the cosmic microwave background (CMB), predict a slower expansion rate than what we observe locally using more recent phenomena like supernovae. As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, this discrepancy "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics" [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Their model shows that in a universe where dark energy and dark matter interact, this difference in expansion rates is not a crisis but an expected outcome. The interaction simply alters the expansion history between the early and late universe.

These phenomenological models find a natural home and a common geometric origin within string theory. We will explore this connection next through the "dark dimension" proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

The turn to string theory is motivated by the cosmological constant problem, where quantum theory predicts a vacuum energy 10¹²⁰ times larger than what we observe. This massive discrepancy has led physicists to explore alternative frameworks [[8]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy). If dark energy and dark matter interact, it could mean they share a common origin [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Work in string theory—the idea that the universe is made of vibrating strings at its most fundamental level—suggests how this might be possible. Vafa's insight comes from the "distance conjecture" in string theory. It posits that when a parameter like the cosmological constant is extremely small, a tower of unexpectedly light particles must appear [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). Building on this, Cumrun Vafa and collaborators proposed in 2019 and 2022 that dark matter particle masses might also be time-dependent. This led them to a shared link: a so-called dark dimension [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

String theory posits the existence of six or seven extra spatial dimensions. These are typically thought to be minuscule, near the Planck scale of 10⁻³⁵ meters. However, the researchers proposed that one of these dimensions could be significantly larger. This "dark dimension" could be on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). That is 29 orders of magnitude larger than the others [[8]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

The mechanism works like this: gravitons, the theoretical particles that mediate gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire mass and become "dark gravitons." These massive gravitons would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would be felt in our familiar dimensions, fulfilling the role typically ascribed to dark matter [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This model also explains why gravity is so much weaker than other forces. Gravity's strength is diluted as it spreads across all extra dimensions, including the larger dark dimension, making it appear feeble in our world [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

This scenario creates what Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago, calls "a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter" [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both dark energy, through its dependence on the volume of spacetime, and dark matter, since the mass of dark gravitons depends on the dimension's radius.

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya and David Wu, demonstrated that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data [[10]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703090B/abstract). Their model predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will both decrease over time. It also predicts that the rate at which dark energy changes is proportional to its own energy density. Because astrophysical measurements show that dark energy's density is extraordinarily small, "it won’t change fast," Vafa said. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A direct consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity. Obied noted that "there could be astrophysical ways of testing this" [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). One key test involves "kick velocities" from decaying dark gravitons. The resulting energy could help particles escape forming galaxies, making them slightly less massive than standard models predict. Recent survey data is consistent with this effect [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

Coincidentally, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden had already looked into such a test back in 2006. They theorized that if dark matter has a stronger gravitational attraction to other dark matter than to ordinary matter, a special kind of "tidal tail"—an extended stream of stars and gas—would form behind a smaller galaxy as it is pulled apart by a larger one. They looked for this effect and did not find it, which allowed them to set an upper limit on the strength of this extra force. The value predicted by Vafa's team fell well within this observational bound [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski said. The theory is also testable in labs. If gravity leaks into a micron-scale dimension, its force should deviate from predictions at that specific distance. High-precision tabletop experiments are currently searching for such a deviation [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

The fact that a prediction from string theory roughly agrees with astrophysical evidence does not confirm these "stringy" models. But any correspondence between theory and experiment is gratifying to Vafa, who has spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the purely conceptual realm of string theory [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The proposal has its skeptics, who argue the underlying conjectures are ambitious and lack strong evidence. As Oxford physicist Joseph Conlon commented, "There are many ideas that would be important if true, but are probably not. This is one of them" [[9]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

Understanding the dark sector is a daunting problem, and as Obied suggests, it is beneficial to approach it from different angles—observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory. "This is how people should do science," he said. "It’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
