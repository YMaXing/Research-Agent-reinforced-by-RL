# The Dark Universe Might Be A Single, Intertwined Entity

Our current models tell us the universe is mostly invisible. Approximately 70% of it is dark energy, a mysterious force pushing everything apart, and another 25% is dark matter, an unknown material holding galaxies together [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). That leaves just 5% for the ordinary matter we can see and touch. The "dark" label is really just a placeholder for "invisible," since these components do not interact with light and have so far been impossible to observe directly.

The standard model of cosmology, known as Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), treats these two as separate and unrelated. It models dark energy as a cosmological constant. This is a fixed, unchanging property of space itself. The idea has a long history. Einstein first proposed it to make his equations fit a static universe, then called it his "biggest blunder" when he learned the universe was expanding. Scientists later revived the concept to explain the observed cosmic acceleration [[3]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time).

However, new data is starting to challenge this picture of two separate dark components. Recent results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) in 2024 and 2025 suggest the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The data indicates it peaked about two billion years ago and has been weakening since. Even more strangely, the results imply that in an earlier era, dark energy might have grown stronger [[4]](https://indico.global/event/15767/contributions/138193/attachments/65687/127027/2025.12%20DPF%20Dark%20Energy%20and%20Cosmology%20v1%20(B%20Field).pdf), [[5]](https://www.ohio.edu/news/2025/03/universe-might-be-changing-new-desi-data-shows-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time), [[6]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve).

This apparent growth is called the "phantom regime." It is like watching a ball roll uphill on its own, seemingly creating energy from nothing. This would violate the laws of energy conservation unless some external force is acting on it [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This has led a growing number of theorists to consider that the "external force" is a physical link to dark matter. As an engineer, when a system behaves in a way that seems to break the rules, the first thing you check is whether the components are truly isolated. It seems cosmologists are starting to do the same.

As particle physicist Tim Tait puts it, while we have assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

We will next examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea of an interaction between dark energy and dark matter is not new. Back in 2005, a model from Justin Khoury and his collaborators explored if dark energy's density could increase by drawing energy from dark matter. This would create the appearance of phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[7]](https://cerncourier.com/four-reasons-dark-energy-should-evolve-with-time), [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). As Khoury stated, "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The mechanism is simple: as the mass of dark matter particles changes due to the interaction, its energy density no longer scales as a simple function of the universe's expansion. An observer who assumes it does will miscalculate, attributing the difference to dark energy [[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0510628).

Two decades later, the DESI results prompted Khoury, along with Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden, to build a new model. They constructed a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory governing the strong nuclear force. Just as QCD describes the interactions between quarks and gluons, their framework proposes a similar set of dynamics operating entirely within the dark sector. This provides a concrete theoretical basis for a system where the density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles change in concert [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A different model, published in January 2025, suggests a similar mechanism where dark matter transfers a small part of its energy to dark energy. This process would reduce the braking effect that dark matter has on the universe's expansion. Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier, explained that "dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion, so easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion of the universe to accelerate" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). These "interacting dark sector models" allow dark energy's influence to change over time by transferring energy between the two components [[9]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2524499122).

Physicist David Andriot argues that the phantom regime is simply a bookkeeping error. He suggests that "any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). If dark matter's mass changes over time but our models assume it is constant, the energy difference gets incorrectly assigned to the dark energy column in our cosmic ledger. This makes dark energy appear to behave in physically impossible ways. From an engineering perspective, this is a classic case of a faulty abstraction; we have treated two coupled components as independent, and the model is breaking at the seams.

Harvard physicist Cumrun Vafa reinforces this point, stating that "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong." He argues that assuming the two are separate is a fundamental flaw. If they are linked, they must be modeled as a coupled system. Treating them independently, as the DESI team and other cosmologists have done, "led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These coupled-sector models also offer a potential solution to the Hubble tension. This tension is the ~9% disagreement in the measured expansion rate of the universe. Measurements from the early universe (like the cosmic microwave background) predict a slower expansion rate today than what is measured from the late universe (like supernovae). As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, this discrepancy "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics." An interaction between dark matter and dark energy could naturally adjust the expansion history, reconciling the two measurements without needing to assume errors or invent new, unrelated physics [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological interaction models receive a natural ultraviolet completion and common geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

String theory provides a framework that might unify dark energy and dark matter. Foundational work by Cumrun Vafa in 2019 and 2022 showed how string theory naturally allows for varying dark energy and, by extension, time-dependent dark matter particle masses [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This led to the "dark dimension" proposal, which suggests a shared geometric origin for both dark components. The idea attempts to solve the cosmological constant problem, where quantum theory predicts a vacuum energy 10¹²⁰ times larger than observed, by postulating a micron-sized extra dimension that aligns the resulting energy density with observations [[10]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

String theory posits the existence of extra spatial dimensions beyond the three we experience. These are usually assumed to be incredibly small, near the Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). The dark dimension proposal, however, suggests one of these extra dimensions is much larger, around a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) in size [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[10]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy). This idea is rooted in the Swampland program, which aims to identify criteria for consistent theories of quantum gravity. One of its principles, the Distance Conjecture, suggests that in certain limits—like when the cosmological constant is extremely small—a "tower" of light particles should emerge. This tower could be the Kaluza-Klein (KK) modes of an extra dimension, with masses tied to its size [[1]](https://indico.global/event/551/contributions/130592/attachments/61097/117637/Dark%20Sector%20and%20the%20Swampland.pdf).

The mechanism is elegant. Gravitons, the theoretical particles that carry the force of gravity, could leak into this larger spatial dimension. By doing so, they would acquire a small mass, becoming "dark gravitons." This process would create a whole "tower" of these particles with a range of masses tied to the cosmological constant [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). The model is theoretically robust, as the small mass of these particles is considered "technically natural" and stable against quantum corrections [[12]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5256007). While these new particles would exist in the extra dimension, their gravitational pull would still affect our own, allowing them to account for the effects we attribute to dark matter [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This setup creates an inherent link between the two dark components. As physicist Georges Obied explains, "there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the extra dimension would simultaneously alter both the dark energy density and the mass of the dark matter particles. This is because the scalar field controlling the dimension's radius affects both the potential energy (dark energy) and the KK graviton masses (dark matter) [[1]](https://indico.global/event/551/contributions/130592/attachments/61097/117637/Dark%20Sector%20and%20the%20Swampland.pdf).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied, Vafa, Alek Bedroya, and David Wu showed that this scenario is consistent with the DESI data. Their model's predictions were validated against observational data, including DESI's Lyman-alpha forest data, which helps constrain parameters of the early universe [[13]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...01..124A/abstract), [[14]](https://cosmolya.ifae.es/post/2026-lyalpha-paper). The model predicts that both dark energy's strength and dark matter's mass will decrease over time, with the rate of change being proportional to dark energy's density [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Because this density is so small, Vafa noted that "it won’t change fast." He added, "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A testable consequence of this coupling is a new, long-range force between dark matter particles [[15]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf). In 2006, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden realized they could test for such a force by observing the tidal tails of galaxies. An extra attractive force would alter how these streams of stars and gas are pulled from smaller galaxies orbiting larger ones. They looked for this effect and, not finding it, set an upper limit on its strength [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The value predicted by Vafa's team is well within this limit. "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski said [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The fact that a prediction from string theory aligns with astrophysical evidence does not validate the model. Still, any connection between theory and experiment is a welcome development for researchers like Vafa, who have worked for decades to produce testable predictions from a highly conceptual field [[1]](https://indico.global/event/551/contributions/130592/attachments/61097/117637/Dark%20Sector%20and%20the%20Swampland.pdf). The puzzle of the dark universe is a massive one, and solving it requires attacking the problem from multiple angles. Observational cosmology provides the data, particle physics offers models for interactions, and string theory attempts to build a fundamental, unified picture. As Obied noted, "it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible... And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This is a process familiar to any engineer. When a system produces unexpected outputs, you must question your underlying assumptions. The story of dark energy and dark matter is a powerful example of this principle at a cosmic scale. The initial, simple model of two separate components is being challenged by new data, forcing us to consider more complex, integrated systems. It is a lesson in systems thinking, reminding us that sometimes the most profound answers are found not in the components themselves, but in the connections between them.
