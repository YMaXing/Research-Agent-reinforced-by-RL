# The Dark Universe Might Be A Single, Intertwined Entity

According to our current understanding, the universe is a dark place. Approximately 70% of its composition is dark energy, an unknown force driving cosmic expansion, while another 25% is dark matter, a mysterious substance holding galaxies together. This leaves only 5% for the ordinary, observable matter that makes up everything we can see. Semantically, "dark" is a misnomer; "invisible" is more accurate, as these components neither emit nor absorb light, and have so far evaded direct observation.

The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), treats dark energy as a cosmological constant (Λ) and assumes it is entirely unrelated to cold dark matter (CDM). They are considered independent phenomena. The concept of a cosmological constant has a long history. Einstein first introduced it to his theory of general relativity to allow for a static universe, but dismissed it as his "biggest blunder" when the universe was found to be expanding. The idea was later revived to represent the constant dark energy driving the observed cosmic acceleration [[18]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time). However, emerging observational evidence is beginning to challenge this long-held assumption, suggesting the two might be physically intertwined.

Recent results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) in 2024 and 2025 indicate that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The data suggests it peaked roughly two billion years ago and has been weakening ever since. More surprisingly, these findings also imply that in an earlier cosmic era, dark energy could have grown stronger [[3]](https://indico.global/event/15767/contributions/138193/attachments/65687/127027/2025.12%20DPF%20Dark%20Energy%20and%20Cosmology%20v1%20(B%20Field).pdf), [[5]](https://www.ohio.edu/news/2025/03/universe-might-be-changing-new-desi-data-shows-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time), [[11]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve). This behavior, where dark energy appears to grow stronger, is known as entering the "phantom regime." It is like watching a ball spontaneously roll uphill, seemingly creating energy from nothing and violating conservation laws unless some external influence is at play [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This has spurred a growing number of theorists to investigate the possibility that this influence is a physical link to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait notes, while scientists have generally assumed dark energy and dark matter "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[17]](https://indico.cern.ch/event/473000/contributions/1993414/attachments/1209863/1764345/tait-Aspen.pdf).

We will next examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the long-standing Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. As early as 2005, a model proposed by Justin Khoury and collaborators explored whether dark energy's density could increase over time by drawing energy from dark matter [[6]](https://cerncourier.com/four-reasons-dark-energy-should-evolve-with-time), [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This would produce what looks like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation. Khoury explained, "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury and his colleagues Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden to construct a model based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD). Their model draws an analogy to QCD, the theory of the strong nuclear force. Just as QCD describes interactions between quarks and gluons, their framework posits a similar set of dynamics operating entirely within the dark sector, providing a concrete field-theoretic basis for the coordinated evolution of dark energy density and dark matter particle mass [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Another recent model, published in January 2025, proposes a similar mechanism. In this scenario, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy in a previous cosmic era. "Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," explained Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the study's authors, so "easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion of the universe to accelerate" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). These "interacting dark sector models" suggest the two phenomena can transfer energy, allowing dark energy's influence to shift over time [[13]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2524499122).

The appearance of a phantom regime, according to physicist David Andriot, is essentially a result of bookkeeping. "Any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy," he explained [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). If dark matter's mass is not constant, but cosmologists assume it is, then the "missing" or "extra" energy is incorrectly attributed to dark energy, making it appear to behave in physically impossible ways.

Harvard physicist Cumrun Vafa agrees, stating that "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong." Vafa’s critique is that this flawed assumption, made by both cosmologists and the DESI team, is fundamentally invalid because it assumes separability where none may exist. If the two quantities are linked at a deeper level, they must be solved for simultaneously. Attempting to isolate one "led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior," which is merely an artifact of a flawed assumption [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Beyond explaining the DESI results, coupled dark-sector models may also resolve one of cosmology's most persistent problems: the Hubble tension. This refers to the approximately 9% discrepancy between measurements of the universe's current expansion rate—the Hubble constant—derived from two different methods. Observations of the early universe, specifically the cosmic microwave background (CMB), predict a lower value for the expansion rate today than what is measured directly from observations of the late universe, such as supernovae in nearby galaxies. As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, this tension "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics." Their model suggests that the interaction provides a physical mechanism to adjust the expansion history, bringing the two measurements into alignment without needing to invoke systematic errors or new, unrelated physics [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological interaction models receive a natural geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

String theory offers a potential solution to one of cosmology’s greatest challenges: the cosmological constant problem. Calculations based on quantum theory predict a vacuum energy density about 10¹²⁰ times larger than what is observed. The dark dimension proposal attempts to resolve this discrepancy; by postulating a micron-sized extra dimension, the resulting energy density aligns with observations [[19]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy). The proposal, put forth by Cumrun Vafa and collaborators, was inspired by Vafa's earlier work on the "distance conjecture" in string theory. This conjecture posits that when a physical parameter, like the value of dark energy, takes on an extreme value—and the observed value is almost zero—other particles in the theory must become exceptionally light [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). This led them to propose that both dark energy and dark matter are linked to a "dark dimension" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

String theory suggests the existence of extra spatial dimensions beyond our familiar three. While these are typically thought to be infinitesimally small (near the Planck scale of 10⁻³⁵ meters), this proposal posits that one "dark dimension" could be significantly larger, on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

The mechanism works as follows: gravitons, the theoretical particles that mediate gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire a small mass, becoming "dark gravitons." This process doesn't just create one type of massive graviton, but a whole "tower" of them, with a range of masses tied to the cosmological constant [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). Theoretically, the model is robust, as the graviton's small mass is considered "technically natural" and stable against quantum corrections [[21]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5256007). While residing in the dark dimension, their gravitational effects would still be felt in our dimensions, allowing them to fulfill the role typically ascribed to dark matter [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In this scenario, "there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter," said Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both dark energy (through its effect on the vacuum energy) and dark matter (by altering the mass of the dark gravitons).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya and David Wu, demonstrated that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data. These checks include comparisons with DESI's Lyman-alpha forest data to help constrain parameters of the primordial universe [[22]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025JCAP...01..124A/abstract), [[23]](https://cosmolya.ifae.es/post/2026-lyalpha-paper). Their model predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will both decrease over time, with the rate of change in dark energy being proportional to its energy density [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Because the observed energy density of dark energy is incredibly small, "it won’t change fast," Vafa noted. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A direct and testable consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity [[12]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf). An extra attractive force would cause dark matter to have a stronger gravitational pull on other dark matter than on ordinary matter. In 2006, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden realized this could be tested by observing the tidal tails of galaxies—the extended streams of stars and gas pulled from a smaller galaxy as it orbits a larger one. They reasoned that if such a force existed, it would affect how these tails form. They looked for this effect and, not finding it, were able to set an upper bound on the strength of this extra force [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The value predicted by Vafa's team falls well within this observational limit.

Furthermore, the model predicts that heavier dark gravitons can decay into lighter ones. This decay would impart a small "kick velocity" to the resulting particles, which could affect how galaxies form by allowing some dark matter to escape the gravitational pull of nascent galaxies. Recent analyses of the Kilo-Degree Survey are consistent with the predicted kick velocity, though more stringent tests are expected from the Euclid space telescope [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). The model is also being tested in laboratories. If gravity leaks into a micron-scale dimension, its force law should deviate from expectations at that specific distance. High-precision tabletop experiments are currently underway to look for such deviations [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). Dark dimension couplings might also influence gravitational wave signals from events like black hole mergers, a potential signature for future observatories [[24]](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12948v1), [[25]](https://www.mdpi.com/2218-1997/12/2/48). "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski remarked [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The fact that a prediction from string theory aligns with astrophysical evidence does not prove the model's validity. However, any correspondence between theory and experiment is gratifying to researchers like Vafa, who have spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the purely conceptual realm of string theory [[12]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf).

Understanding the dark sector is a daunting problem, and attacking it from multiple complementary directions—observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory—is essential. As Obied, one of Vafa's former students, puts it: "This is how people should do science. I mean, it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
