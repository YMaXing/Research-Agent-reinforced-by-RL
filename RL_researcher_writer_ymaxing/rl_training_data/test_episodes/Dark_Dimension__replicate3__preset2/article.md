    # The Phantom Universe: Is Dark Energy Coupled to Dark Matter?

The universe consists of approximately 70% dark energy, an unknown force driving cosmic expansion, and another 25% is dark matter, the mysterious material holding galaxies together. The ordinary matter you can see and interact with makes up a mere 5% of the cosmos. It is important to remember that these components are not literally "dark" but semantically invisible; they do not emit, reflect, or absorb light, and have so far proven impossible to observe directly.

The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), treats dark energy as a cosmological constant (Λ) and assumes it is entirely unrelated to cold dark matter (CDM). They are considered two separate, independent phenomena. The idea of a cosmological constant itself has evolved. Einstein first added the term to his equations to allow for a static universe, which he later dismissed when astronomers discovered the universe was expanding. It was revived decades later to represent the constant dark energy driving the accelerated expansion [[12]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time). However, recent astronomical observations have begun to challenge this assumption, suggesting the two might be physically intertwined.

Data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) in 2024 and 2025 indicates that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The measurements suggest it reached a peak value roughly two billion years ago and has been weakening ever since. More surprisingly, the data implies that in an earlier cosmic era, dark energy could have grown stronger [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[2]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve). This behavior, where dark energy appears to grow stronger and its equation of state parameter `w` drops below -1, is known as the "phantom regime." It is like watching a ball spontaneously roll uphill, seemingly creating energy from nothing and violating the laws of conservation [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This apparent violation has spurred a growing number of theorists to investigate an old idea: what if dark energy is not acting alone? What if it is coupled to dark matter? As Tim Tait, a particle physicist at the University of California, Irvine, noted, “you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

We will next examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter might interact is not new. In 2005, a model by physicist Justin Khoury and his collaborators posed a hypothetical question: could there be a form of dark energy whose energy density increased over time? They found that if dark energy could receive energy from dark matter, it could produce what looked like, but was not, true phantom behavior. “It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this,” Khoury said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0510628).

### A Unified Theory for the Dark Sector

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury and his colleagues Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden to construct a modern model of these dark interactions. Their new framework is based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a cornerstone theory of particle physics. In this model, both the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles change in concert, providing a field-theoretic mechanism for the energy exchange [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Another recent study, published in January 2025, proposes a similar scenario. According to this model, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy during a previous era of cosmic history. Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the study's authors, explained the effect: “Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion,” so easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion to accelerate [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[6]](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf).

### The Phantom Menace: An Accounting Error?

The appearance of phantom behavior, then, is essentially a result of bookkeeping. David Andriot, a physicist at CNRS, the French National Center for Scientific Research, argues that “any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10410). In other words, when observers fit cosmological data, they assume dark matter's density scales predictably with the expansion of the universe. If dark matter's mass is actually changing over time due to an interaction, that unaccounted-for energy change gets misattributed to the dark energy budget, making it look like dark energy is behaving in a physically impossible way.

This bookkeeping artifact can be understood more formally. Cosmological observations identify "matter" as any component whose energy density, `ρ_m`, scales as `a⁻³`, where `a` is the universe's scale factor. In an interacting model, the true dark matter density might evolve as `ρ_DM = A_m(φ) * ρ̄_DM`, where `ρ̄_DM` is the component that scales as `a⁻³` and `A_m(φ)` is a coupling function dependent on a scalar field `φ`. Observers, assuming a non-interacting dark sector, effectively lump the extra variation from `A_m(φ)` into the dark energy budget. This creates an *effective* dark energy density, `ρ_DE_eff = ρ_φ + (A_m - 1)ρ̄_DM`, where `ρ_φ` is the true energy density of the scalar field. This leads to an effective equation of state, `w_DE_eff`, which can dip below -1 even if the true quintessence field has `w_φ ≥ -1`, thus creating the phantom effect without violating any physical laws [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10410).

Cumrun Vafa, a physicist at Harvard University, agrees with this assessment. “The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong,” he said. “That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

### Easing the Hubble Tension

Beyond explaining the phantom regime, these interaction models may also help resolve one of the most persistent problems in cosmology: the Hubble tension. This tension refers to the discrepancy between measurements of the universe's current expansion rate, the Hubble constant (H₀). Measurements based on the early universe, like the cosmic microwave background (CMB), predict a value of about 67.4 km/s/Mpc. In contrast, measurements based on more recent phenomena in the local universe, like Type Ia supernovae, yield a different, higher value around 73.0 km/s/Mpc, a variance of approximately 9% [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

According to Teixeira and her co-authors, this discrepancy “has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[6]](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf). In a universe where dark energy and dark matter interact, this tension is not a crisis but an expected outcome. The interaction alters the expansion history of the universe over cosmic time. This means the expansion rate measured from the early universe would naturally differ from the rate measured in the more recent universe, reconciling the two measurements without needing to invoke systematic errors or entirely new, unrelated physics.

These phenomenological models provide compelling explanations, but they find a more fundamental home within string theory, which offers a common geometric origin for both dark components.

## A Dark Dimension

Ongoing work in string theory suggests a natural way to connect dark energy and dark matter. Building on the idea that dark energy can vary over time, Vafa and collaborators proposed in 2019 and 2022 that the mass of dark matter particles might also be time-dependent. This led them to a framework where both dark components share a common origin in a "dark dimension" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[9]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293).

### String Theory and the Micron-Sized Dimension

Vafa's insight stemmed from his work on the "distance conjecture" in string theory. This conjecture posits that when a physical parameter like the cosmological constant takes on an extreme value—in this case, extraordinarily small—it forces other parameters, such as particle masses, to change predictably. This leads to the emergence of a "tower" of light particles [[13]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

String theory posits the existence of six or seven extra spatial dimensions beyond our familiar three. These are typically thought to be curled up at the smallest possible scale, close to the Planck length (10⁻³⁵ meters). The dark dimension proposal, however, suggests that one of these extra dimensions could be significantly larger. It could be on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This specific size is proposed to address the "cosmological constant problem." This is the massive discrepancy between the vacuum energy density predicted by quantum theory and the tiny value we observe. A micron-scale dimension may naturally produce a dark energy value consistent with observations [[14]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

### Dark Gravitons as Dark Matter

The mechanism works as follows: gravitons, the theoretical particles that mediate gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire a small mass determined by the dimension's radius. These massive "dark gravitons" would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would still be felt in our dimensions. Their collective influence would precisely mimic the effects we attribute to dark matter [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This framework also offers an explanation for why gravity is so much weaker than other fundamental forces. With a large extra dimension for gravitons to permeate, the strength of gravity gets diluted, making it appear feeble in our four-dimensional world [[13]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

This scenario provides what Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago, calls “a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both components. A change in the radius would modulate the dark matter mass (since it is set by the radius) and the dark energy density (which depends on the volume of the extra dimensions). The two are intrinsically linked and cannot be varied independently.

### A Natural Coupling and Testable Predictions

The Obied-Vafa-Bedroya-Wu model, also known as the Fading Dark Sector (FDS) model, provides a concrete physical realization of this idea. It parameterizes the dark energy potential as `V = V₀ exp(-cφ)` and the dark matter mass as `m = m₀ exp(-c'φ)`, where `φ` is a scalar field controlling the radius of the dark dimension, and `c` and `c'` are O(1) constants expected from Swampland principles [[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03090). This model offers a point-by-point consistency with the DESI 2024/2025 data.

First, the FDS model provides a physical explanation for the apparent phantom behavior. As the scalar field `φ` evolves, the dark matter mass changes, leading to the bookkeeping artifact that makes dark energy appear phantom-like. Second, the model shows excellent agreement with the combined DESI and supernova data, achieving a statistical significance comparable to the purely phenomenological CPL parametrization used by the DESI collaboration [[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03090). Third, the model's parameters are constrained by different datasets in a physically intuitive way: DESI data, which primarily probes the matter-dominated era, constrains `c'` (the rate of dark matter mass change), while supernova data, which probes the recent dark-energy-dominated era, constrains `c` (the slope of the dark energy potential).

The model predicts a slow, density-proportional change in dark energy. Because dark energy's density is extraordinarily small, “it won’t change fast,” Vafa explained. “It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This slow evolution aligns with the DESI observations, including the peak around two billion years ago and the subsequent weakening.

A key consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity. This force, mediated by the massive gravitons, would alter the dynamics of galaxies in a way that could be tested astrophysically. If dark matter particles interact with each other through this new force, it would affect how galaxies cluster and how they are held together. The strength of this "fifth force" is proportional to `c'²`. The best-fit value from the FDS model is `c' ≈ 0.05 ± 0.01`, implying a very weak fifth force [[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03090).

### Closing the Loop with Observation

Coincidentally, a test for such a force was already performed in 2006 by physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden. They reasoned that if dark matter has a stronger attraction to itself than to ordinary matter, it would affect the tidal streams of stars pulled from dwarf galaxies as they orbit a larger one. They looked for this effect in the tidal tails of the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy and found no evidence, allowing them to set an upper bound on the strength of any such extra force [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[10]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0606566).

Remarkably, the strength of the force predicted by Vafa's team from their string-theory-derived model fell well within the observational limit set by Kamionkowski and Kesden. The predicted value was about 20 times smaller than the upper bound, making it consistent with current observations. “It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work,” Kamionkowski said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The fact that the FDS model's best-fit value for `c'` is consistent with this independent astrophysical constraint (`c' ≲ 0.2`) provides another layer of validation for the framework [[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03090).

The fact that a prediction from string theory aligns with astrophysical evidence does not prove the model is correct. However, any correspondence between the purely conceptual realm of string theory and the world of observation is gratifying to researchers like Vafa, who have spent decades trying to generate testable predictions. The emerging evidence from DESI suggests that the simple picture of two independent dark components may be wrong. Coupled-sector models, particularly those grounded in string theory's dark dimension, offer a compelling alternative. They provide a unified framework that not only explains the DESI data but also eases the Hubble tension. Understanding the nature of dark energy and dark matter is one of the most complex problems in modern physics. As theorists explore possibilities, the answer will likely come from a convergence of different fields. As Obied puts it, “it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide” [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This interdisciplinary approach, combining observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory, is our best hope for finally shedding light on the dark universe.
