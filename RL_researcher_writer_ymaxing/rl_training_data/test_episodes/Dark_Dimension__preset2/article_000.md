# The Dark Universe Might Be Interacting With Itself

According to our best estimates, about 70% of the cosmos is dark energy, an unknown force driving space to expand, and another 25% is dark matter, a mysterious material holding galaxies together. The remaining 5% is the ordinary, observable matter that makes up stars, planets, and us. Semantically, "dark" energy and matter are not literally dark but invisible; they do not emit, reflect, or absorb light, and have so far proven impossible to observe directly [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM, assumes that dark energy (as a cosmological constant, Λ) and dark matter are separate, independent components. The concept of dark energy has evolved over the past century. It began with Albert Einstein adding a "cosmological constant" to his theory of general relativity to ensure a static universe. He later dismissed it when astronomers discovered the universe was expanding. Decades later, the idea was reclaimed to represent a constant energy driving the universe's accelerated expansion, becoming a cornerstone of the standard model for the past 20 years [[29]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time).

However, emerging observational evidence suggests they may be physically intertwined. Results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) in 2024 and 2025 indicate that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The data hints that it peaked roughly two billion years ago, has weakened since, and may have grown stronger in an even earlier era [[5]](https://www.ohio.edu/news/2025/03/universe-might-be-changing-new-desi-data-shows-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time), [[19]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2524499122).

This behavior is puzzling. Researchers describe the period of strengthening dark energy as entering the "phantom regime" [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). The situation is like a ball spontaneously rolling uphill, seemingly creating energy from nothing and violating conservation laws unless some external influence is at play. A growing number of theorists are now looking into the possibility that this influence is a physical link to dark matter [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Even though scientists have long assumed that dark energy and dark matter "don’t have anything to do with each other," said Tim Tait, a particle physicist at the University of California, Irvine, "you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

We will now examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the long-standing Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. In 2005, a model by Justin Khoury and collaborators posed a hypothetical question: could there be a form of dark energy whose energy density increased over time? They found that if dark energy and dark matter could transfer energy between one another, they could produce what looked like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[6]](https://cerncourier.com/four-reasons-dark-energy-should-evolve-with-time), [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury said [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury, Meng-Xiang Lin, and Mark Trodden to construct a model based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a cornerstone theory of particle physics. In this model, both the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles change in concert [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[18]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf). Another model, published in January 2025, suggests something similar: dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy in the past [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the study's authors, explained that "dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," so easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion to accelerate [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The appearance of a phantom regime is essentially a bookkeeping problem. David Andriot, a physicist at CNRS, argued that "any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy" [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). If dark matter's mass is not constant, but our models assume it is, then the "missing" energy is incorrectly attributed to dark energy, making it appear to violate conservation laws.

Cumrun Vafa, a physicist at Harvard University, agreed, stating that "the notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong." He argued that this flawed assumption, also followed by the DESI team, "led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This perspective also helps address the Hubble tension, one of the most persistent problems in cosmology. The issue is a ~9% discrepancy between measurements of the universe's current expansion rate from the early universe (via the cosmic microwave background) and the more recent universe (via supernovae). Teixeira and her co-authors wrote that this tension "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics." Their model suggests that in a universe where dark energy and dark matter interact, this discrepancy is not a crisis but an expected outcome [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological models find a natural home and a common geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter do interact, it could mean they share a common origin [[17]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Ongoing work in string theory—the idea that the universe is made of vibrating strings—suggests how. Building on the concept that dark energy and dark matter mass may vary over time, Vafa and collaborators proposed in 2019 and 2022 that both could be linked to a "dark dimension" [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The motivation for this comes from the "cosmological constant problem," one of the biggest challenges in theoretical physics. When physicists calculate the expected energy density of the vacuum based on quantum theory, the result is 10¹²⁰ times larger than the value observed. The dark dimension model was inspired by Vafa's "distance conjecture" in string theory, which suggests that when a physical parameter like the cosmological constant takes on an extremely small value, other phenomena—like a large extra dimension and a "tower" of light particles—must emerge [[30]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy), [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

String theory posits the existence of extra spatial dimensions. While these are typically thought to be incredibly small (near the Planck scale of 10⁻³⁵ meters), researchers proposed that one "dark dimension" could be significantly larger—on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[9]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

The mechanism works like this: gravitons, the theoretical particles that mediate gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire a small mass and become part of a "tower" of dark gravitons with a wide range of masses. These massive gravitons would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would be felt in our familiar dimensions, fulfilling the role normally ascribed to dark matter [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

This framework also offers an explanation for why gravity is so much weaker than the other fundamental forces. In this scenario, gravity's strength is diluted because it permeates all dimensions, including the relatively large dark one. This leakage into a roomier dimension would make gravity appear feeble in our four-dimensional world [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

In this scenario, "there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter," said Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both dark energy (as vacuum energy depends on the volume) and dark matter (as the dark graviton's mass depends on the dimension's radius). The two are intrinsically linked [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya and David Wu, found this scenario was consistent with DESI data, including higher-order checks against its Lyman-alpha forest measurements combined with cosmic microwave background observations [[4]](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2025arXiv250703090B/abstract), [[32]](https://cosmolya.ifae.es/post/2026-lyalpha-paper). Their model predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will decrease over time, with the rate of change for dark energy proportional to its density. Because dark energy's density is so small, "it won’t change fast," Vafa said. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[20]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The model also makes other specific, testable predictions. When heavier dark gravitons decay into lighter ones, the resulting particles receive a small "kick velocity." This kick can affect how galaxies form, as gravitons with enough velocity could escape the gravitational pull of a nascent galaxy, resulting in a slightly less massive structure than the standard model predicts. Recent analyses of the Kilo-Degree Survey are consistent with the predicted kick velocity, and more stringent tests are expected from the Euclid space telescope [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

A direct consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity [[22]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Coincidentally, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden had already looked for such an effect in 2006. They reasoned that if dark matter has a stronger self-attraction, it would alter the "tidal tails"—streams of stars and gas pulled from a smaller galaxy as it orbits a larger one. They found no such effect, which allowed them to set an upper limit on the strength of this extra force. The value predicted by the dark dimension model falls comfortably within this observational bound [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski said [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Other potential tests involve looking for subtle effects on gravitational waves from cosmic events like black hole mergers, which could be detected by future observatories, or laboratory experiments searching for deviations from expected gravitational forces at the micron scale [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201), [[33]](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12948v1).

The fact that a prediction from string theory roughly agrees with astrophysical evidence does not confirm the model. Still, any correspondence is gratifying to Vafa, who has spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the theory [[12]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

However, the proposal has its skeptics. Some physicists argue that the conjectures it is based on are ambitious and that the evidence for them remains weak. While they find the search for extra dimensions interesting, they believe the probability of finding them is low [[31]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

Ultimately, understanding dark energy and dark matter requires attacking the problem from multiple, complementary directions. This includes observational cosmology like DESI, particle physics analogues, and string theory frameworks. As Obied noted, "it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
