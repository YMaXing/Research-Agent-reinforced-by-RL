# A Dark Dimension: The Case for a Unified Dark Sector

According to our current understanding, the universe is a profoundly dark place. Approximately 70% of its cosmic budget consists of dark energy, the mysterious force driving space to expand, while another 25% is dark matter, the invisible scaffolding that holds galaxies together. The ordinary matter we can see and touch accounts for a mere 5%. Semantically, these dominant components are not literally "dark" but invisible; they neither emit nor absorb light, and have so far proven impossible to observe directly.

The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), treats dark energy as a strict cosmological constant and assumes it is entirely unrelated to dark matter. However, recent astronomical observations are beginning to challenge this view, suggesting the two might be physically intertwined.

Data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) in 2024 and 2025 indicates that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The measurements suggest it reached a peak roughly two billion years ago and has been weakening ever since. More surprisingly, the data also implies that in an earlier era, dark energy could have grown stronger. This behavior is described as entering the "phantom regime," a scenario likened to a ball spontaneously rolling uphill. It appears to violate the law of energy conservation by creating energy from nothing, unless some external influence is at play.

This has led a growing number of theorists to investigate the possibility that dark energy's behavior is influenced by its ties to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait notes, while scientists have often assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe."

This article will explore the theoretical models that resolve this apparent violation. We will examine how dark-interaction models can turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the long-standing Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. In 2005, a model proposed by physicist Justin Khoury and his collaborators posed a hypothetical question: could there be a form of dark energy whose energy density increased over time? They found that if dark energy and dark matter could affect one another, they could produce what looked like, but was not, true phantom behavior. "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury explained [[17]](#17). This early work laid the foundation for treating the phantom regime not as a violation of physics, but as a signature of a hidden interaction.

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury and his colleagues, Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden, to construct a new model based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a cornerstone of particle physics. In this framework, both the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles change in concert, providing a concrete field-theoretic mechanism for their co-evolution [[17]](#17).

Another recent study, published in January 2025, imagines a similar scenario. According to this model, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy during a previous era of cosmic history. "Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," said Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the authors, so easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion to accelerate [[20]](#20).

These interaction models reframe the phantom regime as a "bookkeeping" problem. Physicist David Andriot argues that the apparent phantom behavior is an artifact that arises when we misinterpret the data. "Any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy," he said [[20]](#20). In other words, if dark matter particles are losing mass over time, that "lost" energy has to go somewhere. An observer who assumes dark matter's mass is constant will incorrectly attribute this energy transfer to the dark energy budget, making it seem as though dark energy is growing stronger on its own.

Harvard physicist Cumrun Vafa agrees with this assessment. "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong," he stated. "That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[20]](#20). The two components are linked at a deeper level and must be solved for simultaneously.

This perspective also offers a potential solution to another major puzzle in cosmology: the Hubble tension. This tension refers to the approximately 9% discrepancy between the universe's expansion rate as measured in the early universe (from the cosmic microwave background) and in the more recent universe (from supernovae). According to Teixeira and her co-authors, this discrepancy "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics." Their model suggests that in a world where dark energy and dark matter interact, this difference in expansion rates is not a crisis but something to be expected [[17]](#17). The interaction modifies the expansion history of the universe over cosmic time, naturally reconciling the measurements from different epochs.

These phenomenological models provide a compelling framework for understanding the DESI results. They receive a natural origin within string theory, which we will explore next through the "dark dimension" proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter do interact, it could mean they share a common origin [[17]](#17). Ongoing work in string theory, pioneered by Vafa and his collaborators in 2019 and 2022, suggests a way these concepts might be connected. String theory naturally allows for a varying dark energy and, by extension, time-dependent dark matter particle masses. This led them to propose that both dark components could originate from a so-called "dark dimension."

String theory posits the existence of extra dimensions beyond our familiar three of space and one of time. While these are traditionally thought to be curled up at the incredibly tiny Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters), this new proposal suggests one of these dimensions could be significantly larger—on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[11]](#11). This is still microscopic, but enormous compared to the others.![A slice of a 3D map produced by the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument team shows connected strands of galaxies forming the structure of our universe.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/DESI-Slice-cr.Claire-Lamman_DESI-collaboration-custom-colormap-package-by-cmastro-scaled.webp)
Image 1: A slice of the 3D map of the universe from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), showing the cosmic web of galaxies. (Source [Claire Lamman/DESI collaboration; custom colormap package by cmastro](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622))

The mechanism works as follows: gravitons, the theoretical particles that carry the force of gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire a small mass and become "dark gravitons." While residing in the dark dimension, their gravitational effects would still be felt in our dimensions, allowing them to fulfill the role normally ascribed to dark matter [[11]](#11).

This scenario provides a built-in, natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter. "There is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter," said Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago. Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both the density of dark energy and the mass of the dark gravitons [[20]](#20). The two cannot be varied independently.

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya and David Wu, demonstrated that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data. Their model predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will decrease over time, with the rate of change being proportional to dark energy's density. Because this density is extraordinarily small, "it won’t change fast," Vafa explained. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[12]](#12).![An artist’s illustration showing two hands reaching toward each other, one solid and one with a branching, root-like pattern, set against a backdrop of the expanding universe filled with galaxies.](https://d2r55xnwy6nx47.cloudfront.net/uploads/2024/04/DarkInteractions-cr-Ada-Zejun-Shen-Lede-copy-1-scaled.webp)
Image 2: An artistic representation of the interaction between dark energy and dark matter, which may have a common origin. (Source [Ada Zejun Shen/Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622))

A direct consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity. This is an effect that could be tested through astrophysical observations. In fact, one such test was already conducted. In 2006, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden imagined what would happen if two galaxies passed close to each other. If dark matter has a stronger attraction to other dark matter than to ordinary matter, a distinctive "tidal tail"—a stream of stars, gas, and dust—would form. They looked for this effect and, not finding it, were able to set an upper limit on the strength of any such extra force [[22]](#22).

The value predicted by the dark dimension model falls comfortably within this observational bound. "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski remarked [[22]](#22).

The fact that a prediction from string theory roughly agrees with astrophysical evidence does not confirm the model. However, any correspondence between string theory and experiment is gratifying to Vafa and other researchers who have spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from a purely conceptual realm [[12]](#12).

Understanding dark energy, dark matter, and their potential relationship is a monumental challenge. As Obied said, "it’s very beneficial to come at this in different ways"—from observational cosmology, particle physics, or string theory. This interdisciplinary approach is how science moves forward. "It’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[22]](#22).
