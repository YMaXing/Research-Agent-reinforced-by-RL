# A Dark Dimension: The Case for a Unified Dark Sector

According to our best estimates, about 70% of the universe is made of dark energy, the force driving cosmic expansion, and another 25% is dark matter, the invisible structure holding galaxies together. The ordinary matter you can actually see and touch—everything from stars to us—makes up a tiny 5% of the cosmos. The rest is effectively invisible. It doesn't emit or absorb light, making it impossible to observe directly.

For a long time, the standard model of cosmology, known as Lambda-CDM (ΛCDM), has treated dark energy and dark matter as separate, unrelated components. It’s a simple and effective model, but new evidence is starting to show cracks in this foundation, suggesting the two might be physically connected.

Recent data from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) has been particularly disruptive. The 2024 and 2025 results indicate that dark energy's strength hasn't been constant. It appears to have peaked about two billion years ago and has been weakening since. Even more strangely, the data suggests that in an earlier cosmic era, dark energy might have grown stronger [[1]](#1). This behavior is known as the "phantom regime," and it’s like watching a ball spontaneously roll uphill. It seems to violate the law of energy conservation, unless some hidden interaction is at play.

This has pushed a growing number of theorists to explore the idea that dark energy is coupled to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait puts it, while we’ve assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[2]](#2).

In this article, we will examine the concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea of an interacting dark sector isn't entirely new. Back in 2005, a model from physicist Justin Khoury and his collaborators explored a simple question: could dark energy's density increase over time by drawing energy from dark matter? They found that such an interaction could create the appearance of phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation. The mechanism is straightforward. Due to the interaction, the mass of dark matter particles changes over time. This means the dark matter energy density no longer redshifts simply as a function of the universe's expansion. An observer who assumes dark matter is non-interacting would misinterpret this change, incorrectly attributing the energy shift to the dark energy component. This makes the effective dark energy density appear to grow, creating a w < -1 signature [[3]](#3). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury said, laying the groundwork for a new way to interpret these strange cosmic signals [[2]](#2).

Fast forward two decades, and the DESI findings have brought this idea back to the forefront. In response, Khoury, along with Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden, developed a new model based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD). In this framework, the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter particles evolve together, providing a solid theoretical mechanism for their interaction [[4]](#4).

Another model, published in January 2025, proposes a similar energy transfer. In this scenario, dark matter gives a small fraction of its energy to dark energy over cosmic history. "Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," explained Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier. Easing up on that brake naturally causes the universe's expansion to accelerate [[5]](#5).

These models suggest the phantom regime is essentially a "bookkeeping" problem. Physicist David Andriot argues that we are misinterpreting the data. "Any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy," he explained [[2]](#2). If dark matter particles are losing mass, that energy must go somewhere. An observer assuming a constant dark matter mass would incorrectly add this transferred energy to the dark energy budget, making it appear as if dark energy is growing stronger on its own.

Harvard physicist Cumrun Vafa puts it more bluntly. "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong," he stated. "That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[2]](#2). The two are fundamentally linked and must be treated as a single, coupled system.

This interacting dark sector framework also offers a compelling solution to the Hubble tension. This long-standing problem refers to the ~9% disagreement in the universe's expansion rate when measured using early-universe data, like the cosmic microwave background (CMB), versus late-universe data from supernovae. As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, this tension "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics" [[4]](#4). In a coupled model, this discrepancy is not a sign of error but an expected outcome. The interaction between dark energy and dark matter changes the expansion history of the universe, naturally reconciling the measurements from different cosmic epochs.

These interaction models are powerful, but they are still phenomenological. They get a more fundamental origin within string theory, which proposes a shared geometric source for both dark components. We will explore this next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter interact, it strongly implies they share a common origin [[4]](#4). Work in string theory by Vafa and his collaborators, starting in 2019, provides a compelling candidate for this origin. This work is part of the Swampland program, which aims to identify the properties that any low-energy effective field theory must have to be consistent with a quantum theory of gravity [[6]](#6). One of its core ideas, the Distance Conjecture, posits that at extreme values of physical parameters, a "tower" of light states must emerge. Applying this to the extremely small value of dark energy leads to a surprising prediction [[7]](#7).

String theory requires the existence of extra spatial dimensions beyond the three we experience. These are typically assumed to be curled up at the minuscule Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). The dark dimension proposal, however, suggests that one of these extra dimensions is much larger, on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[8]](#8). This is still incredibly small, but it is many orders of magnitude larger than the others. This single, mesoscopic dimension becomes the shared geometric origin for both dark energy and dark matter.

Here is how the mechanism works. Gravitons, the hypothetical particles that carry the force of gravity, can leak into this larger dark dimension. As they do, they acquire a small mass set by the dimension's radius, becoming "dark gravitons." Although they exist in the dark dimension, their gravitational influence is still felt in our familiar dimensions. This collective gravitational pull perfectly mimics the effects we attribute to dark matter [[8]](#8).

This model has a natural, built-in coupling between dark energy and dark matter. As Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago, explains, "there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter." Any change in the size of the dark dimension simultaneously alters the density of dark energy (which depends on the volume of extra dimensions) and the mass of the dark gravitons. The two are fundamentally linked and cannot be changed independently [[2]](#2).

In a July 2025 paper, Obied, Vafa, Alek Bedroya, and David Wu showed that this scenario aligns remarkably well with the DESI data. Their model, which they call the Fading Dark Sector (FDS) model, predicts that both dark energy and dark matter mass will decrease over time, with the rate of change tied to dark energy's density. Because this density is so small, the change is incredibly slow. "It won’t change fast," Vafa noted. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[2]](#2). The model successfully reproduces the peak in dark energy strength around two billion years ago and its subsequent weakening, providing a physical explanation for the DESI observations [[6]](#6).

One of the most interesting consequences of this coupling is the prediction of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles. This force would be separate from gravity and could be tested through astrophysical observations. In 2006, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden did just that. They theorized that if two galaxies passed near each other, a stronger self-attraction among dark matter particles would create a unique "tidal tail." This is an extended stream of stars, gas, and dust pulled from one of the galaxies. They searched for this effect in observational data and, not finding it, set an upper limit on the strength of any such extra force [[9]](#9).

The value predicted by the dark dimension model falls well within this established limit. The predicted fifth force is about 0.5% of the strength of gravity, while the observational bound allows for a force up to 20 times stronger [[6]](#6), [[9]](#9). "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski commented [[2]](#2).

Of course, the fact that a prediction from string theory aligns with astrophysical data does not prove the model is correct. Still, any connection between the abstract world of string theory and the concrete world of observation is a step forward. It is a rewarding development for Vafa and other researchers who have worked for decades to produce testable predictions from a theory that has often been criticized as purely conceptual [[6]](#6).

## Conclusion

The puzzles of dark energy and dark matter are some of the biggest in science. As Obied suggests, the best way forward is to attack the problem from multiple angles. We need insights from observational cosmology, particle physics, and string theory. "It’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table," he said. "And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[2]](#2). The convergence of these fields on the idea of an interacting dark sector shows how science progresses, turning apparent paradoxes into new opportunities for discovery.
