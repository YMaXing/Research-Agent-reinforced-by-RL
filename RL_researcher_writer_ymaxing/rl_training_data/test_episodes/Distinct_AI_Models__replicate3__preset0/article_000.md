# The Platonic Representation Hypothesis: Are AI Models Converging on a Shared Reality?

If you read a story about dogs, you might remember it the next time you see one in a park. This is possible because you have a unified concept of "dog" that is not tied to words or images alone. AI systems, however, are not always so lucky. They often learn by processing data of a single type—text for language models or images for computer vision systems. This raises a fundamental question: to what extent do language and vision models have a shared understanding of the same object?

Researchers investigate such questions by peering inside AI systems to study how they represent scenes and sentences. They have found that different AI models can develop similar internal representations, even when trained on different datasets or entirely different data types [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987). Furthermore, these representations grow more similar as the models become more capable [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987), [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). Four AI researchers at MIT dubbed this idea the Platonic Representation Hypothesis, a concept that has since inspired a lively debate and a series of follow-up studies [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987), [[3]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.12121v4), [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01201v5).

The hypothesis gets its name from Plato's 2,400-year-old allegory of the cave. In the original story, prisoners trapped inside a cave perceive the world only through shadows cast on a wall, mistaking these projections for reality. The Platonic Representation Hypothesis adapts this for AI: the real world outside the cave casts machine-readable "shadows" as data streams. AI models are the prisoners, and the hypothesis claims that as these models grow more sophisticated, they converge on a shared "Platonic representation" of the world behind the data [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987), [[5]](https://phillipi.github.io/prh). Phillip Isola, a senior author of the paper, puts it simply: "Why do the language model and the vision model align? Because they’re both shadows of the same world" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Not everyone is convinced. A key point of contention involves which representations to focus on and how to compare them across different models. You cannot inspect a model's internal state for every possible input, so how do you decide which ones are representative? It is unlikely that researchers will reach a consensus soon, but that does not bother Isola. "Half the community says this is obvious, and the other half says this is obviously wrong," he said. "We were happy with that response" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Having framed the hypothesis and its philosophical roots, we will now explore the geometric mechanism that makes such comparisons possible, by examining how representations are compared through the company their vectors keep.

## The Company Being Kept

If researchers do not agree on Plato, they might find common ground with his predecessor Pythagoras, whose philosophy supposedly started from the premise "All is number." This is an apt description of modern neural networks. Their internal representations of words or pictures are just long lists of numbers, each indicating the activation of a specific artificial neuron [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Researchers typically represent these activations as high-dimensional vectors, which are impossible to visualize directly. This makes direct comparison between separate networks nonsensical, as their internal coordinate systems are different. However, we can compare them indirectly by looking at their relational geometry. Within a single model, similar inputs tend to have similar representations. For instance, the vector for "dog" will be close to vectors for "pet" and "furry" but far from "molasses." This reflects a principle from British linguist John Rupert Firth: "You shall know a word by the company it keeps" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

This principle allows us to compare different models. Instead of matching individual vectors, we can ask if two models' representations keep the same "company." Imagine you feed a list of animal words into two language models. Each model will produce a cluster of vectors. While the clusters will not look identical, you can measure how similar their overall shapes are. You would expect the "cat" vector to be close to the "dog" vector in both networks, while the "jellyfish" vector points in a different direction. But do the models agree on whether "dog" is more like "cat" or "wolf"? This is where we can measure alignment.

Ilia Sucholutsky, an AI researcher at New York University, described this process as "measuring the similarity of similarities" [[6]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). Researchers began using this approach in the mid-2010s and found that different models often had similar, though not identical, representations [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). Intriguingly, some studies found that more powerful models seemed to have more similarities than weaker ones. A 2021 paper dubbed this the "Anna Karenina scenario," a nod to the opening line of the famous novel: successful AI models might all be alike, while every unsuccessful model is unsuccessful in its own way [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07682).

Much of this early work focused on computer vision, which was the dominant field of AI research at the time. The rise of powerful language models presented a new opportunity to see just how far representational similarity could go [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). With these measurement tools in hand, we can now look at the hierarchy of experimental evidence showing that this convergence strengthens as models scale.

## Convergent Evolution

The story of the Platonic Representation Hypothesis began in early 2023, a turbulent time for AI researchers. ChatGPT had been released a few months prior, and it was becoming clear that simply scaling up AI models made them better at many tasks. But it was unclear why [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

"Everyone in AI research was going through an existential life crisis," said Minyoung Huh, an OpenAI researcher who was a graduate student in Isola’s lab at the time. He began meeting with Isola and their colleagues to discuss how scaling might affect internal representations [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). When stronger models trained on the same data learn more similar representations, it could just mean they are better at grasping quirks of the training set. A more compelling hierarchy of evidence emerges when we consider different training conditions.

If models trained on *different* datasets converge, it suggests they are grasping shared features of the world behind the data. Even stronger evidence comes from convergence between models trained on entirely different data types, such as language and vision models [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). A year after their initial conversations, Isola and his colleagues wrote a paper reviewing the evidence for this phenomenon and formally presenting the Platonic Representation Hypothesis [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987).

By then, other researchers had already found evidence of alignment between vision and language model representations [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987). Huh conducted his own experiment, testing five vision models and eleven language models on a dataset of captioned pictures from Wikipedia. He fed the pictures into the vision models and the captions into the language models, then compared the resulting vector clusters. He observed a steady increase in representational similarity as models became more powerful, exactly as the hypothesis predicted [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

After reviewing the evidence for convergence, we must examine the experimental choices that can affect the validity and generalizability of these results.

## Find the Universals

Of course, it is never so simple. Measurements of representational similarity involve a host of experimental choices that can affect the outcome. Which layers do you look at in each network? Which of the many available metrics do you use to compare them? And which inputs do you measure in the first place [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107)?

Christopher Wolfram, a researcher at the University of Chicago, has studied this problem and noted, "If you only test one dataset, you don’t necessarily know how [the result] generalizes. Who knows what would happen if you did some weirder dataset?" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107), [[8]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08775). Isola acknowledges the issue is far from settled, but argues that cases where models *do* converge are more compelling. "The endeavor of science is to find the universals," he said. "We could study the ways in which models are different or disagree, but that somehow has less explanatory power than identifying the commonalities" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Other researchers, like Alexei Efros at UC Berkeley, argue it is more productive to focus on where models differ. Efros noted that in the Wikipedia dataset Huh used, the images and text contained very similar information by design. However, much of the data we encounter has features that resist translation. "There is a reason why you go to an art museum instead of just reading the catalog," he said [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Even partial alignment has practical payoffs. Last summer, researchers used this inherent sameness to translate internal representations of sentences from one language model to another [[9]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12540). If language and vision representations are interchangeable to some extent, it could lead to new ways to train models that learn from both data types, a possibility Isola and others explored in a recent paper [[10]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08492). Other studies have shown that explicitly enforcing alignment can improve unimodal encoders, especially when redundant information exists across modalities [[3]](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.12121v4).

Despite these promising developments, it is unlikely that a single theory will fully capture the behavior of modern AI. Jeff Clune, an AI researcher at the University of British Columbia, cautions, "You can’t reduce a trillion-parameter system to simple explanations. The answers are going to be complicated" [[2]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

## Conclusion

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis offers a compelling framework for understanding a fundamental trend in AI: as models scale, their internal "world models" appear to converge. This suggests that despite their different architectures and training data, they are all discovering a shared statistical structure of reality. While the debate over the extent and implications of this convergence continues, the practical benefits are already emerging.

For AI engineers, this convergence is more than a philosophical curiosity. It points toward a future where we can build more reliable and efficient multimodal systems. Understanding that different models may share a common representational language allows us to translate between them, transfer knowledge more effectively, and build agents that can reason across different data types without losing semantic fidelity. The journey to a true "Platonic representation" may be complex, but it is a path worth exploring.
