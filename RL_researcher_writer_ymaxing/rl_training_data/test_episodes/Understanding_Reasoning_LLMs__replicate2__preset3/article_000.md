# The 4 Ways to Build Reasoning Models

Reasoning models are the key 2025 LLM specialization trend. This approach extends beyond familiar patterns like Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) or domain-specific fine-tuning. Instead of injecting external knowledge, reasoning specialization targets the emergence of robust, multi-step logical capabilities.![Image 1: Stages 1-3 are the common steps to developing LLMs. Stage 4 specializes LLMs for specific use cases.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!QwUc!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd6ebc5c9-461f-4d3a-889b-b8ea4e14e5ba_1600x830.png)

This specialization is aimed at complex, multi-step tasks like mathematical proofs, logical puzzles, and competitive programming. It does not replace general-purpose LLMs for everyday generation, summarization, or simple question-answering. However, these reasoning gains come with drawbacks. They often introduce increased inference latency and cost due to longer outputs, a risk of overthinking simple tasks, and trade-offs where improving one capability can degrade another.

Having set the context and roadmap, we now establish a working definition of "reasoning model" that you can use to evaluate future systems and research.

## How do we define "reasoning model"?

A reasoning model is an LLM designed to solve multi-step problems by generating intermediate steps, whether those steps are visible to the user or handled internally. Unlike a standard LLM that might provide a direct answer, a reasoning model breaks down the problem, showing its work or thinking through the process to arrive at a more reliable conclusion.![Image 2: A regular LLM may only provide a short answer (as shown on the left), whereas reasoning models typically include intermediate steps that reveal part of the thought process. (Note that many LLMs who have not been specifically developed for reasoning tasks can also provide intermediate reasoning steps in their answers.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!8oZo!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff2987079-25f4-45fb-a020-1ac936ed16cb_1424x820.png)
Image 2: A regular LLM may only provide a short answer, whereas reasoning models typically include intermediate steps. (Source [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/state-of-llm-reasoning-and-inference-scaling))

For example, asking "How far does a train travel at 60 mph for 3 hours?" is a simple calculation. A standard model might just output "180 miles." A reasoning model, however, would likely first state the formula `distance = speed × time` and then show the calculation, explaining how it arrived at the answer.

All modern LLMs exhibit some level of reasoning, which can be enhanced with techniques like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. However, specialized reasoning models are engineered to excel at difficult benchmarks, such as math olympiad problems or formal proofs, where multi-step logic is non-negotiable.

These intermediate steps can manifest in two primary ways:

1.  **Visible thought traces:** The model generates a step-by-step output that the user can read, similar to showing your work in a math problem.
2.  **Invisible internal iterations:** The model allocates more computational resources at inference time to "think" internally without exposing every step. This is rumored to be part of the approach used for models like OpenAI's o1.![Image 3: "Reasoning" is used at two different levels: 1) processing the input and generating via multiple intermediate steps and 2) providing some sort of reasoning as part of the response to the user.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!DyRP!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F35712d0e-0f40-4855-8d81-4dcea94055ce_1538x810.png)
Image 3: "Reasoning" can refer to the internal process or the visible output trace. (Source [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/state-of-llm-reasoning-and-inference-scaling))

With a clear definition established, we can now examine the practical question of when deploying these more expensive reasoning models actually makes sense for an AI engineer.

## When should we use reasoning models?

Before diving into the technical details, it is important to know when reasoning models are truly needed. They deliver the highest returns on complex problems that require decomposition, logical deduction, or self-correction, such as puzzles, advanced mathematics, and competitive coding. However, they are often overkill for tasks like summarization, simple factual question-answering, or creative writing, where direct generation is sufficient.

The practical downsides are significant. The verbose intermediate steps lead to higher latency and token costs. This increased verbosity can also frustrate users in conversational settings who just want a quick answer. Furthermore, there is a risk of "overthinking," where the model invents unnecessary complications for straightforward problems. This can degrade not only efficiency but also accuracy, as models may fail to reliably identify their own errors or get stuck in reflective loops with diminishing returns [[31]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18585), [[44]](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/3e22bea3b170f4c2aebb9c48d98ae64d-Paper-Conference.pdf), [[45]](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24726v1).![Image 4: The key strengths and weaknesses of reasoning models.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!lnf2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F46dbe029-ab7d-4278-8dfe-7bc4af79a103_1352x524.png)
Image 4: The key strengths and weaknesses of reasoning models. (Source [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/understanding-reasoning-llms))

Understanding when to deploy reasoning models leads naturally to studying a concrete, open pipeline that demonstrates how such capabilities are created at scale.

## A brief look at the DeepSeek training pipeline

The DeepSeek-R1 family of models provides an excellent case study for understanding how reasoning capabilities are developed. The pipeline involves three key variants, each building on the last.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  "DeepSeek-V3 Base" -- "Cold-start Pure RL" --> "DeepSeek-R1-Zero"
  "DeepSeek-R1-Zero" -- "SFT + RL Iterations" --> "DeepSeek-R1"
  "DeepSeek-R1" -- "Distillation" --> "Smaller Distilled Models"
```
Image 5: A flowchart illustrating the development and relationships of the DeepSeek-R1 model variants.

First, there is **DeepSeek-R1-Zero**. This model is created using a "cold-start" pure Reinforcement Learning (RL) process directly from the DeepSeek-V3 base model. Unlike standard RLHF, this approach skips the initial Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) stage. The idea is that by not constraining the model with human-defined reasoning patterns, it can explore and discover novel reasoning pathways on its own. This requires strong, verifiable reward signals, such as checking the correctness of a final answer in a math problem [[4]](https://thelmbook.com/articles#!./DeepSeek-R1.md).

Next is **DeepSeek-R1**, the flagship reasoning model. It refines R1-Zero through additional SFT stages and further RL training. This multi-stage process helps address some of the "RL oddities" found in R1-Zero, like poor readability or mixing languages, making the model more aligned with human preferences while retaining its strong reasoning abilities [[25]](https://artgor.medium.com/paper-review-deepseek-r1-incentivizing-reasoning-capability-in-llms-via-reinforcement-learning-edf4343dcf3a).

Finally, there are the **DeepSeek-R1-Distill** models. This is not classical knowledge distillation where logits are transferred. Instead, it involves taking the high-quality, long chain-of-thought outputs generated by the powerful R1 model and using them as a dataset for SFT on smaller, open-source models like Llama and Qwen. This transfers the reasoning capabilities to more efficient models without repeating the expensive RL process from scratch [[11]](https://medium.com/data-science-in-your-pocket/what-are-deepseek-r1-distilled-models-329629968d5d).

The DeepSeek pipeline incorporates all four main techniques for building reasoning models, which we will now examine in depth.

## The 4 main ways to build and improve reasoning models

The current key techniques to enhance LLM reasoning and build specialized reasoning models can be grouped into four main categories. While the exact workings of OpenAI's o1 and o3 models remain unknown, they are rumored to combine several of these training and inference techniques.

### 1) Inference-time scaling

Inference-time scaling refers to dedicating more computational resources during inference to improve a model's output. The analogy is simple: just as a human can produce a better answer when given more time to think, an LLM can improve its performance on complex tasks with additional "thinking time" [[46]](https://openai.com/index/learning-to-reason-with-llms/).

This can be achieved through several methods. The most basic is **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting**, where a simple phrase like "Let's think step by step" encourages the model to generate intermediate reasoning steps before giving a final answer. This breaks down a complex problem into a sequence of simpler ones [[67]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916). This approach has roots in historical symbolic AI systems, which also relied on structured, step-by-step procedures to perform logical deduction. CoT prompting effectively elicits a similar symbolic-like behavior from modern neural networks [[47]](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/13/11/1707).![Image 6: An example of classic CoT prompting from the 2022 Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners paper.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!VFAa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F523eee5e-afb6-4019-a11b-e0a291d2c286_1600x419.png)
Image 6: An example of classic CoT prompting from the 2022 Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners paper [[67]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916).

More advanced techniques involve search strategies. **Majority voting** or **best-of-N sampling** involves generating multiple responses and selecting the most common or highest-rated one using a verifier model. More sophisticated methods like **beam search** or **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)** explore a tree of possible reasoning paths, pruning unpromising branches and focusing computation on the most likely solutions. These search-based methods often rely on a **Process Reward Model (PRM)**, which evaluates the correctness of each intermediate step in a reasoning chain, providing a fine-grained signal to guide the search [[26]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314). This contrasts with an Outcome Reward Model (ORM), which only judges the final answer. By providing step-by-step feedback, PRMs enable finer-grained alignment for complex reasoning tasks [[48]](https://www.jonvet.com/blog/llm-test-time-compute).![Image 7: Different search-based methods rely on a process-reward-based model to select the best answer.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!YGJO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5cb10e5a-738b-4c9e-ba65-5850d4793706_1600x919.png)
Image 7: Different search-based methods rely on a process-reward-based model to select the best answer. (Source [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/state-of-llm-reasoning-and-inference-scaling))

The development of PRMs typically follows a closed loop: generating process data (through human, automated, or hybrid annotation), training the PRM to evaluate steps, and then using the PRM to improve the reasoning model. This improved model can then generate even better data, continuing the cycle [[49]](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.08049v3). While powerful, PRM-based verification has its own drawbacks, primarily the high computational cost of training and running them. This has spurred research into more efficient alternatives, such as lightweight verifiers that probe a model's internal states to estimate the credibility of its reasoning steps without needing a separate, large reward model [[50]](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.06209v3).

Interestingly, the DeepSeek R1 technical report categorized common inference-time scaling methods like PRM-based search and MCTS under "unsuccessful attempts" [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948). This suggests that their core model's reasoning ability relies more on its training than on complex inference-time search algorithms. However, it is plausible that DeepSeek could still apply these techniques at the application layer to further boost performance.

This trade-off between training-time and inference-time compute might explain why models like OpenAI's o1 are significantly more expensive than models like GPT-4o. The higher cost likely reflects the additional computation required for the model to "think longer" before responding, a direct application of inference-time scaling [[38]](https://neoteric.eu/blog/gpt-o1-vs-gpt-4o-comparison).

### 2) Pure reinforcement learning (RL)

Pure RL, as exemplified by DeepSeek-R1-Zero, represents a departure from the typical RLHF pipeline. Instead of starting with a model already fine-tuned on human demonstrations (SFT), this approach applies RL directly to the base model. While DeepSeek-R1-Zero demonstrates this is possible, a 'cold start' without any SFT is often less stable. An initial SFT phase on high-quality reasoning data provides a better starting policy for RL, allowing it to focus on refining reasoning quality rather than learning basic output formats. Some research even suggests using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) for this initial phase to achieve smoother and more stable convergence during the subsequent RL training [[51]](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.25801v3). The reward signals are not derived from a reward model trained on human preferences, but from rule-based verifiers [[23]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09422-z).![Image 8: The development process of the DeepSeek-R1-Zero model.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_9Z-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa5bb6ecc-7e46-45fe-abff-1eb02e6b0e3a_1556x1162.png)
Image 8: The development process of the DeepSeek-R1-Zero model. (Source [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/understanding-reasoning-llms))

In the DeepSeek-R1 paper, two main types of rewards are used:

1.  **Accuracy rewards:** The model receives a positive reward if its final answer is correct. This is straightforward for tasks with deterministic solutions, like math problems where the answer can be checked, or coding problems where test cases can be run [[24]](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/deepseek-r1-recipe-for-o1).
2.  **Format rewards:** The model is incentivized to follow specific formatting rules, such as enclosing its reasoning process within `<think>` and `</think>` tags. This ensures the output is structured and interpretable [[22]](https://thelmbook.com/articles#!./DeepSeek-R1.md).

One of the most inspiring results from this approach is the emergence of the **"Aha moment"**. During training, the model spontaneously starts generating longer, more complex reasoning traces. It begins to self-reflect, verify its work, and explore alternative solutions without being explicitly taught to do so. The DeepSeek paper highlights an instance where the model generates "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here," before re-evaluating its approach [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).![Image 9: A figure from the DeepSeek R1 technical report showing the emergence of the "Aha" moment.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Prn2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F30f8e37b-ba60-49d2-a95e-9c06b2033ee4_1600x1019.png)
Image 9: A figure from the DeepSeek R1 technical report showing the emergence of the "Aha" moment [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

However, the term "emergence" might be misleading. Recent research suggests that these self-reflection capabilities are not created from scratch by RL. Instead, they appear to be latent abilities already present in the base model from pre-training, which are then amplified and made more frequent by the RL process. Probing studies have shown that while a fine-tuned model might use reflection constantly, the pretrained base model already exhibits the same behavior, just far more rarely [[52]](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12217v1). This underscores how much the undisclosed pre-training data mixture can influence the stability and outcome of any RL-based fine-tuning. The behaviors that RL can amplify depend heavily on what patterns were already seeded in the base model [[53]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SD5raqvYG-0). This demonstrates that sophisticated reasoning capabilities can emerge from pure RL, guided only by outcome-based rewards. It is the first major instance showing that it is possible to develop reasoning models this way, unlocking a new path for AI research.

### 3) Supervised finetuning and reinforcement learning (SFT + RL)

The hybrid SFT + RL approach, used to create the final DeepSeek-R1 model, is a multi-stage process designed to combine the exploratory power of RL with the stability and alignment of SFT. This method addresses the shortcomings of pure RL, such as poor readability and language mixing, resulting in a model that is both a powerful reasoner and user-friendly [[25]](https://artgor.medium.com/paper-review-deepseek-r1-incentivizing-reasoning-capability-in-llms-via-reinforcement-learning-edf4343dcf3a). By providing a strong "prior" through SFT, this approach eliminates much of the instability of a pure RL cold start, leading to faster and more reliable training [[54]](https://cameronrwolfe.substack.com/p/demystifying-reasoning-models).![Image 10: The development process of the DeepSeek-R1 model.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!19pK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdf7f99f0-d154-49e5-b60a-4d148e0a61be_1548x1154.png)
Image 10: The development process of the DeepSeek-R1 model. (Source [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/understanding-reasoning-llms))

The process typically involves several stages:

1.  **Cold-Start Data Generation:** The process begins by using the R1-Zero model to generate a "cold-start" dataset of a few thousand reasoning traces. These traces are then refined by human annotators to ensure they are readable and follow a conversational style [[2]](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/deepseek-r1-recipe-for-o1).
2.  **Initial SFT:** The base model is fine-tuned on this small, high-quality dataset. This "warms up" the model, giving it a foundation in coherent, human-aligned reasoning before the more intensive RL phase begins.
3.  **RL with Consistency Rewards:** The model then undergoes large-scale RL, similar to R1-Zero. However, in addition to accuracy and format rewards, a **language consistency reward** is introduced. This reward penalizes the model for mixing languages within a response, which was a known issue with R1-Zero. While this might cause a slight dip in raw performance on some tasks, it significantly improves user experience [[27]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09422-z).
4.  **Iterative Refinement:** The process can be iterative, with additional stages of SFT and RL to further polish the model's capabilities, incorporating data from both reasoning and general-purpose domains.

This staged refinement allows for a more controlled development process. The initial SFT provides stability, while the subsequent RL phases enhance reasoning capabilities in a targeted manner. The final DeepSeek-R1 model demonstrates performance on par with or even exceeding OpenAI's o1 on several key reasoning benchmarks, particularly in math and coding [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).![Image 11: Benchmark comparison of OpenAI O1 and DeepSeek R1 models.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!22Cm!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff7f73f16-db4e-4047-89b0-823f16cefb33_1556x490.png)
Image 11: Benchmark comparison of OpenAI O1 and DeepSeek R1 models. Annotated figure from the DeepSeek-R1 technical report [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

### 4) Pure supervised finetuning (SFT) and distillation

To recap, we have covered three primary methods for building reasoning models: inference-time scaling, which uses more compute at runtime; pure RL, which discovers reasoning from scratch; and the SFT+RL hybrid, which balances discovery with stability. The fourth approach, pure SFT and distillation, offers a more direct and often more efficient path to enhancing reasoning.

DeepSeek's approach to creating their distilled models is a practical example of this. Instead of the traditional method of knowledge distillation, which involves matching the output distributions (logits) of a student model to a teacher model, DeepSeek uses a simpler form of SFT. They generated a large dataset of 800,000 high-quality reasoning samples using their powerful R1 model and then fine-tuned smaller, existing open-source models (like Qwen and Llama) on this synthetic data [[14]](https://www.bentoml.com/blog/the-complete-guide-to-deepseek-models-from-v3-to-r1-and-beyond).![Image 12: The development process of DeepSeek-R1-Distill models.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xUjE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7db7c46b-fe67-49f4-9f65-b0e7b7e5ac08_1444x1174.png)
Image 12: The development process of DeepSeek-R1-Distill models. (Source [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/understanding-reasoning-llms))

There are two key reasons why DeepSeek developed these distilled models. First, it democratizes access to advanced reasoning. The massive 671B R1 model is impractical for most users to run, but smaller distilled models make these capabilities accessible on consumer-grade hardware. Second, it is highly efficient. This SFT-only process avoids the computational expense and complexity of RL, providing a straightforward way to transfer reasoning skills [[11]](https://medium.com/data-science-in-your-pocket/what-are-deepseek-r1-distilled-models-329629968d5d).

The results are impressive. As shown in the benchmarks, even the smallest distilled models significantly outperform powerful, non-reasoning-focused models like GPT-4o and Claude-3.5-Sonnet on mathematical tasks [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).![Image 13: Benchmark comparison of distilled versus non-distilled models.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!XwZe!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Febc749fb-6a79-483f-bcda-b219f284bc09_1168x604.png)
Image 13: Benchmark comparison of distilled versus non-distilled models. Annotated figure from the DeepSeek-R1 technical report [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

The DeepSeek report also provides a crucial comparison: how does this distillation approach stack up against applying pure RL to smaller models? They trained a 32B model with the same pure RL process as R1-Zero and found that the distilled 32B model performed significantly better across all benchmarks.![Image 14: Benchmark comparison of distillation and RL on a smaller 32B model.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!5_5L!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F05514c9f-eb04-496b-bd98-bb4710c65b14_1448x408.png)
Image 14: Benchmark comparison of distillation and RL on a smaller 32B model. Annotated figure from the DeepSeek-R1 technical report [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

This leads to two key insights. First, for smaller models, distillation is a more effective and economical strategy for imparting reasoning skills than pure RL. The emergent reasoning behavior seen in massive models does not seem to appear as readily at smaller scales. Second, while distillation is powerful, pushing the absolute frontier of AI reasoning likely still requires the combination of massive base models and large-scale reinforcement learning [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948). It would have been interesting to see a comparison with a 32B model trained with the full SFT+RL pipeline, not just pure RL, to understand if that hybrid approach could close the gap with distillation. Also, a comparison with a 32B model simply fine-tuned on general instruction data would have provided a clearer baseline for the gains from reasoning-specific training.

After dissecting these four techniques, we can step back to evaluate the broader significance of the DeepSeek-R1 release.

## Thoughts about DeepSeek R1

The release of the DeepSeek-R1 models is a significant moment for the open-weight AI community. We appreciate several aspects of this release: the open MIT license, which encourages broad use and experimentation; the unusually detailed technical report, which provides a valuable blueprint for other researchers; and the clear demonstration that pure RL from a cold start can produce emergent reasoning behaviors without a supervised warm-up.

When comparing DeepSeek-R1 to OpenAI's o1, the benchmarks show comparable performance on math, coding, and reasoning tasks. However, DeepSeek-R1 appears to have superior inference efficiency. This suggests different strategic bets: DeepSeek invested heavily in training-time compute to bake reasoning into the model, while o1 seems to lean more on expensive inference-time scaling to achieve its results [[41]](https://www.vellum.ai/blog/analysis-openai-o1-vs-gpt-4o).

However, any direct comparison is limited by the lack of transparency from OpenAI. We do not know the size of the o1 model, its architecture, or the exact mix of techniques used in its training. Without this information, it is difficult to definitively attribute performance differences to specific methods. A direct comparison is unwarranted until more details about o1 are disclosed.

There is also the reality of training costs. The widely cited figure of around $294,000 for DeepSeek-R1 likely refers to the final RL and SFT stages, not the full cost of pre-training the massive 671B DeepSeek-V3 base model, which was estimated to be around $5.3 million [[43]](https://epoch.ai/gradient-updates/what-went-into-training-deepseek-r1). The DeepSeek team has not disclosed the exact GPU hours or total development cost for R1, so the true incremental cost of adding this reasoning specialization remains unclear.

Despite these caveats, DeepSeek-R1 stands as a milestone. It proves that high-level reasoning capabilities need not be the exclusive domain of closed-source labs, accelerating open research into all four of the techniques we have discussed.

## Developing reasoning models on a limited budget

The development costs of reasoning models, even when starting from an open-weight base, can be discouraging for researchers and engineers with limited budgets. Fortunately, several recent projects have demonstrated effective, budget-conscious approaches.

One of the most practical methods is **distillation**, as shown by the Sky-T1 project from UC Berkeley’s Sky Computing Lab. By curating a dataset of just 17,000 high-quality reasoning samples, they were able to train a model for under $450 that is competitive with an earlier version of OpenAI's o1 on several reasoning benchmarks. This illustrates how the distillation concept can be scaled down effectively, providing a powerful yet affordable path to specialized reasoning [[17]](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/11/researchers-open-source-sky-t1-a-reasoning-ai-model-that-can-be-trained-for-less-than-450).![Image 15: Benchmark results for the Sky-T1-32B-Preview model.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Y8HI!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F8865a313-2326-4f07-a6dc-72cc94cb2ebe_1364x570.png)
Image 15: Figure from the "Sky-T1: Train your own O1 preview model within $450" article. (Source [magazine.sebastianraschka.com](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/understanding-reasoning-llms))

**Pure RL at a small scale** has also shown promise. The TinyZero project trained a 3B parameter model for under $30 on specific tasks like countdown and multiplication. Even at this small scale, the model exhibited emergent self-verification and reasoning traces, reinforcing the idea that cold-start RL can produce useful behaviors without massive compute [[20]](https://github.com/Jiayi-Pan/TinyZero).![Image 16: A figure from the TinyZero repository showing that the model is capable of self-verification.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ykdn!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6111f4b4-cfb9-494c-8390-ec251702914b_1600x955.png)
Image 16: A figure from the TinyZero repository showing that the model is capable of self-verification [[20]](https://github.com/Jiayi-Pan/TinyZero).

A third, and perhaps most interesting, budget-friendly approach is **journey learning**. This concept, explored in the O1 Replication Journey report, contrasts with "shortcut learning," where models are only trained on "golden paths" or correct solutions. Journey learning involves training the model on the full problem-solving trajectory, including incorrect paths, self-corrections, and backtracking [[30]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18982).![Image 17: Journey learning, as opposed to traditional shortcut learning, includes wrong solution paths in the SFT data.](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TxCO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7a0bfcd0-6d93-4c91-a0d6-28178839b7cf_1492x724.png)
Image 17: Journey learning, as opposed to traditional shortcut learning, includes wrong solution paths in the SFT data. Annotated figure from the O1 Replication Journey: A Strategic Progress Report – Part 1 [[30]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18982).

By exposing the model to mistakes during SFT, it learns robust self-correction mechanisms. Models trained only on perfect solutions can be brittle and struggle when they encounter novel errors. In contrast, a model trained with journey learning understands how to identify and recover from mistakes. However, this approach requires careful data balancing. If incorrect paths and self-corrections dominate the training data distribution, it can lead to unstable convergence or even cause the model to persistently generate incorrect answers, as the error patterns can conflict with the base model's pretrained knowledge [[55]](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01624v1). The O1 Replication Journey report showed that with only 327 training samples, journey learning outperformed conventional SFT by over 8% on the MATH dataset, demonstrating its powerful potential [[30]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18982).

Future directions will likely involve combining insights from these budget-conscious projects. Hybrid pipelines that use distillation for a strong baseline, incorporate journey learning data to build robustness, and apply small-scale RL for targeted improvements could offer a balanced approach to developing capable and reliable reasoning models without breaking the bank.

## Conclusion

We have explored the four primary approaches to building and improving reasoning models. **Inference-time scaling** offers a way to enhance performance without retraining but comes at a high serving cost. **Pure RL** provides a path to emergent, novel reasoning but can be unstable and computationally intensive. The **SFT+RL hybrid** offers a production-ready blueprint that balances stability and performance, especially when verifiable rewards are available. Finally, **distillation** provides an efficient method for transferring capabilities to smaller models, though its performance is ultimately limited by the teacher model.

Looking ahead, we forecast a hybrid future where the most capable systems will combine these techniques. Production-grade reasoning models will likely be built using robust SFT+RL pipelines and then further enhanced with inference-time scaling for the most challenging tasks. This is the pattern we suspect underlies models like o1 and will likely define the next generation of o3-class models.

The impact of these reasoning techniques extends far beyond benchmarks. In fields like robotics, they are being adapted to give autonomous systems the ability to handle long-horizon planning, execute multi-step tasks, and recover from errors in dynamic environments [[56]](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-2688/6/7/158). Similarly, in finance, reasoning LLMs are being deployed as autonomous trading agents that can adhere to complex strategies like value investing or momentum trading, reacting to market dynamics in real-time [[57]](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.10789v1). These applications show that mastering reasoning is not just an academic exercise but a core component of building next-generation AI products.

For AI engineers, the key takeaway is to match the technique to the constraints of the project. There is no one-size-fits-all solution. The choice between these approaches should be a strategic one, guided by your budget, performance targets, latency requirements, and the need for innovation versus proven reliability. Instead of defaulting to the largest proprietary model, a careful consideration of these trade-offs will lead to more effective and sustainable AI systems.
