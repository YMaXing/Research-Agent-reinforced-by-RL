# The Platonic Representation Hypothesis: Are All AI Models Converging?

If you read a story about a dog, you might remember it the next time you see one bounding through a park. This is possible because you have a unified concept of "dog" that is not tied to words or images alone. Whether it is a bulldog or a border collie, whether you hear it barking or see it getting its belly rubbed, a dog remains a dog. As humans, we build these abstract concepts naturally.

AI systems are not always so lucky. They learn by ingesting vast amounts of data, but often, that data is of a single type. You have language models trained on text, vision systems on images, and other specialized models for things like predicting the smell of molecules. This reality of AI development forces a fundamental question for us engineers: to what extent do these separate models have a shared understanding of the world? Does a language model's "dog" have anything in common with a vision model's "dog"?

This is not just a philosophical question. For anyone building multimodal agents, it is a core engineering problem. We need to know if we can reliably translate information between different AI components without losing meaning. Researchers have been digging into this by peering inside AI systems to study how they represent scenes and sentences. A growing body of work has found that different AI models can develop similar internal representations, even when trained on different datasets or entirely different data types. Furthermore, some studies suggest these representations grow more similar as the models become more capable [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13018), [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987).

In a 2024 paper, four researchers at MIT argued that these hints of convergence are no fluke. They called their idea the Platonic representation hypothesis, and it has sparked a lively debate with a series of follow-up papers [[3]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16282), [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05283), [[5]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.03750), [[6]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.02767). The hypothesis gets its name from Plato's 2,400-year-old Allegory of the Cave, where prisoners only see shadows of real objects. Plato argued that the objects we see are just imperfect copies of ideal "forms" in a higher, perfect world.

The AI version of this is more concrete. The real world is outside the cave, and it casts machine-readable shadows as streams of data. AI models are the prisoners. The hypothesis claims that very different models, exposed only to these data streams, are starting to converge on a shared "Platonic representation" of the world behind the data. This idea is also related to "convergent realism" from the philosophy of science, which suggests that scientific theories are progressively getting closer to a true description of reality [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987), [[7]](https://3dvar.com/Huh2024The.pdf).

Of course, the idea is not without its critics. A major point of contention is how you even measure this. You cannot inspect a model’s internal state for every possible sentence or image. How do you decide which representations matter? How do you compare them across completely different architectures? A consensus is unlikely anytime soon. Phillip Isola, the senior author of the paper, is not bothered. “Half the community says this is obvious, and the other half says this is obviously wrong,” he said. “We were happy with that response” [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Having framed the hypothesis and its philosophical roots, we transition to the precise geometric mechanism that makes such comparisons possible by examining how representations are compared through the company their vectors keep.

## The Company Being Kept

If you are an AI engineer, you know that under the hood, everything is just numbers. This brings to mind Pythagoras's philosophy, "All is number." It is a perfect description for neural networks. Their representations of words or pictures are just long lists of numbers, or vectors, with each number showing the activation level of an artificial neuron [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

To make the math manageable, researchers usually focus on a single layer in the network. They treat the neuron activations as a vector, which is just an arrow pointing in a specific direction in a high-dimensional space. You cannot visualize these spaces directly, but vectors give you a straightforward way to compare representations. If two vectors point in similar directions, their representations are considered similar [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Inside a single model, similar inputs get similar representations. For a language model, the vector for "dog" will be close to vectors for "pet," "bark," and "furry," but far from "Platonic" or "molasses." This follows a principle from the linguist John Rupert Firth, who famously said: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps” [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). The meaning of a concept is defined by its relationships to all other concepts.

To compare representations across different models, you cannot just match up the vectors directly. Instead, you can use Firth's principle and see if the representations keep the same company. For example, to compare how two language models represent animals, you would feed a list of words—dog, cat, wolf, jellyfish—into both models and record the resulting vectors. This gives you two clusters of vectors. The question then becomes: how similar are the overall shapes of these two clusters? “It can kind of be described as measuring the similarity of similarities,” said Ilia Sucholutsky, an AI researcher at New York University [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107), [[9]](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/representational-similarity-analysis-rsa).

You would expect some basic similarities. The "cat" vector should be near the "dog" vector in both models, while the "jellyfish" vector would be off on its own. But the fine-grained structure might differ. Is "dog" more like "cat" or "wolf"? If the models were trained on different data or have different architectures, they might disagree [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107). This technique, known as Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA), transforms high-dimensional activity into matrices of dissimilarities, allowing for these cross-system comparisons [[9]](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/representational-similarity-analysis-rsa).

Researchers started studying this in the mid-2010s and found that representations were often similar, but not identical [[10]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1511.07543). One early technique was *model stitching*, where an intermediate layer from one model is "stitched" into another via a learned transformation. If the combined model performs well, it indicates the representations were compatible. This showed that a vision model trained on ImageNet could be aligned with one trained on the Places-365 dataset, suggesting a degree of data independence [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987).

Interestingly, a few studies noticed that more powerful models seemed to have more similar representations. A 2021 paper called this the "Anna Karenina scenario," referencing the novel's opening line. The idea is that all successful AI models are alike, while every unsuccessful model is unsuccessful in its own way. This aligns with the hypothesis that there is a single, optimal structure for them to discover [[11]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07682).

Much of this early research was limited to computer vision, which was the dominant field at the time. The arrival of powerful language models opened up new possibilities to test just how far this representational similarity could go. With these measurement tools in hand, we can now look at the hierarchy of experimental evidence showing that convergence strengthens as models scale.

## Convergent Evolution

The title of this section is a direct analogy to a concept from evolutionary biology. In biology, convergent evolution describes how unrelated species independently evolve similar traits to solve similar problems. A classic example is wings in birds, bats, and insects. The argument, extended to AI, is that when very different systems like a biological brain and a silicon neural network arrive at similar representational strategies, it suggests those strategies are effective, perhaps even necessary, solutions for processing information about the world [[12]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2319709121).

The story of the Platonic representation hypothesis paper began in early 2023. ChatGPT had been out for a few months, and it was clear that just scaling up AI models made them better at many tasks. But no one really knew why. “Everyone in AI research was going through an existential life crisis,” said Minyoung Huh, an OpenAI researcher who was a graduate student in Isola’s lab at the time. He started meeting with Isola and their colleagues to discuss how scaling might affect internal representations [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

If models trained on the same data learn similar representations as they get better, it does not prove they are building a better world model. They could just be memorizing the training data more effectively. A much stronger piece of evidence would be if models trained on *different* datasets also converge. And the strongest evidence of all would be convergence between models trained on entirely different types of data, like language and vision models [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

A year later, Isola and his team published their paper arguing for the Platonic representation hypothesis [[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987). By then, other researchers had already found similarities between vision and language model representations [[13]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15162), [[14]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06555), [[15]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05224). Huh ran his own experiment, testing five vision models and 11 language models on captioned images from Wikipedia. He fed the images to the vision models and the captions to the language models, then compared their vector clusters. He found a steady increase in similarity as the models became more powerful, which was exactly what the hypothesis predicted [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

After reviewing the evidence for convergence, we must examine a host of experimental choices with respect to the measurements of representational similarity that may affect the validity and generalizability of the result.

## Find the Universals

As with any scientific claim, the devil is in the details. Measuring representational similarity is not a simple plug-and-play process. It involves a host of choices that can influence the outcome. Which layers of the network do you compare? Which of the many similarity metrics do you use, such as Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) or other forms of RSA [[16]](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/systems-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/neuro.06.004.2008/full), [[17]](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.00414), [[18]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.06329)? And what data do you use to test the models?

“If you only test one dataset, you don’t necessarily know how [the result] generalizes,” said Christopher Wolfram, a researcher at the University of Chicago. “Who knows what would happen if you did some weirder dataset?” Isola acknowledges this but argues that cases where models *do* converge are more compelling than cases where they do not. “The endeavor of science is to find the universals,” Isola said. “We could study the ways in which models are different or disagree, but that somehow has less explanatory power than identifying the commonalities” [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107), [[19]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08775).

This search for universals is not limited to vision and language. In physics-informed AI, for example, models that incorporate physical laws as constraints also show more reliable convergence. This suggests that grounding representations in objective, external truths is a general principle for building more robust systems [[20]](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13777v1).

However, other researchers believe it is more productive to focus on where models differ. Alexei Efros, a researcher at UC Berkeley, is one of them. “They’re all good friends and they’re all very, very smart people,” Efros said of the MIT team. “I think they’re wrong, but that’s what science is about.” He pointed out that in the Wikipedia dataset Huh used, the images and captions were designed to contain very similar information. In the real world, data is much messier. “There is a reason why you go to an art museum instead of just reading the catalog,” he said [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

Even if the alignment is not perfect, it can still be incredibly useful from an engineering standpoint. In 2025, researchers demonstrated that it was possible to translate the internal representations of sentences from one language model to another. If language and vision representations are somewhat interchangeable, it could open up new, more efficient ways to train multimodal models. This is a possibility that Isola and others have already started to explore. This principle is also being applied in robotics, where fusing data from cameras, LiDAR, and radar into a unified representation is critical for robust navigation. Future work may extend this to more complex agentic reasoning by developing neuro-symbolic architectures that leverage this cross-modal understanding [[21]](http://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12540), [[22]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08492), [[23]](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/multi-modal-sensor-fusion), [[24]](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08516v5).

Despite these promising directions, some researchers remain skeptical that a single, elegant theory can explain the behavior of modern AI. “You can’t reduce a trillion-parameter system to simple explanations,” said Jeff Clune, an AI researcher at the University of British Columbia. “The answers are going to be complicated” [[8]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/distinct-ai-models-seem-to-converge-on-how-they-encode-reality-20260107).

The hypothesis continues to be tested and refined. For instance, the UReason benchmark was introduced in 2026 to diagnose cross-modal alignment in unified multimodal models (UMMs) that generate images from textual reasoning. The benchmark evaluates models in three settings: direct generation, reasoning-guided generation, and de-contextualized generation. The findings revealed a significant "alignment gap": while reasoning improves performance, the final generated image often fails to faithfully reflect the textual reasoning that preceded it. This suggests that any shared representations might be fragile and susceptible to contextual interference, challenging a simplistic view of convergence [[25]](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08336v2).

Other new benchmarks are also emerging to probe alignment in different domains. KRIS-Bench, introduced in 2025, evaluates alignment in image editing tasks across new multimodal architectures. In a different field, the GENEB benchmark, slated for 2026, quantitatively tests representational alignment across 40 genomic foundation models, finding that model rankings vary significantly across different biological task categories. These specialized benchmarks provide a more nuanced picture, showing that while convergence may be a general trend, its strength and nature depend heavily on the specific domain and task [[25]](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.08336v2), [[26]](https://rewire.it/blog/genomic-foundation-models-in-2026).

This tension between a clean philosophical story and the messy reality of engineering highlights the core of the debate. While the Platonic hypothesis offers an elegant explanation, real-world models may have their own unique quirks and specific behaviors that coexist with any convergent structures. The debate continues, fueled by new architectures and ever-larger datasets, pushing the boundaries of how we understand intelligence, both artificial and our own.
