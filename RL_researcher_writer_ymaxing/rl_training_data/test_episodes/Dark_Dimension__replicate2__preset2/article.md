# A Dark Dimension Could Link the Universe’s Greatest Unknowns

If you look at the cosmos, you might think it is a dark place. According to our current estimates, approximately 70% of the universe consists of dark energy, an unknown force pushing space to expand. Another 25% is dark matter, a mysterious material holding galaxies together. The remaining 5% is the ordinary, observable matter that makes up everything we can see and touch. But dark energy and dark matter are not so much “dark” as they are invisible. They do not emit, reflect, nor absorb light, and have so far proven impossible to observe directly [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The concept of dark energy has a long history. It began with Einstein adding a "cosmological constant" to his theory of general relativity to keep his model of the universe static. He dismissed this number when it became clear the universe was expanding. Decades later, the idea was reclaimed to represent a constant dark energy driving the universe’s accelerated expansion [[2]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time).

For a couple of decades, this has been the bedrock of our standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM. This model assumes dark energy is time-independent and that it has nothing to do with dark matter. But as our data gets more precise, we are finding potential cracks in this model, suggesting the two might be physically intertwined [[2]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time). In 2024 and 2025, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration found evidence that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. Using an instrument with 5,000 robotic arms to create the largest 3D map of the universe, DESI's data suggests dark energy peaked about two billion years ago and has been weakening since. In an even earlier era, it may have grown stronger [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[3]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve).

This behavior, where dark energy grows stronger, is described as entering the "phantom regime." The situation is like a ball rolling uphill without any apparent external force. It seems to violate the law of energy conservation [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This has led a growing number of theorists to investigate the possibility that dark energy is influenced by its ties to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait notes, while we have generally assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Next, we will examine concrete dark-interaction models that explain this apparent phantom behavior as a bookkeeping artifact and, in doing so, may also ease the long-standing Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. In 2005, a model proposed by physicist Justin Khoury and his collaborators explored a hypothetical question: could there be a form of dark energy whose energy density increased over time? They found that if dark energy and dark matter could affect one another, they could produce what looked like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0510628). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury and his colleagues Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden to construct a model of dark interactions based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD). QCD is a cornerstone theory of particle physics that describes the strong force. In their new model, both the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter change in concert, mirroring the complex dynamics seen in QCD but operating entirely within the dark sector [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[13]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf).

Another recent study, published in January 2025, imagines something similar. In this model, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy in a past cosmic era. Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the authors, explains that "Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," so easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion to accelerate [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[5]](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf).

The appearance of a phantom regime is essentially a result of bookkeeping. This argument, articulated by physicist David Andriot, is based on how we observationally define our cosmic components. We identify matter by its characteristic energy density, which scales inversely with the cube of the universe's scale factor (a⁻³). Any deviation from this scaling is, by definition, not considered matter [[6]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10410).

If dark matter's mass changes over time due to a coupling, this variation is not attributed to the matter component. Instead, it gets captured by the unknown dark energy component. This "effective" dark energy density now includes the extra variation from the matter coupling. As a result, its equation of state can dip below -1, creating the illusion of phantom energy even if the underlying physics is sound. As Andriot puts it, "Any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Cumrun Vafa, a physicist at Harvard University, agrees with this perspective. "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong," he said. "That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

An interaction between the dark sectors could also help resolve one of cosmology's most persistent problems: the Hubble tension. This issue centers on the Hubble constant (H₀), the current expansion rate of the universe. When we infer its value from the early universe via the cosmic microwave background (CMB), we get H₀ ≈ 67.4 km/s/Mpc. However, measurements from the late universe using Type Ia supernovae give a higher value of H₀ ≈ 73 km/s/Mpc [[5]](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf).

This 9% discrepancy, with a statistical significance over 5-sigma, has provoked heated debates about whether it's due to systematic errors or new physics. Coupled dark sector models offer a third option. The interaction adjusts the expansion history between the early and late universe epochs, reconciling the different measurements without needing to find errors or invent unrelated new particles. As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, their model "posits that in a world where dark energy and dark matter interact, what seemed to be a crisis caused by disparate expansion rates instead becomes something to be expected" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological models find a natural home and a common geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter interact, it could mean they share a common origin [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Ongoing work in string theory, which posits that our universe is made of vibrating strings, suggests a way these concepts might be connected. Building on this foundation, Vafa and collaborators proposed in 2019 and 2022 that dark matter and dark energy could be linked to a so-called "dark dimension" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293).

The motivation for this proposal stems from the cosmological constant problem. The calculated vacuum energy of space is 10¹²⁰ times larger than what we observe, one of the biggest challenges in modern physics [[8]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy). Vafa's team applied string theory's "distance conjecture," which requires a tower of light particles to appear when a parameter like dark energy is extremely small. For this to happen, one extra dimension must be much larger than the others [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

String theory suggests the existence of six or seven extra spatial dimensions. These are typically thought to be minuscule, close to the Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). However, the researchers proposed that one of these could be significantly larger. This "dark dimension" would be on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293). This is still tiny by everyday standards, but enormous compared to the others.

This framework provides a mechanism for the origin of dark matter. Gravitons, the theoretical particles that mediate gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire mass and become "dark gravitons." These particles would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would be felt in our familiar dimensions, fulfilling the role typically ascribed to dark matter [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This scenario establishes an inherent connection between dark energy and dark matter. According to Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago, "there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both components. The density of dark energy is tied to the volume of extra dimensions, and the mass of dark matter is determined by the radius of the dark dimension.

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya of Princeton and David Wu of Harvard, found that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[9]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03090). Their model, called the Fading Dark Sector model, predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will decrease over time. Furthermore, the rate at which dark energy changes is proportional to its energy density. "Because astrophysical measurements tell us that the energy density of dark energy is extraordinarily small, ‘it won’t change fast,’" Vafa said. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The model makes other testable predictions. For instance, when heavier dark gravitons decay, they impart a "kick velocity" to the lighter ones they produce. This could cause galaxies to be slightly less massive than standard models predict, a finding consistent with recent Kilo-Degree Survey data [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). The coupling might also alter gravitational wave signals from black hole mergers, creating subtle waveform changes detectable by future observatories [[10]](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12948v1).

A direct consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles. In a 2006 study, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden investigated a test for such a force. They theorized that if dark matter has a stronger self-attraction, a special "tidal tail" of stars and gas would form behind a smaller galaxy as it is gravitationally tugged by a larger one [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[12]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0606566).

Kesden and Kamionkowski looked for this effect and did not find it, which allowed them to set an upper limit on the possible strength of this extra force. The value predicted by Vafa’s team fell well within this observational bound. "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The fact that a prediction from string theory aligns with astrophysical evidence does not confirm these "stringy" models. However, any correspondence between theory and experiment is gratifying to researchers like Vafa. He has spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the purely conceptual world of string theory [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Not all physicists are convinced. Some, like Juan Maldacena of the Institute for Advanced Study, are skeptical about the underlying conjectures and believe the probability of finding these dimensions is low, though the search remains a worthwhile endeavor [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). Understanding dark energy and dark matter is a daunting problem. As Obied stated, it is beneficial to approach it from different angles, be it from observational cosmology, particle physics, or string theory. "This is how people should do science. I mean, it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
