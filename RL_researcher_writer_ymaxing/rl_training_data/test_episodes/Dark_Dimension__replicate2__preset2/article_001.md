# A Dark Dimension Could Link the Universe’s Greatest Unknowns

Our universe appears to be a dark place. According to current estimates, approximately 70% of the cosmos consists of dark energy, an unknown force pushing space to expand, and another 25% is dark matter, a mysterious material holding galaxies together. The remaining 5% is the ordinary, observable matter that makes up everything we can see. But dark energy and dark matter are not so much “dark” as they are invisible. They do not emit, reflect, nor absorb light, and have so far proven impossible to observe directly [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The concept of dark energy has a long history, starting with Einstein adding a "cosmological constant" to his theory of general relativity to achieve a static universe. He later dismissed it when the universe's expansion was discovered, only for the idea to be reclaimed decades later to explain the universe's *accelerated* expansion [[2]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time). The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM, assumes that dark energy (as a cosmological constant) and dark matter are separate, unrelated phenomena. However, recent astronomical observations are challenging this view, suggesting the two might be physically intertwined. In 2024 and 2025, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) collaboration found evidence that the strength of dark energy has not been constant. The data suggests it attained a maximum value about two billion years ago and has been weakening since. In an even earlier era, it may have grown stronger [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[3]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve).

This behavior, where dark energy grows stronger, is described as entering the "phantom regime." The situation is like a ball rolling uphill without any apparent external force. It seems to violate the law of energy conservation [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). This has led a growing number of theorists to investigate the possibility that dark energy is influenced by its ties to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait notes, while scientists have generally assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Next, we will examine concrete dark-interaction models that explain this apparent phantom behavior as a bookkeeping artifact and, in doing so, may also ease the long-standing Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter interact is not new. In 2005, a model proposed by physicist Justin Khoury and his collaborators explored a hypothetical question: could there be a form of dark energy whose energy density increased over time? They found that if dark energy and dark matter could affect one another, they could produce what looked like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[4]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0510628). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury and his colleagues Meng-Xiang Lin and Mark Trodden to construct a model of dark interactions based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a cornerstone theory of particle physics. In their new model, both the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter change in concert [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Another recent study, published in January 2025, imagines something similar. In this model, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy in a past cosmic era. Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the authors, explains that "Dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," so easing up on that brake would have caused the expansion to accelerate [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[5]](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf).

The appearance of a phantom regime is essentially a result of bookkeeping. This argument, articulated by physicist David Andriot, posits that observational cosmology identifies matter by its characteristic energy density, which scales inversely with the cube of the scale factor of the universe (a⁻³). Any deviation from this scaling, such as a change in dark matter's mass due to a coupling, is not attributed to matter itself. Instead, it is captured by the unknown dark energy component. This "effective" dark energy density therefore includes the extra variation from the matter coupling. As a result, its "effective" equation of state parameter, *w*, can dip below -1, creating the appearance of phantom energy even if the underlying physical components are perfectly well-behaved [[6]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10410). As Andriot puts it, "Any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Cumrun Vafa, a physicist at Harvard University, agrees with this perspective. "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong," he said. "That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This interaction between the dark sectors could also help resolve one of cosmology's most persistent problems: the Hubble tension. The issue centers on the Hubble constant (H₀), the current expansion rate of the universe. Its value can be inferred from observations of the early universe, specifically the cosmic microwave background (CMB), which gives a value of H₀ ≈ 67.4 km/s/Mpc. However, measurements from the late universe, using "standard candles" like Type Ia supernovae, yield a higher value of H₀ ≈ 73 km/s/Mpc. This discrepancy of about 9%, with a statistical significance exceeding 5-sigma, has led to intense debate about whether it stems from systematic errors or signals new physics [[5]](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/9lf2-33zf). As Teixeira and her co-authors wrote, their model "posits that in a world where dark energy and dark matter interact, what seemed to be a crisis caused by disparate expansion rates instead becomes something to be expected" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

These phenomenological models find a natural home and a common geometric origin within string theory, which we explore next through the dark-dimension proposal.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter interact, it could mean they share a common origin [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Ongoing work in string theory, which posits that our universe is made of vibrating strings, suggests a way these concepts might be connected. Building on this foundation, Vafa and collaborators proposed in 2019 and 2022 that dark matter and dark energy could be linked to a so-called "dark dimension" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293).

The motivation for this proposal stems from the cosmological constant problem, where the calculated vacuum energy of space is 10¹²⁰ times larger than observed [[8]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy). Vafa's team applied string theory's "distance conjecture," which requires a tower of light particles to appear when a parameter like dark energy is extremely small. For this to happen, one extra dimension must be much larger than the others [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

String theory suggests the existence of six or seven extra spatial dimensions. These are typically thought to be minuscule, close to the Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters). However, the researchers proposed that one of these—the dark dimension—could be significantly larger, on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293).

This framework provides a mechanism for the origin of dark matter. Gravitons, the theoretical particles that mediate gravity, could leak into this enlarged dark dimension. In doing so, they would acquire mass and become "dark gravitons." These particles would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would be felt in our familiar dimensions, fulfilling the role typically ascribed to dark matter [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This scenario establishes an inherent connection between dark energy and dark matter. According to Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago, "there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). Any change in the size of the dark dimension would simultaneously affect both components: the density of dark energy is tied to the volume of extra dimensions, and the mass of dark matter is determined by the radius of the dark dimension.

In a July 2025 paper, Obied and Vafa, along with Alek Bedroya of Princeton and David Wu of Harvard, found that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[9]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03090). Their model predicts that the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will decrease over time. Furthermore, the rate at which dark energy changes is proportional to its energy density. "Because astrophysical measurements tell us that the energy density of dark energy is extraordinarily small, ‘it won’t change fast,’" Vafa said. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Another prediction involves a "kick velocity" imparted to lighter dark gravitons when heavier ones decay. This could cause galaxies to be slightly less massive than standard models predict, a finding consistent with recent Kilo-Degree Survey data [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). A direct consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity. The coupling might also alter gravitational wave signals from black hole mergers, creating subtle waveform changes detectable by future observatories [[10]](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12948v1). This offers a testable prediction. In a 2006 study, physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden investigated one such test. They theorized that if dark matter has a stronger gravitational attraction to other dark matter than to ordinary matter, a special kind of "tidal tail" would form behind a smaller galaxy as it is tugged on by a larger one [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[12]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0606566).

Kesden and Kamionkowski looked for this effect and did not find it, which allowed them to set an upper limit on the possible strength of this extra force. The value predicted by Vafa’s team fell well within this observational bound. "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The fact that a prediction from string theory aligns with astrophysical evidence does not confirm these "stringy" models. However, any correspondence between theory and experiment is gratifying to researchers like Vafa, who has spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the purely conceptual realm of string theory [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Not all physicists are convinced. Some, like Juan Maldacena of the Institute for Advanced Study, are skeptical about the underlying conjectures and believe the probability of finding these dimensions is low, though the search remains a worthwhile endeavor [[11]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201). Understanding dark energy and dark matter is a daunting problem. As Obied stated, it is beneficial to approach it from different angles, be it from observational cosmology, particle physics, or string theory. "This is how people should do science. I mean, it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
