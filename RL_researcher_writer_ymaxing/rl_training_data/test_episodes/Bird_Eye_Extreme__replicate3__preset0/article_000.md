# Sight Without Oxygen: The Biological Paradox of the Bird Retina

In human medicine, a blood clot in the retinal artery is a catastrophic event. Known as retinal vascular occlusion, it starves the eye's light-sensing tissue of oxygen, causing rapid and irreversible vision loss. Yet, birds operate with retinas that are almost entirely avascular—they have no internal blood vessels. Despite this, a hawk can spot a mouse from hundreds of feet in the air, achieving a level of visual acuity that far surpasses our own.

This presents a long-standing biological paradox. The retina is one of the most energy-hungry tissues in the animal kingdom, consuming oxygen and glucose at two to three times the rate of the brain [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). For centuries, scientists assumed birds must possess some undiscovered mechanism for acquiring oxygen, because based on everything we know about physiology, their retinas simply should not be able to function [[2]](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/translational-medicine/how-bird-retinas-function-without-oxygen-may-inform-future-stroke-therapies). As evolutionary physiologist Christian Damsgaard of Aarhus University puts it, "It was a complete paradox" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

A recent study finally resolves this mystery, and the answer is not a novel oxygen-delivery system. Instead, the inner layers of the bird retina exist in a permanent state of oxygen deprivation, or chronic anoxia. They survive by relying on anaerobic glycolysis, a much less efficient method of energy production [[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09978-w). This finding documents an evolutionary extreme, a case of highly active tissue that thrives without oxygen, and it may hold valuable lessons for treating human conditions caused by ischemia, such as stroke [[4]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036).

Before we can appreciate how birds solved this paradox, we need to revisit how oxygen came to dominate complex life and why its absence is normally catastrophic.

## Oxygenated Life: The Great Oxidation Event and Metabolic Trade-offs

Around 3.4 billion years ago, cyanobacteria developed photosynthesis and began pumping oxygen into the atmosphere. This Great Oxidation Event fundamentally reshaped life on Earth by unlocking a far more efficient way to produce energy [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

The biochemical trade-off is stark. Anaerobic glycolysis, the process of breaking down glucose without oxygen, yields a mere two molecules of ATP—life's energy currency. In contrast, aerobic respiration, which uses oxygen, can produce up to 32 molecules of ATP from a single glucose molecule [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). This fifteen-fold increase in efficiency was a transformative advantage, allowing for the evolution of complex, multicellular organisms. As molecular physiologist Gary Lewin of the Max Delbrück Center in Berlin states, “We’ve been hooked on 20% [atmospheric] oxygen for millions of years” [[1]](httpshttps://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/BirdFlying-crJean-PaulWettstein-scaled.webp> 
Image 2: Birds, such as this alpine chough (in the crow family), use their exceptional vision to hunt, forage, and migrate. (Image by Jean-Paul Wettstein from [Quanta Magazine [1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513))

This dependency makes most animals extremely vulnerable to oxygen deprivation. Humans can suffer irreversible brain damage after just a few minutes without oxygen. Other species show greater tolerance. The naked mole rat, a subterranean rodent, can survive for 18 minutes in an anoxic environment by switching to fructose-driven glycolysis [[5]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aab3896). At the far end of the spectrum, some freshwater turtles can survive for months at the bottom of frozen lakes with no oxygen at all [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). For humans, however, a steady supply of oxygen is non-negotiable, especially for high-demand tissues like the brain and retina, where energy-intensive processes shut down almost immediately in its absence.

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/NakedMoleRats-crJavierAbalos-scaled.webp>

(Image by Javier Ábalos from [Quanta Magazine [1]](httpshttps://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513))

With this metabolic context established, we can now examine the mysterious vascular structure that enables birds to push the vertebrate eye to an extreme never seen in other lineages.

## A Mysterious Structure: The Pecten Oculi and Chronic Anoxia

When Christian Damsgaard first learned that bird retinas lack blood vessels, he was puzzled. He reviewed the existing research, which all pointed to a strange structure called the pecten oculi. First described in the 17th century, the pecten is a comb-like, highly vascularized organ that protrudes into the eye. It had generated over 30 different hypotheses about its function, most centered on oxygen delivery, but none were based on direct physiological measurements [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). "Nobody had really done direct physiological measurements on this structure," Damsgaard noted. "That’s where we came in" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

<https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Bird_Retina-Fig1-crMarkBelan_Desktopv1.svg> 
Image 4: A diagram showing the location of the pecten oculi inside the bird eye. (Image by Mark Belan/Quanta Magazine from [Quanta Magazine [1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513))

Using microsensors to measure oxygen levels in the eyes of zebra finches, pigeons, and chickens, Damsgaard's team made a striking discovery. The inner retina, which completely lacks blood vessels, had no oxygen at all. The outer retina, which is near the blood vessels of the choroid, did have oxygen. "Half of the retina lives in a chronic state of anoxia, where there’s no oxygen present at all," Damsgaard said [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). This single finding overturned centuries of assumptions.

To understand how the tissue survived, the researchers used spatial transcriptomics to map gene activity. The results were clear: genes for aerobic respiration were active only in the oxygenated outer retina, while genes for anaerobic glycolysis were exclusively active in the anoxic inner retina [[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09978-w). This inefficient process demanded a massive amount of fuel. The team found that the inner retina's glucose demand was 2.5 times higher than other parts of the bird's brain [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[6]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen).

This led them back to the pecten oculi. Their data showed that genes for glucose transporters were highly active there, suggesting the structure's true function: it acts as a metabolic gateway, pumping huge amounts of glucose into the retina to fuel anaerobic glycolysis [[7]](https://uol.de/en/news/article/birds-retinas-function-without-oxygen). Anaerobic glycolysis produces lactic acid as a toxic byproduct. The team also found that genes for lactic acid transporters were highly active in the pecten, indicating it simultaneously removes this waste [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[8]](https://www.sdu.dk/en/om-sdu/fakulteterne/naturvidenskab/nyheder-2026/bird-retina).

The conclusion was extraordinary. Roughly half of the bird retina exists in a state of permanent anoxia, a metabolic strategy never before documented in any vertebrate tissue. Thomas Baden, a neuroscientist at the University of Sussex, called the finding surprising, noting that the oxygen level "really gets properly down to zero" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513). While cancer cells also use anaerobic glycolysis (a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect), and our muscles do so temporarily during intense exercise, the bird retina is the first known example of a vertebrate tissue that survives this way for a lifetime in a healthy state [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

Having established how the avian retina operates, we can now trace when and why this radical solution evolved and what it teaches us about selective pressures and future applications.

## Eyes Like a Hawk: Evolutionary Origins, Selective Pressures, and Implications

The bird’s retina and its no-oxygen power system are so unusual that they naturally raise questions about how they could have evolved. Evolution often acts not as an engineer with a master plan, but as a tinkerer, repurposing existing parts for new functions. In a 1977 essay, biologist François Jacob argued that evolution "does not produce novelties from scratch. It works on what already exists" [[9]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.860134). This is precisely what appears to have happened with the vertebrate eye. Instead of inventing a new oxygen-delivery system, evolution tinkered with the existing blueprint.

Researchers pinpoint the origin of this adaptation to the theropod dinosaur lineage, after it split from crocodilians. By comparing bird retinas to those of reptiles like turtles and caimans, which have normal oxygen levels, the team concluded that the oxygen-free tissue likely evolved around the same time the retina began to thicken [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[10]](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2025.12.028). The pecten oculi, or a similar precursor structure, was repurposed from a simple vascular extension into a sophisticated metabolic support system [[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09978-w).

The selective pressures driving this change were likely intense. High-acuity vision is critical for birds' survival, enabling them to hunt, forage, and navigate during long-distance migrations, sometimes at high altitudes where oxygen is scarce [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[8]](https://www.sdu.dk/en/om-sdu/fakulteterne/naturvidenskab/nyheder-2026/bird-retina). Blood vessels scatter light, which would have limited the density of photoreceptors and neurons, thereby reducing visual resolution [[11]](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0008992). By eliminating the need for internal blood vessels, evolution allowed for a thicker, more densely packed retina, which directly translates to sharper vision [[3]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09978-w).

Whether this avascular design was a direct adaptation for better vision or an evolutionary coincidence that was later co-opted for high-altitude flight remains an open question. Damsgaard speculates that the system first evolved in theropod dinosaurs for tracking prey and was later exapted for migration when birds took to the skies [[1]](httpshttps://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513), [[6]](https://www.thetransmitter.org/vision/inner-retina-of-birds-powers-sight-sans-oxygen). However, others urge caution, noting that the current research has not yet included migratory species, so universal claims about its function in high-altitude flight are still speculative [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).

Beyond its evolutionary significance, this discovery has direct biomedical relevance. In conditions like stroke, human tissues are damaged by both a lack of oxygen and the accumulation of metabolic waste [[4]](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1113036). By studying how birds and other anoxia-tolerant animals like the naked mole rat cope with these challenges, scientists can gain new insights into how to protect tissues from hypoxic damage. "Maybe we can get inspiration for how nature solved these problems by millions of years of natural selection," says Damsgaard. "There’s so much to be learned from these animals that are able to do something that we cannot do" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/how-the-bird-eye-was-pushed-to-an-evolutionary-extreme-20260513).
