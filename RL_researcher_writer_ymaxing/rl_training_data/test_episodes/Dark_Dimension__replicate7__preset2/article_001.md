# The Dark Dimension: A Unified Theory for the Universe's Greatest Unknowns

According to our current understanding, the universe is a dark place. Approximately 70% of it is dark energy, an unknown force driving cosmic expansion, and another 25% is dark matter, a mysterious substance holding galaxies together. The remaining 5% is the ordinary matter we can see and interact with. But "dark" is a bit of a misnomer; it really means invisible. These components do not emit, reflect, or absorb light, and they have so far proven impossible to observe directly.

The concept of dark energy has a long history. It began with Einstein, who added a "cosmological constant" to his theory of general relativity to ensure a static universe. He later dismissed it when the universe was found to be expanding, but the idea was reclaimed in the late 1990s to explain the discovery that this expansion is accelerating [[1]](https://www.ukri.org/news/desi-results-suggest-dark-energy-may-evolve-over-time).

The standard model of cosmology, Lambda-CDM, treats dark energy (as a cosmological constant, Λ) and dark matter as separate, unrelated phenomena. However, recent astronomical observations are challenging this assumption, suggesting they might be physically intertwined. In 2024 and 2025, results from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) provided the most precise measurements of the universe's expansion history to date. Their analysis indicated that the strength of dark energy has not been constant, suggesting it peaked about 2 billion years ago, has been weakening ever since, and may have even grown stronger in an earlier cosmic era [[3]](https://news.fnal.gov/2025/03/new-desi-results-strengthen-hints-that-dark-energy-may-evolve), [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

This last part is particularly puzzling. A dark energy that grows stronger enters what researchers call the "phantom regime." This behavior is deeply counterintuitive, akin to a ball spontaneously gathering speed as it rolls uphill, seemingly creating energy from nothing. Such an observation would appear to violate the fundamental law of energy conservation unless an unseen interaction is at play, where energy is being transferred from another source.

This has led a growing number of theorists to investigate the possibility that dark energy is influenced by its ties to dark matter. As particle physicist Tim Tait notes, while scientists have often assumed they "don’t have anything to do with each other, you can imagine a case where one influences the other. And it would not be surprising if [they] were manifestations of a kind of unified theory of the dark universe" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). We next examine concrete dark-interaction models that turn the phantom appearance into a bookkeeping artifact and simultaneously ease the Hubble tension.

## Dark Interactions

The idea that dark energy and dark matter might interact is not new. Back in 2005, a model by Justin Khoury and his collaborators posed a hypothetical question: could there be a form of dark energy whose energy density increased over time? They found that if dark energy and dark matter could transfer energy between one another, they could produce what looked like phantom behavior without actually violating energy conservation [[9]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0510628). "It is the most natural, simplest way of achieving this," Khoury said [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The model proposed that this transfer could be mediated by a direct coupling between the two sectors. This would allow dark matter's energy density, which decreases as the universe expands, to feed into the dark energy component. This would cause the dark energy density to increase, or decrease more slowly than expected, mimicking phantom energy without requiring any exotic physics that violates conservation laws.

Two decades later, the DESI findings spurred Khoury, Meng-Xiang Lin, and Mark Trodden to construct a new model based on a dark-sector analogue of quantum chromodynamics (QCD), a fundamental theory in particle physics. In this model, the energy density of dark energy and the mass of dark matter change in concert. Their framework draws an analogy to the strong nuclear force, which binds quarks into protons and neutrons. In their "dark QCD" model, there are dark quarks and dark gluons. As the universe expands and cools, these particles undergo a phase transition, releasing latent energy that gets converted into dark energy, while the mass of the dark matter particles also changes. This provides a concrete particle physics mechanism for the coupled evolution [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[5]](https://indico.global/event/14705/contributions/155116/attachments/72358/141320/PASCOS2026.pptx%20(1).pdf).

Another study, published in January 2025, imagines something similar. According to this model, dark matter could have transferred a small fraction of its energy to dark energy in the past. Elsa Teixeira, a cosmologist at the University of Montpellier and one of the study's authors, explains that "dark matter is the main brake on [the universe’s] expansion," so easing up on that brake would cause the expansion to accelerate. In their scenario, this energy transfer would have been most prominent in the more recent cosmic past. By reducing the energy density of dark matter, the universe's primary gravitational "brake" on expansion is slightly released. This allows the repulsive effect of dark energy to dominate more strongly, leading to the observed late-time acceleration [[2]](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2524499122), [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[10]](https://journals.aps.org/prd/pdf/10.1103/9lf2-33zf).

The appearance of phantom behavior, then, is essentially a result of bookkeeping. David Andriot, a physicist at CNRS, the French National Center for Scientific Research, argues that "any change or evolution of the mass of dark matter has been put into the box of dark energy" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[11]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10410). This view is shared by Cumrun Vafa, a physicist at Harvard University. "The notion that you can compute dark energy independently of dark matter is wrong," he stated. "That assumption, often made by cosmologists and also followed by the DESI team, led to the physically unacceptable phantom behavior" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

Beyond explaining the phantom regime, an interacting dark sector could also help resolve one of cosmology's most persistent problems: the Hubble tension. This tension refers to the approximately 9% discrepancy between measurements of the universe's current expansion rate based on the early universe (the cosmic microwave background) and the more recent universe (supernovas). According to the standard model, these values should be the same. This discrepancy "has provoked heated debates in the cosmology community about whether this difference could be due to systematic errors or whether it is a signal of new physics," Teixeira and her co-authors wrote. Their model suggests that in a universe where dark energy and dark matter interact, this tension is not a crisis but an expected outcome [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

An interacting dark sector can resolve this tension by altering the universe's expansion rate at different times. The interaction could be negligible in the early universe, leaving the physics that produced the cosmic microwave background (CMB) largely unchanged. However, the interaction could become active later on, modifying the expansion history during the era of supernovas. This would mean that extrapolating the early-universe expansion rate to the present day would naturally yield a different value than measuring it directly, resolving the tension as a feature of the model, not an error in the data. These interaction models provide a compelling phenomenological framework, but they find an even more natural home within string theory, which offers a common geometric origin for both dark components.

## A Dark Dimension

If dark energy and dark matter interact, it could mean they have a common origin. Ongoing work in string theory, which posits that the universe is made of vibrating strings, suggests how this might be possible. Building on the idea that dark energy can vary, Vafa and collaborators proposed in 2019 and 2022 that dark matter particle masses might also change over time. This led them to connect both dark matter and dark energy to a "dark dimension" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[12]](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.08261), [[13]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12293).

The motivation stems from the cosmological constant problem, where theory predicts a vacuum energy 10¹²⁰ times larger than observed. Vafa's "distance conjecture" in string theory suggests such an extreme value must be accompanied by a tower of light particles, implying a new large dimension [[6]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy), [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

String theory requires the existence of six or seven extra spatial dimensions. While these are typically thought to be incredibly small—around the Planck scale (10⁻³⁵ meters)—the researchers proposed that one of these, the dark dimension, could be much larger. They estimated its size to be on the order of a micron (10⁻⁶ meters) [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[6]](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/a-dark-dimension-could-help-explain-the-origin-of-dark-energy).

In this scenario, gravitons, the theoretical particles that mediate gravity, could leak into this enlarged dimension. By doing so, they would acquire mass and become "dark gravitons." These massive particles would reside in the dark dimension, but their gravitational effects would be felt in our familiar dimensions. This allows them to play the role normally ascribed to dark matter. This mechanism also offers an explanation for why gravity is so much weaker than the other fundamental forces. With a large extra dimension for gravity to "leak" into, its strength becomes diluted in our four-dimensional world [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

In this framework, "there is a very natural coupling between dark energy and dark matter," said Georges Obied, a physicist at the University of Chicago. Any change in the size of the dark dimension would affect both dark energy (through its effect on the vacuum energy) and dark matter (by changing the mass of the dark gravitons) [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). A paper from July 2025 by Obied, Vafa, Alek Bedroya, and David Wu found that this scenario was consistent with the DESI data. Their model predicts that both the strength of dark energy and the mass of dark matter will decrease over time, with the rate of change for dark energy being proportional to its energy density [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[14]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03090).

Since dark energy's density is extraordinarily small, "it won’t change fast," Vafa explained. "It’s not surprising that we didn’t see it until now. We had to wait the entire age of the universe to detect something that small" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

A direct consequence of this coupling is the emergence of a new, long-range force between dark matter particles, separate from gravity. The good news, Obied said, is that "there could be astrophysical ways of testing this" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622). In fact, such a test was already performed in 2006. Physicists Marc Kamionkowski and Michael Kesden theorized that if dark matter has a stronger attraction to itself than to ordinary matter, it would affect how galaxies are tidally disrupted. Specifically, it would produce a distinctive "tidal tail." This is an extended stream of stars, gas, and dust behind one of the galaxies. They looked for this effect and did not find it, which allowed them to set an upper limit on the strength of this extra force [[15]](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0606566).

The value predicted by Vafa’s team's dark dimension model fell well within this observational bound. "It is interesting that we are now finding connections between that fairly abstract work and observational and experimental work," Kamionkowski remarked [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).

The model makes other testable predictions. The decay of heavier dark gravitons into lighter ones would impart a "kick velocity" to the resulting particles. This could affect galaxy formation, making galaxies slightly less massive than the standard model predicts. This is a signature that future surveys like the Euclid space telescope can search for. Furthermore, physicists are planning tabletop experiments to look for deviations from Newton's inverse-square law of gravity at the micron scale. If gravity is leaking into a dark dimension of this size, its force should appear slightly different over these very short distances, providing a direct test of the theory's core geometric assumption. Future gravitational wave observatories might also detect signals modified by dark dimension couplings [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201), [[8]](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.12948v1).

The fact that a prediction from string theory aligns with astrophysical evidence does not confirm these models, and some physicists remain skeptical. They caution that the underlying conjectures are ambitious and lack direct proof. Still, any correspondence between theory and experiment is gratifying to Vafa, who has spent decades trying to generate testable predictions from the purely conceptual realm of string theory [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622), [[7]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/in-a-dark-dimension-physicists-search-for-missing-matter-20240201).

## Conclusion

The recent hints of a time-varying dark energy from DESI have invigorated the search for physics beyond the standard cosmological model. The possibility that dark energy and dark matter are not separate entities but are instead coupled components of a unified dark sector offers a compelling explanation for these observations. This approach elegantly resolves the apparent phantom behavior as a bookkeeping effect and provides a natural mechanism to alleviate the Hubble tension.

Models rooted in string theory, such as the dark dimension scenario, provide a deeper, geometric origin for this coupling. They make specific, testable predictions that are now being scrutinized by a new generation of experiments. This convergence of observational cosmology, particle physics, and fundamental theory highlights a powerful, interdisciplinary approach to tackling the universe's greatest mysteries. As Georges Obied notes, "it’s the job of theoretical physicists to explore everything that’s possible, to get all the possibilities on the table. And eventually, the data will help us decide" [[4]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/a-dark-dimension-could-link-two-of-the-universes-great-unknowns-20260622).
