# The 2025 Playbook for Reasoning LLMs: A Deep Dive into DeepSeek-R1

Reasoning models are the key LLM specialization trend for 2025. This evolution extends beyond the patterns we already know, such as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and domain-specific fine-tuning. Instead of just injecting knowledge, reasoning models aim to develop robust, multi-step logical capabilities.

Having set the context and roadmap, we now establish a working definition of "reasoning model" that you can use to evaluate future systems and research.

## How do we define "reasoning model"?

A reasoning model is one that generates multi-step intermediate thinking to solve complex queries. This process can involve explicit token traces that we can read or hidden internal iterations that happen within the model. This stands in contrast to the direct factual recall or single-pass pattern matching that is sufficient for simpler prompts. For example, asking "What is the capital of France?" requires a simple fact retrieval. But a question like "If I have 3 apples and eat one, then buy twice the remaining amount, how many do I have?" requires a sequence of steps to solve.

(Note that many LLMs who have not been specifically developed for reasoning tasks can also provide intermediate reasoning steps in their answers. It is important to recognize that reasoning exists on a spectrum. All modern LLMs exhibit some basic reasoning, which can be improved with techniques like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. However, specialized reasoning models are engineered to achieve exceptional performance on difficult benchmarks, such as math olympiad problems, formal proofs, or novel puzzles.

The intermediate steps in these models can manifest in two primary ways. The first is through visible thought traces, which are the step-by-step outputs that a user can read and follow. The second is through invisible internal iterations, like those rumored to be in OpenAI's o1 model, where the model allocates more computation time at inference ("test-time compute") without necessarily showing every step to the user. This distinction is crucial because it separates the user-facing explanation from the internal problem-solving process.

This evolution from visible to invisible reasoning is a direct result of the success of earlier techniques. The concept began with explicit Chain-of-Thought prompting, where users instructed the model to "think step by step." Researchers found this so effective that they worked to automate it, leading to the development of test-time scaling models. These models can initiate and manage their own internal thought processes without explicit prompting, representing a significant step in AI's ability to handle complex reasoning autonomously [[21]](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/glossary/cot-prompting).

With a clear definition established, we can now examine the practical question of when deploying these more expensive reasoning models actually makes sense for an AI engineer.

## When should we use reasoning models?

Before we dive into the technical details, it is important to know when reasoning models are truly needed. They deliver the highest returns on tasks that require decomposition or self-correction, such as puzzles, advanced mathematics, and competitive coding. However, for tasks like summarization, simple factual question-answering, or creative writing, where direct generation is sufficient, they are often overkill.

The practical downsides are significant. The verbose intermediate steps lead to higher latency and increased token costs. This verbosity can also frustrate users in conversational settings who are looking for a quick, direct answer. Furthermore, there is a risk of "overthinking," where the model might invent unnecessary complications for a straightforward problem that a base model would solve correctly in a single pass. This is not just a hypothetical risk. Recent research has systematically documented this "overthinking" phenomenon, showing that reasoning performance often follows an inverted U-shaped curve with respect to the length of the thought process [[22]](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10739v1). After a certain point, more thinking leads to diminishing returns and can even be harmful, causing a model to second-guess and "flip" a correct answer to an incorrect one, especially on simpler problems.

## A brief look at the DeepSeek training pipeline

The DeepSeek-R1 series offers a transparent look into how reasoning capabilities can be cultivated. The series includes three main variants, each built upon the others [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

Image 5: Development process of DeepSeeks three different reasoning models that are discussed in the DeepSeek R1 technical report. (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!z-dr!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdb19df56-c5bf-4a0c-aafb-4629a39b13f5_1542x1166.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!z-dr!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdb19df56-c5bf-4a0c-aafb-4629a39b13f5_1542x1166.png))

First is **DeepSeek-R1-Zero**, a model produced via a "cold-start" pure Reinforcement Learning (RL) process directly from the DeepSeek-V3 base model. This approach is unconventional because it skips the standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) stage that usually precedes RL. The implication is that the model must discover reasoning pathways on its own, which requires strong, verifiable reward signals to guide it.

Next is **DeepSeek-R1**, the flagship reasoning model. It refines R1-Zero by adding iterative stages of SFT and further RL training. This hybrid approach aims to make the model's reasoning more stable, human-readable, and performant.

Finally, there are the **DeepSeek-R1-Distill** models. These are smaller models that inherit the capabilities of the larger R1 model without undergoing the full, computationally expensive RL process. It is important to note that this is not classical, logit-based knowledge distillation. Instead, it is a form of SFT where smaller models are trained on the high-quality, step-by-step reasoning outputs generated by the large R1 model [[2]](https://redwerk.com/blog/what-is-model-distillation), [[3]](https://medium.com/data-science-in-your-pocket/what-are-deepseek-r1-distilled-models-329629968d5d).

The DeepSeek pipeline incorporates all four main techniques for building reasoning models, which we will now examine in depth. This allows for a direct comparison of their mechanisms, the phenomena that emerge, and their empirical outcomes.

## The 4 main ways to build and improve reasoning models

The current landscape of techniques to enhance LLM reasoning and build specialized models is rich and varied. While the exact workings of frontier models like OpenAI's o1 and o3 remain undisclosed, they are rumored to combine both inference-time and training-time techniques. Here, we will outline the four key approaches, using the DeepSeek-R1 project as a running example.

### 1) Inference-time scaling

Inference-time scaling refers to the practice of dedicating more computational resources during the inference or "test-time" phase to improve a model's output. This is analogous to giving a human extra time to think through a difficult problem. The idea is that with more compute, a model can explore more possibilities, refine its initial thoughts, and ultimately arrive at a better solution. This concept is closely tied to "test-time compute scaling laws," which suggest that performance can improve as inference-time compute increases [[4]](https://magazine.sebastianraschka.com/p/state-of-llm-reasoning-and-inference-scaling).

Several methods fall under this category. **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting** is a foundational technique where the model is prompted to "think step by step," generating an explicit reasoning trace before giving a final answer. This simple instruction can dramatically improve performance on complex tasks [[5]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916).

Image 6: An example of classic CoT prompting from the 2022 Large Language Models are Zero-Shot Reasoners paper [[5]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.11916). (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!VFAa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F523eee5e-afb6-4019-a11b-e0a291d2c286_1600x419.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!VFAa!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F523eee5e-afb6-4019-a11b-e0a291d2c286_1600x419.png))

More advanced methods involve search algorithms. **Majority voting**, or best-of-N sampling, involves generating multiple independent answers and selecting the most frequent one. **Beam search** and **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)** are more sophisticated techniques that explore a tree of possible reasoning paths, pruning unpromising branches and focusing compute on more likely solutions. These search methods often rely on a **Process Reward Model (PRM)**, which is a verifier trained to score the correctness of each intermediate step in a solution, providing a much finer-grained signal than just evaluating the final answer [[6]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314).

Image 7: Different search-based methods rely on a process-reward-based model to select the best answer. Annotated figure from the LLM Test-Time Compute paper [[6]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.03314). (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!YGJO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5cb10e5a-738b-4c9e-ba65-5850d4793706_1600x919.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!YGJO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5cb10e5a-738b-4c9e-ba65-5850d4793706_1600x919.png))

Interestingly, the DeepSeek-R1 technical report categorizes both PRMs and MCTS under "unsuccessful attempts" [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948). They argue that while these methods can be useful for re-ranking a set of generated answers, the computational overhead they introduce during large-scale RL training is not justified by the limited performance gains. This suggests that DeepSeek prioritized training-time optimization over inference-time search. However, this does not mean they completely disregard inference-time scaling. It is plausible that such techniques are still applied at the application layer, even if they were not central to the core training pipeline.

The high cost of OpenAI's o1 models compared to models like GPT-4o might be explained by their reliance on inference-time scaling. Models like o1 are designed to "think" longer before answering, which requires more computational resources per query [[7]](https://neoteric.eu/blog/gpt-o1-vs-gpt-4o-comparison), [[8]](https://www.uctoday.com/unified-communications/chatgpt-4o-vs-o1-which-openai-model-is-best). This extra computation time is likely used for internal search, verification, or other refinement processes, leading to higher accuracy on complex tasks but also higher operational costs [[9]](https://francpetracci.medium.com/openai-gpt-o1-api-pricing-a-comprehensive-guide-b93fdaed217c).

This trade-off is not just about cost. The assumption that more thinking always leads to better answers is flawed. A systematic study of test-time compute scaling found that beyond a certain point, the marginal utility of additional reasoning tokens diminishes and eventually becomes negative [[22]](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.10739v1). This "overthinking" causes models to abandon correct initial answers, a phenomenon measured by "negative flips" (correct → incorrect). This effect is more pronounced on easier problems, suggesting that a uniform, high compute budget for all tasks is a suboptimal strategy.

### 2) Pure reinforcement learning (RL)

The second approach, exemplified by DeepSeek-R1-Zero, is to use pure reinforcement learning directly on a base model without any preliminary supervised fine-tuning. This "cold-start" method is a significant departure from typical RLHF pipelines, which first align a model with human-like responses through SFT before optimizing for preferences with RL.

Image 8: The development process of DeepSeek-R1-Zero model. (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_9Z-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa5bb6ecc-7e46-45fe-abff-1eb02e6b0e3a_1556x1162.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!_9Z-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fa5bb6ecc-7e46-45fe-abff-1eb02e6b0e3a_1556x1162.png))

In this paradigm, the reward signal is not derived from a reward model trained on human preferences. Instead, it comes from rule-based verifiers that provide objective feedback. The DeepSeek-R1 paper details two primary types of rewards used for R1-Zero [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948), [[10]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09422-z):
1.  **Accuracy rewards:** These are binary signals that indicate whether the model's final answer is correct. For math problems, this is a simple string match against the ground truth. For coding tasks, it is a pass/fail from a compiler running against predefined test cases.
2.  **Format rewards:** These rewards incentivize the model to follow a specific output structure, such as enclosing its reasoning process within `<think>` and `</think>` tags. This ensures the model's output is parsable and its thought process is explicit.

The algorithm DeepSeek used to apply these rewards is known as Group-based Reward Policy Optimization (GRPO). Unlike the more common Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), GRPO is a critic-less algorithm. It works by generating multiple responses for the same prompt, grouping them, and then normalizing the rewards based on the mean and standard deviation within that group. This approach is memory-efficient and computationally simple, making it a natural fit for verifiable tasks where multiple samples can be generated per prompt [[23]](https://cameronrwolfe.substack.com/p/grpo).

A key phenomenon observed during this pure RL training is the "Aha moment" [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948), [[11]](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/a9GR7m4nyBsqjjL8d/deepseek-r1-for-beginners). This is a point in training where the model's behavior shifts dramatically. It starts to spontaneously generate long, detailed reasoning traces, including self-reflection, verification of its own steps, and exploration of alternative approaches. This emergent behavior, which was not explicitly taught, demonstrates that the model is learning to reason more deeply to maximize its accuracy reward.

Image 9: A figure from the DeepSeek R1 technical report showing the emergence of the "Aha" moment [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948). (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Prn2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F30f8e37b-ba60-49d2-a95e-9c06b2033ee4_1600x1019.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Prn2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F30f8e37b-ba60-49d2-a95e-9c06b2033ee4_1600x1019.png))

The success of R1-Zero is a milestone. It is the first clear demonstration that a model can develop sophisticated reasoning capabilities through pure RL, driven only by a verifiable outcome, without being pre-conditioned on human-written reasoning examples.

### 3) Supervised finetuning and reinforcement learning (SFT + RL)

The third approach is a hybrid model that combines the strengths of both SFT and RL. This is the method used to create the flagship DeepSeek-R1 model, which builds upon the foundation laid by R1-Zero. The goal of this multi-stage pipeline is to refine the raw reasoning abilities of R1-Zero, making them more stable, human-readable, and performant across a wider range of tasks [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

Image 10: The development process of DeepSeek-R1 model. (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!19pK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdf7f99f0-d154-49e5-b60a-4d148e0a61be_1548x1154.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!19pK!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fdf7f99f0-d154-49e5-b60a-4d148e0a61be_1548x1154.png))

The process begins with generating a "cold-start" dataset. This involves using early versions of the R1-Zero model to generate detailed reasoning traces for a few thousand tasks. These traces are then filtered for correctness and refined by human annotators to ensure they are clear and follow a consistent, conversational style. The base model is then fine-tuned on this small, high-quality dataset. This initial SFT stage gives the model a solid foundation in generating coherent, human-friendly reasoning before it enters the more exploratory RL phase [[11]](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/a9GR7m4nyBsqjjL8d/deepseek-r1-for-beginners).

Next, the model undergoes large-scale RL, similar to the R1-Zero training. However, in addition to the accuracy and format rewards, a **language consistency reward** is introduced. This reward penalizes the model for mixing languages (e.g., English and Chinese) within a single response. While the paper notes that this slightly degrades raw accuracy on some tasks, it significantly improves readability and aligns the model's output with human preferences [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948), [[12]](https://transitions.substack.com/p/the-laymans-introduction-to-deepseek).

The pipeline then iterates through further stages of SFT and RL. The model's own high-quality outputs are used to create new, larger SFT datasets (a technique known as rejection sampling), which are then used for another round of fine-tuning. This is followed by a final RL stage that mixes reasoning tasks with general instruction-following and preference data, using model-based rewards to improve helpfulness and harmlessness. This staged refinement process allows the model to absorb the best of its RL-discovered strategies into a more stable, supervised format, while also broadening its capabilities beyond pure reasoning. The result is a model that achieves state-of-the-art performance on reasoning benchmarks, often comparable to or exceeding that of closed models like OpenAI's o1 [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

### 4) Pure supervised finetuning (SFT) and distillation

To quickly review, we have covered three primary methods for developing reasoning models. **Inference-time scaling** enhances performance by allocating more computational resources during inference, without altering the model's weights. **Pure RL** cultivates reasoning abilities from a base model using verifiable reward signals, leading to the emergence of complex behaviors. The **SFT+RL hybrid** approach refines these emergent abilities through iterative stages of supervised learning and reinforcement learning, resulting in more stable and human-aligned models.

The fourth major approach is to use pure SFT, most notably in the form of distillation. This is the method DeepSeek used to create its R1-Distill series of smaller, more accessible reasoning models. The process involves using the large, powerful DeepSeek-R1 model to generate a massive dataset of high-quality reasoning traces—around 800,000 examples—and then using this dataset to fine-tune smaller, open-weight base models like Qwen and Llama [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

Image 12: The development process of DeepSeek-R1-Distill models. (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xUjE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7db7c46b-fe67-49f4-9f65-b0e7b7e5ac08_1444x1174.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!xUjE!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7db7c46b-fe67-49f4-9f65-b0e7b7e5ac08_1444x1174.png))

It is crucial to understand that this is not traditional knowledge distillation, where a smaller "student" model learns to mimic the output probabilities (logits) of a larger "teacher" model. Instead, this is a form of data distillation or SFT, where the student learns to replicate the teacher's final text output, including the entire chain-of-thought [[2]](https://redwerk.com/blog/what-is-model-distillation), [[13]](https://www.bentoml.com/blog/the-complete-guide-to-deepseek-models-from-v3-to-r1-and-beyond). The student model is not trained with RL; it simply learns to imitate the reasoning patterns discovered by the teacher.

DeepSeek developed these distilled models for two key reasons. First, to make powerful reasoning capabilities more accessible. The massive 671B parameter R1 model is computationally expensive to run, and the distilled models offer a more efficient alternative for users with limited resources. Second, to provide a valuable resource for the research community to study the mechanisms of long-CoT reasoning models [[3]](https://medium.com/data-science-in-your-pocket/what-are-deepseek-r1-distilled-models-329629968d5d). As shown in the benchmarks, even the 1.5B distilled model can surpass strong, non-reasoning baselines on mathematical tasks.

Image 13: Benchmark comparison of distilled versus non-distilled models. Annotated figure from the DeepSeek-R1 technical report [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948). (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!XwZe!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Febc749fb-6a79-483f-bcda-b219f284bc09_1168x604.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!XwZe!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Febc749fb-6a79-483f-bcda-b219f284bc09_1168x604.png))

The DeepSeek report provides a direct comparison of the effectiveness of these approaches. While the distilled models are not as powerful as the full R1 teacher model, they significantly outperform models of a similar size that are trained with pure RL from a cold start. For example, the R1-Distill-Qwen-32B model achieves much higher scores on reasoning benchmarks than a 32B model trained with the R1-Zero (pure RL) methodology [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948).

This leads to two important insights. First, for smaller models, distillation appears to be a more economical and effective strategy than attempting to elicit emergent reasoning through pure RL, which may require a certain scale to be successful. Second, while distillation is powerful, it is ultimately a method of knowledge transfer, not knowledge creation. To push the boundaries of AI intelligence, more powerful base models and large-scale RL are still necessary to discover novel reasoning strategies in the first place [[14]](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/deepseek-r1-recipe-for-o1). The table above could have been more useful if it included a comparison with the base model to show the uplift from RL, and a comparison with the teacher model to show the performance drop from distillation.

However, distilling reasoning to smaller models presents significant open challenges. The process can dilute the nuanced, multi-step logic of the teacher, and it is particularly difficult when there is a large architectural gap between the teacher and student. Furthermore, complex reasoning like CoT has been observed as an emergent property of models at a scale of over 100B parameters. Simply training smaller models on CoT data does not guarantee they can replicate the underlying reasoning capabilities, especially for tasks requiring rare factual knowledge or precise computation [[24]](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/29821/31426).

After dissecting the four techniques and seeing them embodied in DeepSeek-R1, we can step back to evaluate the release's broader significance and limitations.

## Thoughts about DeepSeek R1

The release of the DeepSeek-R1 series is a significant event for the AI community. The open MIT license, the detailed technical report, and the suite of models (R1-Zero, R1, and distilled versions) provide an invaluable resource for researchers and engineers. The project's transparency is a welcome contrast to the secrecy surrounding many frontier models. Most importantly, it provides the first concrete, open-weight demonstration that pure RL from a "cold start" can lead to the emergence of sophisticated reasoning and self-correction behaviors without any initial supervised data [[11]](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/a9GR7m4nyBsqjjL8d/deepseek-r1-for-beginners).

When comparing DeepSeek-R1 to OpenAI's o1, the benchmarks show that they are highly competitive, especially on math, coding, and reasoning tasks [[1]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948). However, DeepSeek-R1 appears to be more inference-efficient. This suggests a difference in strategy: DeepSeek seems to have invested heavily in a complex, multi-stage training pipeline, while o1 may lean more on expensive inference-time scaling techniques to achieve its performance [[14]](https://www.interconnects.ai/p/deepseek-r1-recipe-for-o1).

However, any direct comparison is limited by the lack of information about o1. We do not know its model size, whether it uses a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture, the exact mix of training techniques, or the strength of its base model. Without these details, it is impossible to definitively attribute performance differences to specific architectural or training choices. A direct comparison is therefore unwarranted until more information about o1 is disclosed.

There is also the reality of training costs. While a figure of around $294,000 has been reported for the training of R1, this number does not include prior expenses for developing the V3 base model [[15]](https://www.reuters.com/world/china/chinas-deepseek-says-its-hit-ai-model-cost-just-294000-train-2025-09-18). The true incremental cost of adding the specialized reasoning capabilities remains undisclosed.

Despite these caveats, DeepSeek-R1 stands as a milestone for open-weight reasoning models. It proves that high-level reasoning is not the exclusive domain of closed-source labs. By open-sourcing their models and detailing their methods, DeepSeek has significantly accelerated research into all four of the techniques we have discussed.

## Developing reasoning models on a limited budget

The development costs of reasoning models, even when starting from an open-weight base, can be discouraging for researchers and engineers with limited budgets. However, several recent projects have shown that it is possible to achieve impressive reasoning capabilities without massive computational resources.

One of the most practical approaches is distillation. The **Sky-T1** project from UC Berkeley's Sky Computing Lab is a prime example. By carefully curating a dataset of just 17,000 high-quality reasoning examples and using them to fine-tune a 32B parameter model, the team was able to approach the performance of an early version of OpenAI's o1 on several benchmarks. The entire training process cost less than $450, illustrating that smart data curation and distillation can be an extremely effective, budget-conscious strategy [[16]](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/11/researchers-open-source-sky-t1-a-reasoning-ai-model-that-can-be-trained-for-less-than-450).

It is also possible to explore pure RL on a smaller scale. The **TinyZero** project demonstrated this by training a 3B parameter model on specific tasks like the Countdown game for under $30 [[18]](https://github.com/Jiayi-Pan/TinyZero), [[19]](https://www.dailycal.org/news/campus/research-and-ideas/campus-researchers-replicate-disruptive-chinese-ai-for-30/article_a1cc5cd0-dee4-11ef-b8ca-171526dfb895.html). Even at this small scale, the model exhibited emergent behaviors like self-verification, reinforcing the idea that the "cold-start" RL approach can yield useful reasoning patterns without massive compute.

This emergence of capabilities in smaller models is not entirely random. Research suggests it is governed by predictable scaling laws. A key insight is that a model's pre-training loss is a more fundamental predictor of emergent abilities than parameter count alone [[25]](https://gregrobison.medium.com/emergent-properties-in-large-language-models-a-deep-research-analysis-d6886c37061b). This implies that even a sub-7B model can develop sophisticated behaviors like self-verification if it is trained efficiently enough to reach a critical loss threshold, providing a theoretical basis for projects like TinyZero.

Image 16: A figure from the TinyZero repository showing that the model is capable of self-verification [[18]](https://github.com/Jiayi-Pan/TinyZero). (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ykdn!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6111f4b4-cfb9-494c-8390-ec251702914b_1600x955.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ykdn!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F6111f4b4-cfb9-494c-8390-ec251702914b_1600x955.png))

Another promising, low-cost technique is **journey learning**. This concept, explored in the O1 Replication Journey report, proposes a shift in how we approach SFT [[20]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18982). Instead of training a model only on "shortcut" solutions (the direct, correct path to an answer), journey learning involves training on the entire problem-solving trajectory, including incorrect paths, self-corrections, and backtracking.

Image 17: Journey learning, as opposed to traditional shortcut learning, includes wrong solutions paths in the SFT data. Annotated figure from the O1 Replication Journey: A Strategic Progress Report – Part 1 [[20]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18982). (Source [https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TxCO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7a0bfcd0-6d93-4c91-a0d6-28178839b7cf_1492x724.png](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!TxCO!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F7a0bfcd0-6d93-4c91-a0d6-28178839b7cf_1492x724.png))

By exposing the model to its own mistakes and the process of correcting them, it learns more robust self-correction mechanisms. A model trained only on "golden" solutions may be brittle when it encounters a novel error, as it has never seen a recovery path. In contrast, a model trained on journey-style data is better equipped to recognize and correct its own mistakes. The O1 Replication Journey report showed that with only 327 training samples, journey learning outperformed conventional shortcut learning by over 8% on the MATH dataset, demonstrating its potential [[20]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.18982).

The future of budget-conscious reasoning model development likely lies in combining insights from these projects. A hybrid pipeline could involve using a small-scale RL model like TinyZero to generate initial reasoning traces, curating them using journey learning principles, and then using that high-quality, small dataset to distill a capable model, similar to the Sky-T1 approach. This would balance the emergent discovery of RL with the efficiency of distillation, offering a path to powerful reasoning without an enormous budget.

## Conclusion

We have explored the four primary approaches to building and improving reasoning models. **Inference-time scaling**, which includes techniques like CoT prompting and MCTS, enhances performance without any training cost but incurs high serving costs. **Pure RL**, as demonstrated by DeepSeek-R1-Zero, is a powerful research tool that can unlock emergent behaviors like self-correction from a cold start. The **SFT+RL hybrid** approach, used for the flagship DeepSeek-R1, offers a production-ready blueprint that combines the stability of supervised learning with the exploratory power of RL, especially effective when verifiable rewards are available. Finally, **distillation** provides an efficient method for transferring capabilities to smaller models, though the resulting models are ultimately derivative of the teacher.

Looking ahead, the future of the most capable systems will likely be a hybrid one. We suspect that frontier models like o1, and future o3-class systems, already combine complex SFT+RL training pipelines with sophisticated inference-time scaling. This allows them to benefit from both the robust, emergent behaviors discovered during training and the on-the-fly refinement and search at inference time.

For AI engineers, the key takeaway is to match the technique to the constraints of the project. There is no one-size-fits-all solution. The choice between these four approaches should be driven by a clear understanding of the trade-offs between budget, performance targets, innovation needs, and deployment latency. Rather than defaulting to the largest, most expensive proprietary model, a strategic engineer will consider whether the task truly requires the power of a full reasoning model, or if a more efficient, distilled model or even a general-purpose LLM with smart prompting would suffice. The art of AI engineering lies in making these informed decisions to build systems that are not just powerful, but also practical and cost-effective.

The development of powerful reasoning models is not an end in itself. The next frontier is integrating them into larger, more capable systems. They are poised to become the core reasoning engines in multi-agent frameworks, where specialized agents collaborate to solve problems far beyond the scope of a single model [[26]](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/multi-agent-reasoning-frameworks). This is already catalyzing a paradigm shift in fields like scientific discovery, where autonomous agents can now manage complex research workflows from hypothesis to analysis [[27]](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.13259v1). Ultimately, these engineering efforts bring us closer to understanding and replicating the mechanisms of human cognition, a central question in cognitive science [[28]](https://www.browndailyherald.com/article/2026/01/llm-reasoning-has-striking-similarities-with-human-cognition-brown-researchers-find).
