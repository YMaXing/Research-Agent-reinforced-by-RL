# Where Did Earth's Water Come From? The Three-Way Battle Between Comets, Asteroids, and Earth Itself

At this moment, a spacecraft is headed from Earth to Europa, an ice-veiled moon of Jupiter thought to contain a vast subsurface ocean. Affixed to the craft is a metal plate engraved with a poem by Ada Limón, which reads in part: “*And it is not darkness that unites us, not the cold distance of space, but the offering of water, each drop of rain, each rivulet, each pulse, each vein*” [[32]](https://poets.org/poem/praise-mystery-poem-europa). For decades, the search for water has dominated our exploration of the solar system, because as far as we know, water is essential for life.

It may come as a surprise, then, that we do not really know how water first arrived here on Earth. For years, the leading theory was that icy comets, relics from the dawn of the solar system, delivered our oceans. As spacecraft ventured out to meet these cosmic travelers, however, the data they sent back told a different story. The chemical signature of their water did not match our own.

After that, “comets sort of fell out of favor,” said Ashley King, a meteoriticist at the Natural History Museum in London [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). Asteroids—rockier and more metallic—then became the most popular candidates. They strike Earth far more frequently, and the water found in meteorites, fragments of asteroids that have fallen to Earth, looked a lot more like ours. But asteroids have their own problems, and a radical new idea is gaining momentum: that rocky planets like Earth have a way to make water all by themselves.

The long-held belief in an external delivery, or exogenous, model has been challenged by this theory of homegrown, or endogenous, water. The debate has shifted from a simple contest between comets and asteroids to a more complex question of whether our planet's water was delivered from space or forged in its own fiery depths. In this article, we will explore the evidence for and against each of these cosmic water carriers and delve into the science behind the possibility that Earth made its own oceans.

## A Showdown Between Comets and Asteroids

Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago. Much about its earliest eon has been lost to history, but the basics are agreed upon: It began as a ball of mostly molten rock. Then it became a blue marble. How?

Comets provided a compelling answer. These objects, often described as dirty snowballs, formed in the cold outer reaches of the solar system, in the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune or the even more distant Oort cloud. When a comet’s orbit brings it close to the sun, its ice and frozen gases vaporize, creating a spectacular tail. Because they are so rich in water ice, comets give you “a lot of bang for your buck,” said James Bryson, a meteoriticist at the University of Oxford [[26]](https://www.earth.ox.ac.uk/article/earth-sciences-conversation-james-bryson). It seemed logical that a barrage of these icy bodies crashing into a young, cooling Earth could have filled its oceans.

Scientists thought they could prove this if comets contained Earth-like water. The key lies in the water’s chemical "flavor"—specifically, the ratio of deuterium to hydrogen (D/H). Deuterium is a heavier form of hydrogen, and almost all water on Earth is H₂O. However, a small fraction is "heavy water," containing deuterium. If comets are responsible for our oceans, their water should have a similar D/H ratio. In the 1980s, the European Space Agency (ESA) decided to check. Their first deep-space mission, Giotto, was sent to get an up-close look at Halley’s Comet.

In 1986, Giotto intercepted the famous comet and sent back the first-ever close-up images of a cometary nucleus. But it was the measurement of the material surrounding the comet that raised eyebrows. The D/H ratio on Halley's comet was twice that of Earth's oceans. “It didn’t seem logical that today’s oceans should match the D/H chemistry of the primordial water present at Earth’s formation,” said Karen Meech, a planetary astronomer at the University of Hawai‘i [[22]](https://research.hawaii.edu/noelo/water-and-habitability).![ESA’s Giotto spacecraft during launch preparations.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Giotto_launch_preparations-cr.ESA_.webp)

(Source: [ESA/Quanta Magazine [1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612/))![Close-up image of Comet Halley's nucleus taken by Giotto.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Giotto_images_of_Comet_Halley-cr.MPAE-courtesy-Dr.-H.U.-Keller.webp)

More cracks appeared in the comet theory through the 1990s and 2000s as observations of other comets, like Hale-Bopp, also found high levels of heavy water [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). The final blow came in 2014 from Giotto's successor, ESA's Rosetta mission. Rosetta not only orbited the duck-shaped comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko but also landed a probe on its surface. Its precise measurements revealed that 67P had the highest concentration of deuterium of any comet measured, more than three times that of Earth's oceans [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[2]](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Rosetta/Rosetta_fuels_debate_on_origin_of_Earth_s_oceans).![Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko photographed by the Rosetta mission.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/TheComet_crChristianStangl-Lede-2-scaled.webp)
Image 2: Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko, photographed in 2014 by ESA’s Rosetta mission. (Source: [ESA/Christian Stangl/Quanta Magazine [1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612/))

With comets seemingly ruled out, attention turned to asteroids. These rocky bodies, mostly found between Mars and Jupiter, impact our planet as meteorites far more often than comets do. Scientists have collected thousands of meteorites and found that the water in a particular group, carbonaceous chondrites, closely resembles our own. A meteorite that fell in Winchcombe, UK, in 2021 was found to have a D/H ratio that almost perfectly matched Earth's oceans [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[3]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abq3925).

However, meteorites can be contaminated during their fiery descent and crash landing. To get around this, spacecraft have flown to asteroids to collect samples directly from their surfaces. In 2023, an analysis of material from the asteroid Ryugu, visited by Japan's Hayabusa2 spacecraft, confirmed an Earth-like D/H ratio [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[4]](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/2041-8213/acc393). This aligns with theories that asteroids were much richer in water during the Late Heavy Bombardment, around 3.8 billion years ago, than their drier remnants are today [[36]](https://www.space.com/36661-late-heavy-bombardment.html).![The asteroid Ryugu as seen by the Hayabusa2 spacecraft.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/arrival-bennu-full-rotation-cr.NASAs-Goddard-Space-Flight-Center_University-Of-Arizona-still.jpg)
Image 3: The asteroid Ryugu, visited by the Hayabusa2 spacecraft in 2018, contained water with a D/H ratio similar to that found on Earth. (Source: [NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center/University of Arizona/Quanta Magazine [1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612/))

But the asteroid theory is not without its own problems. While the water matches, other elements do not. Asteroids contain small amounts of noble gases like argon, krypton, and xenon, and their isotopic mixtures do not align with what we find in Earth's atmosphere [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-022-00929-9). Furthermore, both the comet and asteroid theories rely on a period of intense bombardment late in Earth's formation, an event whose scale and even existence are still debated. This event is thought to be the result of a dynamical instability in the outer solar system, where the giant planets shifted their orbits and scattered a vast number of comets and asteroids inwards, a scenario known as the Nice model [[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Heavy_Bombardment).

Because both leading exogenous models have significant flaws, a more radical possibility has gained traction: Earth may have made most of its water by itself. Since the evidence for external delivery has unresolved inconsistencies, the next section explores whether our planet could have manufactured its own oceans by looking at the chemistry of its very building blocks.

## Hydrogen, Meet Magma

When astronomers simulate how exoplanets form, they find that many rocky worlds likely began their lives shrouded in hydrogen-rich atmospheres. This has led scientists to wonder if Earth’s early years were similar. This line of inquiry is supported by direct observations; astronomers can detect water vapor in the atmospheres of distant exoplanets, such as K2-18b, by analyzing their starlight with spectral analysis [[38]](https://joss.tcnj.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/176/2024/04/2024-Pedowitz-Physics.pdf). For a long time, the answer was thought to be no. This conclusion was based on the study of enstatite chondrites, a type of meteorite with a chemical makeup so similar to Earth that they are considered our planet's best-preserved building blocks [[6]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba1948). These meteorites appeared to lack hydrogen, so it was assumed Earth did too [[26]](https://www.earth.ox.ac.uk/article/earth-sciences-conversation-james-bryson).

However, recent studies have found that there was hydrogen in these meteorites all along, hidden within their organic molecules and sulfide minerals [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[7]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0019103525001356). This discovery opened the door to a new possibility: if early Earth was awash in hydrogen, and its surface was a searing ocean of magma full of oxygen, what would happen if the two mixed? A 2023 paper explored this very question, concluding that such a process could allow a planet to make its own water, though it was unclear how much [[8]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-05823-0).

Two years later, a set of ambitious experiments provided an answer. Researchers wanted to understand how sub-Neptunes, common exoplanets larger than Earth, could have water-rich atmospheres despite orbiting close to their stars. They suspected that a reaction between a hydrogen atmosphere and a magma ocean could be the cause, but only if the hydrogen was under immense pressure [[9]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09630-7).

To test this, they recreated the extreme conditions of an adolescent sub-Neptune in the lab. Using diamond-anvil cells to put hydrogen gas under intense pressure and lasers to melt rock samples, they simulated the interface between a magma ocean and a dense atmosphere. It took five years to perfect the technique. "We broke a lot of diamonds," said Sang-Heon Shim, a geophysicist at Arizona State University [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). The results were explosive. The reaction was so efficient that it produced far more water than scientists had predicted [[9]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09630-7), [[10]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09816-z). The experiments showed that at pressures above 2 gigapascals—conditions found at the base of a magma ocean—water production was 2,000 to 3,000 times higher than theoretical models had predicted [[39]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12571897). "It doesn’t seem unreasonable [that you could] produce a huge amount of water quite quickly," said Paul Byrne, a planetary scientist at Washington University in St. Louis. "And this is all homegrown, indigenous water" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

Does this mean Earth created its own oceans? This is where the waters get murky. While the researchers behind the experiments think it is a valid link to make, others are more cautious. The main issue is whether early Earth had enough gravity to hold on to the massive hydrogen atmosphere needed to create the pressure for the reaction. Sub-Neptunes are far more massive than Earth, making them better at retaining light gases. Atmospheric escape is a powerful force, especially around young, active stars. Planets orbiting M-dwarf stars, for instance, are thought to lose the equivalent of 3 to 20 Earth oceans of water to space due to intense stellar radiation [[40]](https://astrobiology.com/2023/08/the-role-of-magma-oceans-in-maintaining-surface-water-on-rocky-planets-orbiting-m-dwarfs.html). “Earth is right on the edge of where that kind of thing can start happening,” said H. W. Horn, a physicist at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

Some scientists are doubtful that an Earth-mass planet could manufacture its own water at scale [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). But the experiments suggest the reaction would not need to last long. Earth might have been a water factory for only a brief moment, but that moment could have been enough to forge its oceans. If that is the case, the implications are profound. It would mean that countless rocky planets could be "born water-rich," a necessary condition for hosting life, without the need for a chance delivery of water from comets or asteroids. This idea also challenges conventional models of planet formation. Traditionally, water-rich worlds were thought to form only beyond a system's "snow line" and then migrate inwards. Endogenous production suggests wet planets can form close to their stars, meaning water on an exoplanet may not be evidence of migration [[41]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09630-7).

While the endogenous mechanism offers a compelling new chapter in the story of Earth's water, it does not close the book entirely. As we will see, this internal production may be just one part of a more complex, mixed-source narrative.

## Drowning in a Sea of Possibilities

Just as the case against comets seemed closed, new evidence has brought them back into the picture. By the time Rosetta reached comet 67P in 2014, scientists had studied the water of 11 other comets. All had D/H ratios unlike Earth's—except one. In 2011, ESA's Herschel Space Observatory found that the water in comet Hartley 2 had a much more Earth-like signature [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[11]](https://sci.esa.int/web/herschel/-/49386-herschel-finds-first-evidence-of-earth-like-water-in-a-comet).![Image of Comet Hartley 2.](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Comet-Hartley-2-cr-NASA-JPL-Caltech-UMD-still-1.jpg)
Image 5: Comet Hartley 2, studied by the Herschel Space Observatory, has an Earth-like ratio of heavy to normal water. (Source: [NASA/JPL-Caltech/UMD/Quanta Magazine [1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612/))

This observation seemed anomalous at the time. But in 2024, a reanalysis of Rosetta's data from comet 67P suggested that dust in the comet's coma might have skewed the original measurements, and the comet's body itself could have been more Earth-like than initially thought [[12]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adp2191). Then, in 2025, observations of comet 12P/Pons-Brooks also detected a D/H ratio similar to Earth's oceans [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

So, where does that leave us? "I suspect it was a combination of all of them," says Meech [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). Earth's oceans are most likely a cocktail, with contributions from asteroids, a minority fraction from comets that may account for up to 10% of the total volume [[42]](http://web-static-aws.seas.harvard.edu/climate/eli/Courses/EPS281r/Sources/Origin-of-oceans/more/Morbidelli%202000%20water%20on%20Earth.pdf), and a foundational amount produced endogenously in its own magma ocean. The reason it is so hard to find a perfect extraterrestrial match for our planet's water is that the original signature has been tweaked and filtered over billions of years by geological processes, atmospheric escape, and life itself [[27]](https://www.aaas.org/membership/member-spotlight/where-did-earths-water-come-aaas-fellow-karen-meech-investigates). Our own solar system provides a stark example. Venus likely had early oceans but lost them to a runaway greenhouse effect, which boiled the water into the atmosphere where it was stripped away by the sun. This process preferentially removes lighter hydrogen, leaving behind a telltale signature: Venus's atmosphere today has a deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio 160 times higher than Earth's, a clear scar of its lost water [[43]](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140017487/downloads/20140017487.pdf), [[44]](https://solarsystem.wustl.edu/wp-content/uploads/reprints/2004/No.%20109%20Fegley%202004%20atmospheric%20evolution%20on%20Venus%20ms..pdf). Geological filtering includes a deep water cycle, where surface water is carried into the mantle via plate tectonics and later returned to the atmosphere through volcanic outgassing, further mixing the planet's water reservoirs over eons [[45]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12783251).

In many ways, the search for the origin of Earth's water mirrors the search for the origin of life. The more we learn, the less certain we become of any single, simple answer. "We may never know," Meech said. But that does not mean scientists will stop trying. "It’s like asking, what’s the origin of life?" she added. "The more you learn, the less you know—but the story becomes richer and more exciting" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).
