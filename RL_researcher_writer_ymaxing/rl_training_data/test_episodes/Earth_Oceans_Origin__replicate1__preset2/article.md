# Where Did Earth's Water Come From? The Three-Way Battle Between Comets, Asteroids, and Earth Itself

As engineers, we build systems based on evidence. When new data challenges our assumptions, we update our models. The scientific quest to understand the origin of Earth's water is a perfect case study in this process. For decades, a simple, elegant model held sway: water was delivered to a dry, primordial Earth by icy comets. It was a clean explanation that fit the available data. But as our observational capabilities improved, sending spacecraft to rendezvous with these cosmic travelers, new data emerged that broke the old model.

This is a story familiar to anyone who has shipped a product. Your v1 works, but as you gather more user data, you discover edge cases and fundamental flaws you never anticipated. You are forced to refactor, or even completely re-architect, your system. In planetary science, the "system" is our understanding of planetary formation, and the "data" comes from missions that cost billions of dollars and take decades to execute. The stakes are high, and so is the rigor.

For years, the top theory was that comets, icy relics from the dawn of the solar system, were the primary source of our oceans. This model faced serious challenges when the chemical signature of cometary water was found to be a poor match for our own. After that, "comets sort of fell out of favor," said Ashley King, a meteoriticist at the Natural History Museum in London [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). The community pivoted to a new model centered on asteroids, which appeared to be a much better fit. But this model, too, has its own problems. Now, a third, more radical hypothesis is gaining traction: Earth may have generated a significant portion of its own water internally.

This article will break down the evidence for and against each of these models. We will start by examining the data that drove the showdown between comets and asteroids. Then, we will explore the new, homegrown water theory. Finally, we will synthesize these competing ideas into a more complex, mixed-source model that reflects our current understanding.

## A Showdown Between Comets and Asteroids

The core problem is straightforward. Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago as a ball of mostly molten rock. It eventually cooled and became the blue marble we know today. The question is, where did the water come from?

Comets were the early frontrunner. Formed in the frigid outer solar system—in the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune or the more distant Oort cloud—these bodies are composed largely of water ice. This makes them incredibly efficient delivery vehicles. As meteoriticist James Bryson of the University of Oxford puts it, with comets, you get "a lot of bang for your buck" [[26]](https://www.earth.ox.ac.uk/article/earth-sciences-conversation-james-bryson). A sustained bombardment of these icy bodies could have easily filled Earth's oceans.

The key to testing this model is the deuterium-to-hydrogen (D/H) ratio. Deuterium is a heavy isotope of hydrogen, and its proportion in water acts as a chemical fingerprint, revealing its origin. If comets supplied our water, their D/H ratio should match that of Earth's oceans. In the 1980s, the European Space Agency (ESA) launched its first deep-space mission, Giotto, to test this hypothesis by getting an up-close look at Halley's Comet.

In 1986, Giotto flew through the comet's coma, sending back the first-ever close-up images of a cometary nucleus. The data it collected on the surrounding gas and dust, however, presented a problem. The D/H ratio of Halley's water was roughly twice that of Earth's oceans. This mismatch was a significant blow to the comet theory. As planetary astronomer Karen Meech noted, “It didn’t seem logical that today’s oceans should match the D/H chemistry of the primordial water present at Earth’s formation” [[22]](https://research.hawaii.edu/noelo/water-and-habitability).![https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Giotto_launch_preparations-cr.ESA_.webp](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Giotto_launch_preparations-cr.ESA_.webp)

Over the next two decades, spectroscopic observations of other comets, such as Hale-Bopp, confirmed similarly high deuterium levels [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). The definitive data point arrived in 2014 with ESA's Rosetta mission. Rosetta orbited comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko and deployed a lander to its surface, making the most precise measurements of a comet's composition to date. The result was unambiguous: 67P's water had a D/H ratio more than three times that of Earth's oceans, effectively ruling out comets from this family as the primary source of our planet's water [[2]](https://www.esa.int/Science_Exploration/Space_Science/Rosetta/Rosetta_fuels_debate_on_origin_of_Earth_s_oceans).![https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/TheComet_crChristianStangl-Lede-2-scaled.webp](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/TheComet_crChristianStangl-Lede-2-scaled.webp)
Image 2: *Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko was photographed in 2014 by ESA’s Rosetta mission, which also sent a lander to the comet’s surface. ESA/ [Christian Stangl]*

With the comet model faltering, the focus shifted to asteroids. These rocky bodies, which mostly orbit between Mars and Jupiter, are the parent bodies of meteorites. While drier than comets, they impact Earth far more frequently. Analysis of a specific class of meteorites, carbonaceous chondrites, revealed a D/H ratio that was a much closer match. The 2021 fall of the Winchcombe meteorite provided a pristine sample, recovered just hours after landing. Analysis showed it was a CM-type carbonaceous chondrite containing about 11% water by weight, with a hydrogen isotopic composition nearly identical to that of Earth's water [[3]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.abq3925).

Because meteorites are subject to terrestrial contamination, sample-return missions are the gold standard. Japan's Hayabusa2 mission collected material from the Cb-type asteroid Ryugu and returned it to Earth. Analysis of these pristine samples confirmed the meteorite data. The D/H ratio of hydrous minerals in Ryugu was estimated to be indistinguishable from that of Earth's oceans, reinforcing the link between these types of asteroids and terrestrial water [[4]](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/2041-8213/acc393).![https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/arrival-bennu-full-rotation-cr.NASAs-Goddard-Space-Flight-Center_University-Of-Arizona-still.jpg](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/arrival-bennu-full-rotation-cr.NASAs-Goddard-Space-Flight-Center_University-Of-Arizona-still.jpg)
Image 3: *The asteroid Ryugu, visited by the Hayabusa2spacecraft in 2018, contained water similar to the most common kind found on Earth. NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center/University of Arizona*

However, the asteroid model is not a perfect fit. While the water's D/H ratio matches, the isotopic compositions of other volatile elements, particularly noble gases like krypton and xenon, do not align with Earth's atmosphere [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[5]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11214-022-00929-9). These inert elements serve as crucial tracers for planetary formation processes, and the mismatch suggests that asteroids cannot be the sole source of Earth's volatiles.

Furthermore, both the comet and asteroid delivery models depend on a period of intense impacts known as the Late Heavy Bombardment. This scenario, often explained by the "Nice model" of planetary migration, posits that the shifting orbits of Jupiter and Saturn scattered a vast number of small bodies into the inner solar system [[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Heavy_Bombardment). However, the magnitude and even the occurrence of this event are still heavily debated in the scientific community [[42]](http://web-static-aws.seas.harvard.edu/climate/eli/Courses/EPS281r/Sources/Origin-of-oceans/more/Morbidelli%202000%20water%20on%20Earth.pdf). The reliance on this uncertain delivery mechanism is a fundamental weakness for both exogenous models.

The unresolved issues in both the comet and asteroid hypotheses have pushed scientists to consider a different paradigm. If external delivery cannot fully account for our oceans, perhaps the answer lies closer to home. The next section examines the evidence that Earth could have manufactured its own water, shifting the focus from late-stage delivery to the planet's initial formation.

## Hydrogen, Meet Magma

When we analyze the demographics of exoplanets, a consistent picture emerges. Many rocky planets larger than Earth, known as super-Earths and sub-Neptunes, likely formed with thick, hydrogen-rich atmospheres accreted from their protoplanetary disks [[8]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-05823-0). This has led to a re-evaluation of Earth's own beginnings. Could our planet have also started with a hydrogen envelope blanketing a global magma ocean?

The first piece of evidence comes from a fresh look at enstatite chondrites, the meteorites considered to be Earth's primary building blocks. For decades, these rocks were assumed to be "dry" because they formed in the hot inner solar system. However, recent, more sensitive laboratory analyses have revealed significant amounts of hydrogen locked within their minerals and organic matter [[6]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aba1948), [[7]](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0019103525001356). This discovery suggests that the raw materials of Earth were not as hydrogen-poor as once believed.

If a young Earth had both a hydrogen-rich atmosphere and a surface of molten rock, the conditions would be ripe for chemistry. The magma ocean would be a massive reservoir of oxygen bound in silicate minerals. A 2023 study modeled the interaction between such an atmosphere and magma ocean, demonstrating that water could be produced as atmospheric hydrogen reacted with oxygen from the melt [[8]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-05823-0). The key unknown was the efficiency of this reaction under planetary-scale pressures.

This is where recent laboratory breakthroughs come in. To understand how water-rich sub-Neptunes can exist close to their stars, researchers designed experiments to simulate the extreme conditions at the core-envelope boundary of these planets. Using diamond-anvil cells to generate immense pressures and lasers to melt silicate samples, they replicated the environment where a dense hydrogen atmosphere meets a magma ocean [[9]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09630-7). The experiments were challenging; as geophysicist Sang-Heon Shim noted, "We broke a lot of diamonds" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

The results were dramatic. At pressures between 16 and 60 GPa and temperatures over 4,000 K, the reaction between hydrogen and the silicate melt produced copious amounts of water [[10]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09816-z). The water yields were up to thousands of times higher than what previous theoretical models, based on low-pressure extrapolations, had predicted [[9]](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09630-7). The experiments showed that the reduction of iron oxide in the melt was a key process, leading to the formation of iron-rich blebs and marked amounts of water [[13]](https://faculty.epss.ucla.edu/~eyoung/reprints/Miozzi_etal_2025.pdf). "It doesn’t seem unreasonable [that you could] produce a huge amount of water quite quickly," said planetary scientist Paul Byrne. "And this is all homegrown, indigenous water" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

The debate then shifts to whether an Earth-mass planet could sustain the necessary conditions. Sub-Neptunes are much more massive and their stronger gravity is better at retaining a light gas like hydrogen against atmospheric escape. "Earth is right on the edge of where that kind of thing can start happening," according to H. W. Horn, a physicist involved in the experiments [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). Some scientists remain doubtful that Earth had the gravitational heft to maintain a hydrogen atmosphere dense enough for large-scale water production [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

However, the high efficiency of the reaction means it might not have needed to last long. A relatively brief period as a "water factory" could have been sufficient to produce a substantial hydrosphere before the hydrogen atmosphere was stripped away. This possibility has profound implications for habitability. If rocky planets can be "born water-rich," it decouples the presence of water from the stochastic process of late-stage delivery by comets or asteroids [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). This would greatly expand the number of potentially habitable worlds in the galaxy, as a local water source would be a common outcome of planet formation [[16]](https://www.mpg.de/20541783/water-discovered-in-rocky-planet-forming-zone-offers-clues-on-habitability).

This endogenous mechanism provides a powerful new piece of the puzzle, but it does not necessarily exclude external contributions. The final picture is likely a composite one. The next section will synthesize these different models and explore the complicating factors that make a definitive answer so elusive.

## Drowning in a Sea of Possibilities

Just as the case against comets seemed closed, new evidence has prompted a re-evaluation. By the time Rosetta arrived at comet 67P, scientists had studied the water of 11 other comets. All had D/H ratios unlike Earth's. There was one exception. In 2011, ESA's Herschel Space Observatory found that the water in comet Hartley 2 had a much more Earth-like signature [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612), [[11]](https://sci.esa.int/web/herschel/-/49386-herschel-finds-first-evidence-of-earth-like-water-in-a-comet).![https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Comet-Hartley-2-cr-NASA-JPL-Caltech-UMD-still-1.jpg](https://www.quantamagazine.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Comet-Hartley-2-cr-NASA-JPL-Caltech-UMD-still-1.jpg)
Image 5: *Comet Hartley 2, studied by the Herschel Space Observatory, has an Earth-like ratio of heavy to normal water. NASA/JPL-Caltech/UMD*

This finding was initially seen as an anomaly. However, a 2024 reanalysis of Rosetta's data from 67P suggested that dust in the comet's coma could have contaminated the samples, artificially inflating the measured D/H ratio. The comet's nucleus ice, it was argued, might be closer to terrestrial values [[12]](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adp2191). More recently, observations of comet 12P/Pons-Brooks also detected an Earth-like D/H ratio, strengthening the case that not all comets are isotopically "heavy" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).

So, where does this leave us? "I suspect it was a combination of all of them," says Karen Meech [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612). The most plausible model is a mixed one. Earth's oceans are likely a cocktail, with a foundational amount produced endogenously in the magma ocean, a majority of the inventory delivered by D/H-matched asteroids, and a smaller fraction contributed by comets.

The reason a single, clean source is so hard to identify is that the original chemical fingerprints have been altered over billions of years. Karen Meech explains that isotopic signatures can be erased over time due to geological processes, suggesting Earth's water likely came from primordial sources that have since been mixed and differentiated [[27]](https://www.aaas.org/membership/member-spotlight/where-did-earths-water-come-aaas-fellow-karen-meech-investigates). One major process is atmospheric escape, which preferentially removes lighter isotopes and can drastically alter D/H ratios over geological time. Venus provides a stark example; it likely had early oceans but lost them to a runaway greenhouse effect, leaving behind an atmosphere with a D/H ratio 160 times higher than Earth's [[43]](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140017487/downloads/20140017487.pdf), [[44]](https://solarsystem.wustl.edu/wp-content/uploads/reprints/2004/No.%20109%20Fegley%202004%20atmospheric%20evolution%20on%20Venus%20ms..pdf).

Geological cycling also plays a role. Plate tectonics carries surface water deep into the mantle, where it mixes with primordial water reservoirs before being outgassed through volcanoes. This deep water cycle constantly remixes the planet's various water sources, blurring their original signatures [[45]](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12783251). Finally, life itself has altered the planet's chemistry.

The search for the origin of Earth's water is much like the search for the origin of life. The more we learn, the more we realize there is no single, simple answer. "We may never know," Meech admits. But the pursuit itself refines our models and deepens our understanding of how habitable worlds come to be. "The story becomes richer and more exciting" [[1]](https://www.quantamagazine.org/where-did-earth-get-its-oceans-maybe-it-made-them-itself-20260612).
