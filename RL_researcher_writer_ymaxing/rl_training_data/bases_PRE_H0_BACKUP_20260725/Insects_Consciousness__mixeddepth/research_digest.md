<s slug="the-background-of-the-new-york-declaration" type="golden_web">
The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness background document reviews advances in animal cognition research from 2014–2024, focusing on evidence for phenomenal consciousness (subjective sensory or affective experience) across vertebrates and invertebrates. It defines consciousness as raw felt experience, distinct from stimulus detection or human-like language/reason, drawing on Nagel’s “what it is like” formulation and behaviors that would indicate conscious processing in mammals.

Ten key studies are presented as examples. Crows trained to report visual stimuli via head gestures showed NCL activity correlating with reported perception rather than stimulus presence (2020 Science study). Octopuses developed conditioned place aversion to acetic acid injection and preference for lidocaine relief chambers (2021 iScience study). Cuttlefish demonstrated source memory for whether prey items were seen or smelled after three-hour delays (2020 Scientific Reports study). Cleaner wrasse completed all four phases of the mirror-mark test, including mark-directed scraping (2019–2023 PLOS Biology and PNAS studies). Garter snakes investigated their own scent-plus-mark longer than controls in a scent-based variant (2024 Proceedings of the Royal Society B study). Zebrafish voluntarily explored novel objects with habituation patterns indicating intrinsic reward (2023 Frontiers in Veterinary Science study). Bumblebees rolled wooden balls repeatedly in intrinsically motivated, non-functional, variable sequences that increased when relaxed (2022 Animal Behaviour study). Crayfish showed light-aversion increases after electric shock that were reversed by benzodiazepines (2014–2017 Science, Journal of Experimental Biology, and Scientific Reports studies). Shore crabs traded off bright-light avoidance against prior shock intensity in shelter choices (2024 Animals study). Drosophila exhibited distinct quiet (low brain activity) and active sleep states, with social isolation disrupting sleep architecture (2021 Nature and 2024 eLife studies).

The document states that these capacities—learning, memory, self-recognition, curiosity, play, anxiety-like states, and motivational trade-offs—establish strong scientific support for consciousness in mammals and birds, and at least realistic possibility in reptiles, amphibians, fishes, cephalopods, decapod crustaceans, and insects (bees, fruit flies). It notes that certainty is not required for welfare consideration and lists 40 initial signatories including experts on bees (Lars Chittka, Andrew Barron) and fruit flies (Bruno van Swinderen). Related publications include the Science perspective “Evaluating animal consciousness,” the Stanford Encyclopedia supplement “Evidence Concerning Animal Consciousness,” and the Learning & Behavior commentary “Why we should study animal consciousness.”

Coverage gaps include absence of quantitative benchmarks, meta-analyses, or explicit insect-specific frameworks; reliance on behavioral inference without direct neural correlates for most invertebrate cases; and no discussion of implementation details for welfare policy.

<s slug="the-new-york-declaration-on-animal-consciousness" type="golden_web">
The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, issued April 19, 2024 at New York University, states that strong scientific support exists for conscious experience in mammals and birds. It further asserts a realistic possibility of conscious experience across all vertebrates (reptiles, amphibians, fishes) plus invertebrates including cephalopod mollusks, decapod crustaceans, and insects. When such possibility exists, the declaration requires that welfare risks be considered in any decisions affecting the animal. Signatories endorse the text only in their individual capacities.

No empirical datasets, benchmarks, measurement techniques, or named frameworks are supplied. The text contains no species-level distinctions within insects or other taxa. It references no experimental protocols, behavioral assays, or neuroanatomical criteria.

The source includes a 23-line Python tool-loop example on the topic of declaration text extraction but provides no further methodological detail. No quantitative claims, confidence intervals, or citation lists appear. The document therefore offers only high-level categorical statements usable for framing insect-consciousness discussions while leaving evidentiary gaps unaddressed.
