<s slug="the-background-of-the-new-york-declaration" type="golden_web">The Background of the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness presents recent advances in animal cognition research indicating subjective experiences (phenomenal consciousness or sentience) across vertebrates and many invertebrates. It defines consciousness as raw felt experience, encompassing sensory qualities and affective states such as pleasure or pain, without requiring human-like language or reason, and contrasts this with mere stimulus detection. The document lists ten key empirical examples. For insects specifically, a 2022 study found bumblebees exhibit play by repeatedly rolling wooden balls in ways meeting five criteria: intrinsic reward without external reinforcement, no apparent function, non-rehearsal of foraging or mating behaviors, variable execution, and increased occurrence during relaxed states. A 2023 eLife study on Drosophila fruit flies induced distinct “active” sleep (persistent brain activity without overt behavior, hypothesized to support cognition) and “quiet” sleep (decreased brain activity, linked to metabolic slowing and stress regulation). A 2021 Nature study showed social isolation disrupts fruit fly sleep patterns, with flies sleeping more in the presence of conspecifics. Broader examples include NCL neuronal correlates of visual experience in crows (2020 Science study), conditioned place preference for pain relief in octopuses (2021 iScience study using acetic acid and lidocaine), source memory in cuttlefish (2020 Scientific Reports study distinguishing visual vs. olfactory encounters after three-hour delays), mirror-mark test passage by cleaner wrasse (2019–2023 studies across four phases), scent-based self-recognition in garter snakes (2024 Proceedings of the Royal Society B study), curiosity-driven object exploration in zebrafish (2023 Frontiers in Veterinary Science study), anxiety-like bright-light aversion in crayfish reversible by benzodiazepines (2014–2017 Science and Journal of Experimental Biology studies), and motivational trade-offs between light avoidance and shock in shore crabs (2024 Animals study). The text states that evidence now supports “strong scientific support” for consciousness attributions in mammals and birds, and “at least a realistic possibility” in reptiles, amphibians, fishes, cephalopods, decapod crustaceans, and insects (explicitly naming bees and fruit flies), sufficient to justify further research and welfare consideration. It notes the absence of “proof” or “certainty” given ongoing debates about consciousness mechanisms. The declaration itself, signed by experts including Lars Chittka, Andrew Barron, Martin Giurfa, and Bruno van Swinderen on bees and flies, aims to encourage rigorous study and policy reflection without mandating specific regulations. Related publications cited include the Science perspective by Andrews, Birch, and Sebo and the Stanford Encyclopedia supplement on evidence. The source references dozens of DOIs for the listed studies but provides no primary datasets, benchmarks, or computational tools. Coverage gaps include absence of quantitative effect sizes, replication rates, or cross-species comparative frameworks beyond the listed cases.</s>
<s slug="the-new-york-declaration-on-animal-consciousness" type="golden_web">The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness, published April 19, 2024 at New York University and signed by 599 individuals in personal capacity, asserts that strong scientific support exists for conscious experience in mammals and birds. It states that empirical evidence indicates at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates (reptiles, amphibians, fishes) and many invertebrates, explicitly naming cephalopod mollusks, decapod crustaceans, and insects. The declaration concludes that when such a realistic possibility exists, decisions affecting those animals must incorporate welfare risks rather than ignore them. The document names no new empirical studies, benchmarks, or quantitative thresholds. It references no specific techniques, frameworks, or APIs. It lists signatories with relevant expertise, including Lars Chittka (Professor of Sensory and Behavioural Ecology, Queen Mary University of London), Martin Giurfa (Exceptional-Class Professor of Neurosciences, Sorbonne University), Andrew Barron (Macquarie University), Bruno van Swinderen (Queensland Brain Institute), and Barrett Klein (Professor of Entomology and Animal Behavior, University of Wisconsin–La Crosse). Additional signatories span philosophy, ethology, neurobiology, and law, among them Jonathan Birch, Jeff Sebo, Peter Singer, and Christof Koch. Coverage limitations include the absence of cited data, experimental protocols, or effect sizes; the text notes only that “much uncertainty remains.” No insect-specific behavioral assays, neural correlates, or welfare metrics are provided. The declaration functions solely as a consensus statement on existing attributions rather than a primary research source.</s>
<s slug="CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness" type="exploitation">The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (July 7, 2012) asserts that non-human animals possess the neuroanatomical, neurochemical, and neurophysiological substrates of conscious states and intentional behaviors. It states that the absence of a neocortex does not preclude affective states, with convergent evidence from homologous brain circuits, subcortical networks, and early evolutionary structures extending to insects and cephalopod mollusks. Key concepts include selective facilitation and disruption of circuits correlated with conscious experience, artificial arousal of subcortical regions generating emotional behaviors, and parallel evolution of consciousness in birds. Neural circuits for attentiveness, sleep, and decision-making are identified in the invertebrate radiation, explicitly naming insects alongside octopus. REM sleep and neurophysiological patterns previously attributed only to mammalian neocortex are documented in zebra finches. Mirror self-recognition is reported in magpies, comparable to great apes, dolphins, and elephants. Pharmacological interventions with hallucinogens disrupt cortical feedforward and feedback processing in humans and produce analogous behavioral perturbations in non-human animals. Deep brain stimulation of affect-related subcortical systems elicits matching affective states across species. Young mammals without neocortices retain these functions. Specific claims include: abundant new techniques enabling periodic reevaluation of preconceptions; evidence that mammalian and avian emotional networks and cognitive microcircuitries are more homologous than previously assumed; and the explicit declaration that “non-human animals, including all mammals and birds, and many other creatures, including octopuses, also possess these neurological substrates.” A footnote clarifies that all vertebrates (including fish and reptiles) and additional invertebrates (decapod crustaceans, cephalopod mollusks, insects) have supporting evidence, with octopuses singled out due to a conference presentation. Techniques referenced comprise non-invasive human neuroimaging, deep brain stimulation, pharmacological interventions, behavioral/electrophysiological assays of sleep states, and comparative neuroanatomical mapping of subcortical homologies. No benchmarks or quantitative performance metrics are provided. Coverage gaps include absence of detailed insect-specific neuroanatomy, lack of named experimental protocols or measurement tools beyond general categories, and reliance on conference consensus without primary data tables or statistical results. The declaration notes that comparative research remains limited by communication barriers in non-human subjects.</s>
<s slug="What Is It Like to Be a Bat" type="exploitation">Nagel’s 1974 paper “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?” examines why consciousness renders the mind-body problem intractable. The central claim is that an organism possesses conscious mental states if and only if there is something it is like to be that organism; this subjective character of experience cannot be captured by any currently available reductive analysis of the mental. Reductionist programs (Smart, Lewis, Putnam, Armstrong, Dennett) analyze mental phenomena in terms of functional states, intentional states, or causal roles, yet remain logically compatible with the total absence of experience, as such states could be ascribed to automata that behave like people but experience nothing. Consequently, any physicalist theory that omits the subjective character misstates the problem it seeks to solve. The paper illustrates the divergence between subjective and objective standpoints with the example of microchiroptera bats. These mammals perceive via sonar (echolocation), correlating outgoing high-frequency shrieks with returning echoes to discriminate distance, size, shape, motion, and texture. Although bat sonar constitutes a form of three-dimensional forward perception, its operation is unlike any human sense, and there is no reason to suppose it is subjectively like anything humans can experience or imagine. Attempts to imagine oneself with webbed arms, poor vision, and upside-down roosting yield only what it would be like for a human to behave as a bat behaves, not what it is like for a bat to be a bat. Extrapolation from “our own case” therefore remains incomplete; at best one can form a schematic conception that ascribes general experiential types (pain, fear, hunger, lust, additional non-sonar perception) while conceding that each type possesses a specific subjective character beyond human conception. Nagel extends the point to inter-species and inter-personal cases: facts about what it is like to be a deaf-blind person or an extraterrestrial are inaccessible from a different point of view, yet their existence is not thereby rendered doubtful. The subjective domain thus contains facts that embody a particular point of view and cannot be fully apprehended from other points of view. Objective physical theory, by contrast, proceeds by reducing dependence on species-specific standpoints; lightning or sound can be described in terms of properties observable from multiple viewpoints once the initial perceptual appearances are left behind. Experience does not fit this pattern: removing the bat’s viewpoint leaves nothing recognizable as what it is like to be a bat. The paper notes that successful reductions (sound as wave phenomena) succeed precisely because the species-specific viewpoint is omitted from what is reduced; the same omission is impossible for experience itself. Davidson’s argument that mental events with physical causes and effects must possess physical descriptions is cited as providing indirect reason to regard sensations as physical processes, yet Nagel maintains that no present conception explains how this could be true. The status of physicalism is therefore analogous to a pre-Socratic claim that matter is energy: the identity statement cannot be understood without a missing theoretical framework. A speculative proposal concludes the discussion: an “objective phenomenology” could be developed that describes structural features of subjective experience in terms comprehensible to beings incapable of having those experiences. Such a method would not rely on empathy or imagination and might eventually allow questions about the physical basis of experience to assume a more intelligible form, though the paper supplies no concrete technique or example beyond the suggestion that intermodal structural analogies could be refined beyond loose comparisons such as “red is like the sound of a trumpet.” The source contains no quantitative benchmarks, code, or empirical datasets. It leaves open whether an objective phenomenology can be realized and whether any future physical theory could accommodate the necessary connection between physical states and subjective character without reintroducing irreducible mental properties.</s>
